## Biology

Core-binding factor is an obligate heterodimer: RUNX1 provides the DNA-binding runt homology
domain, CBFβ is a non-DNA-binding subunit that allosterically stabilises RUNX1-DNA contact [1,2].
CBF governs definitive hematopoiesis, and the two fusions attack the same node from opposite
sides — one corrupts the DNA-binding subunit, the other hijacks its partner.

**t(8;21) / RUNX1::RUNX1T1.** The fusion retains RUNX1's runt domain (so it still binds CBF
consensus sites) joined to nearly all of RUNX1T1/ETO [1]. RUNX1T1 contributes four Nervy
homology regions that recruit NCoR/SMRT, Sin3A and histone deacetylases, converting a
CBF-activated promoter into a repressed one; the net effect is a block in myeloid
differentiation and impaired apoptosis [26]. Dominant repression is not the whole mechanism:
repression-independent activities of both fusion proteins are now considered comparably
important [27]. An alternatively spliced isoform, **AML1-ETO9a**, lacking the C-terminal
NHR3/NHR4 region, is far more leukemogenic in mice than the full-length fusion [28].

**inv(16) / CBFB::MYH11.** The fusion joins CBFβ's RUNX1-interaction domain to the
smooth-muscle myosin heavy chain tail. CBFβ-SMMHC **outcompetes wild-type CBFβ for RUNX1** and
multimerises through the myosin tail, sequestering RUNX1 into inert complexes [29]. Direct
pharmacological proof of this mechanism exists: **AI-10-49**, a protein-protein interaction
inhibitor that disrupts CBFβ-SMMHC:RUNX1 binding, restores RUNX1 activity, kills primary
inv(16) blasts and delays leukemia in mice **[preclinical]** [29]. No CBFβ-SMMHC inhibitor has
reached registration as of 9 Sep 2026.

Neither fusion is sufficient. t(8;21) is detectable in neonatal blood spots years before
disease onset, establishing an in-utero first hit with a long latency requiring cooperating
events [30]. Fusion-type heterogeneity matters for assay design and biology: *CBFB::MYH11*
type A accounts for **182/208 (87%)** of de novo inv(16), and the **26 (13%)** non-type-A cases
carried **no *KIT* mutations at all** (vs 27% of type A, P = .002) [31].

Phenotype follows genotype: t(8;21) shows dysgranulopoiesis, long slender Auer rods and aberrant
CD19/CD22/CD56; inv(16) shows myelomonocytic morphology, abnormal eosinophils and CD123/CD86
overexpression [6]. Both express CD33 densely — the rationale for antibody-drug conjugates.

## Epidemiology and co-mutation landscape

Frequencies are in the At-a-glance table. Two age patterns are firm: CBF fusions are
**enriched in children and adolescents** relative to adults [6,7], and **depleted in older
adults** [4]. This age gradient is one reason trial-derived CBF outcomes look better than
population outcomes — the trial cohorts are young.

The cooperating-mutation landscape splits the two entities cleanly. In **215 CBF-AML patients
aged 1-60 y** (French CBF-2006/ELAM02 trials), tyrosine-kinase-signalling mutations (*KIT*,
*N/KRAS*, *FLT3*) were frequent in both, but mutations in chromatin-conformation genes
(**42%**) and cohesin-complex genes (**18%**) were common in t(8;21) and **nearly absent in
inv(16)** [32]. Whole-genome/exome sequencing of **165 CBF-AML** (87 pediatric, 78 adult)
independently found *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* enriched for *ASXL2*, *ZBTB7A*, *DHX15*, cohesin and
epigenetic regulators, plus recurrent stabilising **CCND2** mutations as a shared cooperating
pathway [33]. Targeted 230-gene sequencing of **350 adult CBF-AML** added *SRCAP* (5%) and
*DNM2* (6% of t(8;21)), and showed the fusion arises before proliferative signalling
mutations [34].

Signalling-gene frequencies from the 1540-patient AMLSG genomic study: in the inv(16) class,
*NRAS* **53%**, *KIT* **15%**, *FLT3*-TKD **15%**, +8/8q **16%**; in the t(8;21) class, *KIT*
**38%**, loss of Y **33%**, del(9q) **18%** [3]. Secondary cytogenetic changes are non-random
and prognostically live: in 5876 younger MRC adults, additional abnormalities did **not** worsen
t(8;21), whereas in inv(16) additional changes — **particularly +22** — predicted a *better*
outcome (P = .004) [35].

## Prognostic significance

**The favourable label is real but oversold.** ELN 2022 places both fusions in the favorable
group without qualification [8,9]. Yet in the Alliance validation cohort, CBF-AML 5-year DFS
was **51-57%** and 5-year OS **63-66%** [9]. Long-term single-centre follow-up of **70 patients
uniformly given 7+3** (Mayo Clinic, 1998-2021; t(8;21) 31, inv(16) 39) gave CR/CRi **98.5%**,
relapse **45% (31/70)**, **5-year OS 61%, 10-year OS 50%, 10-year RFS 45%** [36]. CALGB 10801
investigators put long-term survival at "only ~50% to 60%" [37], and a Chinese registry found
CBF-AML DFS *inferior* to non-CBF AML [5]. The honest statement: **roughly half of adults with
CBF-AML are cured with chemotherapy alone; 30-40% relapse** [32,38]. Children do better: across
COG AAML0531/AAML1031 (n=427; t(8;21) 251, inv(16) 176), CR was **95.2%**, **10-year OS 84.4%**
and 10-year cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) **29.4%** [39].

**The two fusions are not one disease prognostically.** In the pediatric COG cohort, t(8;21) had
*lower* 10-year OS (80.7% vs 89.5%, P = .027) despite *lower* CIR (25.4% vs 35.2%, P = .026) —
inv(16) relapses more but is salvaged better, and carries a CNS-relapse excess (11.1% vs 2.3%)
[39]. In 779 adults at five Chinese centres, 3-year OS was **80.6% for *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* vs 90.2%
for *CBFB::MYH11*** (P = .011) [40].

***KIT*.** The founding CALGB analysis (61 inv(16), 49 t(8;21), all given higher-dose cytarabine
consolidation) found mutant *KIT* in 29.5% of inv(16) and 22% of t(8;21); CR rates were
unaffected, but 5-year CIR rose from 29% to 56% in inv(16) (80% for exon-17 mutations, P = .002)
and from 36% to 70% in t(8;21) (P = .017) [41]. A meta-analysis found *KIT* mutation raised
relapse risk in CBF-AML overall (**RR 1.43, 95% CI 1.20-1.70**) and in t(8;21) (**RR 1.70,
1.31-2.21**) but **not** OS (RR 1.09, 0.97-1.23), with **no significant effect in inv(16)** and
the t(8;21) OS effect confined to non-Caucasian cohorts [42]. Location matters: exon-17 (not
exon-8) mutations cause aberrant KIT phosphorylation and TKI-sensitive growth in transfection
assays, and on COG AAML0531 (63/205 *KIT*-mutant, 31%) exon-17 worsened DFS (51% vs 73%) whereas
exon-8 did not [43].

***FLT3*-ITD** is uncommon in CBF-AML. In an international collection of **97 CBF-AML with
*FLT3*-ITD**, 4-year RFS was **38%** in all cases except inv(16) with trisomy 22 (n=11, RFS
80%, P = .02), leading the authors to argue these patients "should not be classified as
favorable-risk"; allogeneic transplant in CR1 was **not** beneficial (P = .60) [44].

**RAS mutations behave differently**: high *N/KRAS* mutant allele ratios associated with absence
of *KIT*/*FLT3* mutations and a **favorable** outcome [32], and *NRAS* and *WT1* conferred
superior prognosis in LASSO modelling of 350 patients [34].

**MRD dominates, but does not abolish, genetics.** The prospective French CBF-2006 trial
(n=198) found higher WBC, *KIT* and/or *FLT3* mutations and <3-log MRD reduction all
associated with relapse univariately, but **MRD was the only independent factor**; 36-month CIR
was 22% vs 54% and RFS 73% vs 44% by 3-log MRD reduction after first consolidation (both
P < .001) [38]. The 2026 refinement (RetroCBF training n=461 + CBF-2006 validation n=195,
36-gene panel) is more nuanced: adjusting for MRD, **KIT-TKD in *RUNX1::RUNX1T1*** and
***FLT3*-ITD in *CBFB::MYH11*** remained independently prognostic — 3-year CIR **22% vs 53%**
(csHR 3.21, 95% CI 1.83-5.62, P < .0001) between the combined low- and high-risk groups [45].
Pediatric data disagree on *KIT*: in 289 children on C-HUANAN-AML-15 neither *KIT* (35.6%) nor
*FLT3*-ITD (5.5%) affected EFS, OS or CIR while post-cycle-2 MRD did [46]; a Taiwanese cohort
(n=112) did find *KIT* D816 lowered 5-year RFS in *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* (P = .017) [47].
**Treatment intensity plausibly modifies the *KIT* effect** — a hypothesis, not an established
interaction [46].

## Diagnostic testing and MRD

Diagnosis requires karyotype plus FISH and/or RT-PCR, and **molecular testing must not be gated
on blast count**: 16/196 CBF cases (8.2%) presented with <20% marrow blasts, some mimicking
MDS/MPN [6]. WHO-HAEM5 removed the 20% blast requirement for these entities [11]; ICC 2022
lowered it to ≥10% rather than abolishing it [14] — a divergence that changes whether a
low-blast case is called AML [12], though the therapy that follows is the same either way [13].

MRD monitoring uses fusion-transcript RT-qPCR normalised to a control gene (usually *ABL1*) with
the standardised Europe Against Cancer primer/probe sets [25]. ELN 2021 recommended qPCR or dPCR
reaching a limit of detection **≤10⁻³**, assessed **after 2 cycles (preferably blood)**, **at end
of consolidation (marrow)**, then **every 4-6 weeks in blood for 24 months**; molecular relapse =
conversion from negative to positive, or a **≥1 log₁₀ rise between two positive samples**,
confirmed within 4 weeks [22]. The **2025 ELN-DAVID update** (56 recommendations, 53 at ≥90%
consensus) keeps fusion-specific assays preferred over surrogate *WT1*/*MECOM* expression and
adds an optimal/warning/high-risk response grading [24].

Operational thresholds from the primary trials:

| Cohort | Timepoint | Threshold | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| MRC AML15, 278 pts (163 t(8;21), 115 inv(16)) [23] | post-induction | t(8;21): **>3-log** reduction in BM; inv(16): **>10** copies in PB | most informative multivariate predictors of relapse |
| MRC AML15 [23] | surveillance | t(8;21): BM >500 / PB >100 copies; inv(16): BM >50 / PB >10 copies | thresholds associated with a **100% relapse rate** |
| AMLSG, 155 *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* pts [48] | cycle 1 / cycle 2 / end of treatment | **MR2.5**, then **MR3.0**, then MRD-negative BM | lower relapse risk (P = .034; P = .028); at EOT 4-y CIR **17% vs 36%** (P = .021), 4-y OS **93% vs 70%** (P = .007) |
| AMLSG [48] | surveillance | BM >150 / PB >50 copies | predicted relapse in 77% / 84% |
| 186 *CBFB::MYH11* pts, Tianjin [49] | after cycle 2 | **≥1%** transcript | inferior DFS (HR 3.84, P < .001) and OS (HR 3.98, P = .003) |

Three practical cautions. (i) **Kinetics are fast**: 42/45 AMLSG relapses (93%) occurred within
18 months of diagnosis, with median molecular-to-morphologic latency 0.8 months (BM) /
1.6 months (PB) — surveillance intervals must be short [48]. (ii) **PB is adequate in follow-up
but not during therapy**: 44% of PB-negative samples had positive marrow on treatment [48].
(iii) **Low-level positivity is not automatically relapse** — CBF fusions persist at end of
treatment in patients who never relapse, and the ELN-DAVID 2025 document notes signal may derive
from non-leukemic mast cells [24]; ESMO/ELN accordingly recognise a CR-with-low-copy-number
category (<100-200 copies/10⁴ *ABL1*, i.e. <1-2% target-to-reference) handled as molecular
remission [16]. Systemic mastocytosis is *uncommon* in *KIT* D816V-positive CBF-AML, so such
signal is not generally explained by a second mast-cell neoplasm [50].

## Therapeutic implications

**High-dose cytarabine (HiDAC) — the historical foundation.** In 285 CALGB patients randomised to
100, 400 or 3000 mg/m² post-remission cytarabine, the dose effect was largest in the **57 CBF
patients**: 5-year continuous CR **78% vs 57% vs 16%** (P < .001), versus 40/37/20% in
normal-karyotype and ≤21% at any dose in other karyotypes [51]. That single observation is why
CBF-AML is treated with cytarabine-intensive consolidation; ESMO recommends **3 cycles of
intermediate-dose cytarabine** [II, B] [16]. Dose intensity at *induction* may matter for only
one fusion: in 536 Chinese patients ≤60 y, intermediate-dose induction improved
*RUNX1::RUNX1T1* outcomes (5-year OS 77.7% vs 60.3%, P < .001) but made **no difference in
*CBFB::MYH11*** (86.4% vs 85.3%, P = .99) [52]. A phase 3 randomisation of standard- vs
intermediate-dose induction restricted to *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* is recruiting (NCT06744504,
n=284) [21].

**Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO) — the strongest CBF-specific therapeutic claim, with caveats.**
The individual-patient-data meta-analysis of **5 randomised trials, 3325 adults** found GO did
not improve CR (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.77-1.07, P = .3) but reduced relapse (OR 0.81, 0.73-0.90,
P = .0001) and improved 5-year OS (OR 0.90, 0.82-0.98, P = .01); at **6 years the absolute
survival gain was 20.7% in favourable-cytogenetics patients (OR 0.47, 0.31-0.73, P = .0006)**
versus 5.7% intermediate and 2.2% (non-significant) adverse **[phase 3 positive, subgroup]**
[19]. Three honest caveats: the favourable-cytogenetics stratum was small (~250 patients) [19];
**ALFA-0701, the trial that established the approved fractionated 3+3+3 mg/m² schedule,
contributed only 9 of those ~251 favourable-risk patients (3.6%)** — so the approved regimen is
*not* the regimen that generated the CBF signal, most of whom received a single 3 mg/m² dose in
induction [16,17]; and MRC AML15 (n=1113), the largest contributor, showed **no overall**
response or survival difference, with benefit only in the predefined cytogenetic interaction
(P = .001) [53]. Registry corroboration arrived in 2025: in **265 CBF-AML patients** from the
SAL/AMLCG/CELL groups, GO gave 2-year OS **90% vs 80%** (HR 0.45, 0.21-0.95, P = .036), 2-year
EFS **51% vs 36%** (HR 0.69, 0.48-0.99, P = .046) and far more MRD-negative remissions
(**77% vs 49%**, P < .001) — retrospective [54]. In children, AAML0531 (n=1022) improved 3-year
EFS (53.1% vs 46.9%, HR 0.83, P = .04) but not OS (P = .39), with increased post-remission toxic
mortality (6.6% vs 4.1%) [55]; within CBF-AML, GO abrogated the adverse effect of exon-17 *KIT*
[43], and its benefit was concentrated in inv(16) (CIR 28.9% with GO vs 58.6% without,
P = .01) [39].

**Record the failures.** GO received accelerated FDA approval in 2000 and was **voluntarily
withdrawn from the US market in 2010** after a confirmatory trial failed and showed excess
fatal toxicity [15]. That trial, SWOG S0106 (n=637), used 6 mg/m² on day 4 and closed early for
excess fatal induction adverse events without CR or RFS improvement **[phase 3 negative]** [56].
GO was re-approved 1 Sep 2017 at fractionated 3 mg/m² dosing (days 1, 4, 7 induction; day 1
consolidation) [15,17] and **carries a boxed warning for hepatotoxicity including severe or fatal
hepatic veno-occlusive disease / sinusoidal obstruction syndrome** [17]; the indication reached
pediatric patients aged ≥1 month on 16 Jun 2020 [18]. The EU indication (23 Apr 2018) is
narrower: with daunorubicin and cytarabine, previously untreated de novo CD33-positive AML,
**age ≥15 years**, excluding APL [57].

**KIT inhibition — a cautionary tale in full.** Two single-arm studies looked convincing. AMLSG
11-08 (phase Ib/IIa, n=89) added dasatinib to induction, consolidation and 1-year maintenance:
4-year CIR **33.1%**, OS **74.7%** [58]. CALGB 10801 (phase 2, n=61) reported 3-year DFS **75%**
and OS **77%**, with *KIT*-mutant patients matching wild-type (3-year DFS 67% vs 75%) —
explicitly raising the question of whether dasatinib overcomes *KIT* [37]. The randomised answer,
published April 2026: in **202 adults with CBF-AML** (94 t(8;21), 108 inv(16); 58 (28.7%)
*KIT*-comutated), dasatinib **did not improve EFS (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.63-1.33, P = .66)** or any
secondary endpoint, with no benefit in any subgroup including *KIT*-mutated, and **serious adverse
events in 64% vs 36%** **[phase 3 negative]** (NCT02013648) [20]. Selective KIT inhibition is
being retested: **avapritinib** with standard induction in newly diagnosed *KIT*-mutant CBF-AML
(NCT07028073, phase 1/2, n=78) and in relapsed/refractory pediatric disease (NCT06316960, phase
2, n=50), both recruiting **[phase 1-2, no results retrieved]** [21].

**Transplant is MRD-directed, not routine.** ESMO recommends chemotherapy consolidation for
favourable-risk AML [I, A]; autologous transplant improves RFS but not OS in CBF-AML [II, B] [16].
Retrospective data suggest a role in MRD non-responders: among *RUNX1::RUNX1T1* patients not
clearing MRD after two consolidations, CR1 allo-HCT reduced relapse (8.3% vs 18.9%, P = .024) and
improved OS (85.8% vs 71.4% vs 64.9% for CR1-HCT vs later HCT vs chemotherapy, P < .001), while
for *CBFB::MYH11* deferring to CR2 gave comparable results [40]. In 186 *CBFB::MYH11* patients
with post-cycle-2 transcript ≥1%, CR1 allo-HCT gave 3-year DFS 91.7% vs 47.8% (HR 0.24,
P = .023) **but no OS difference** [49]. None of it is randomised.

## Resistance and relapse

Relapse is the dominant failure mode: **30-40%** after standard intensive therapy [32,38],
**45% at 10 years** in an unselected single-centre cohort [36]. It is early — 93% of relapses
within 18 months of diagnosis [48] — so the window for surveillance is narrow and front-loaded.

Molecular surveillance changes what relapse looks like. Among **303 patients with CBF or
*NPM1*-mutant AML** monitored after first-line intensive therapy without CR1 transplant, 51%
never relapsed, 31% had molecular relapse (53 treated pre-emptively, 42 progressing to
morphologic relapse first) and 18% relapsed morphologically outright; 3-year OS was **78% with
pre-emptive therapy vs 51% for salvage after progression vs 51% for upfront morphologic
relapse** (P = .01), pre-emptive allo-HCT performing best (92%). Allocation was not
randomised [59].

Salvage is comparatively good. Among **145 CBF-AML patients in first relapse** (French
intergroup), CR2 was **88%**, 5-year DFS 50% and OS 51%; GO-containing salvage gave higher
5-year DFS (68% vs 42%, P = .05) and OS (65% vs 44%, P = .02), retained in multivariable
analysis and in the 78 patients transplanted in CR2 — retrospective [60]. Anatomical patterns
differ: inv(16) carries the higher CNS relapse risk (11.1% vs 2.3% in children) [39], and
extramedullary disease occurred in 11.2% of a 196-case CBF series [6]. Venetoclax + azacitidine
+ G-CSF for HiDAC-resistant *CBFB::MYH11* molecular residual disease was first reported in 2026
**[phase 1-2 signal / small series]** [61].

Mechanism is only partly mapped. Signalling mutations (*KIT*, *FLT3*, *N/KRAS*) arise after the
fusion and after transcriptional/epigenetic lesions [34], making them plausible relapse drivers,
but **serial paired diagnosis-relapse sequencing across a large CBF cohort was not retrieved for
this monograph** — the trail goes cold here as of 9 Sep 2026.

## Open questions

1. **Does anything beat cytarabine-plus-GO?** No positive randomised CBF-restricted
   intervention exists; the one taken to definitive readout — dasatinib — failed [20].
2. **Is *KIT* actionable at all?** The exon-17 biology is convincing [43]; the phase 3
   dasatinib result is not [20]. Whether selective D816-active inhibitors succeed where a
   multikinase inhibitor failed is open [21].
3. **Should the two fusions be separated in risk classification and treated differently?**
   Co-mutation landscapes [32,33], induction dose-response [52], transplant benefit [40] and
   CNS risk [39] all differ; ELN 2022 still groups them [8].
4. **Which MRD-defined subgroup benefits from CR1 transplant, and by how much?** All supporting
   data are retrospective [40,49,52]; no randomised MRD-triggered transplant trial was retrieved.
5. **Can pre-emptive therapy at molecular relapse be shown to work prospectively?** The
   retrospective signal is large [59]; the randomised test is missing.
6. **What is the source of persistent low-level fusion transcript in cured patients?** The
   mast-cell hypothesis [24] is unresolved and affects how MRD results are acted on.
7. **Is the CBF benefit of GO reproducible with the approved fractionated schedule?** Never
   tested prospectively in a CBF-restricted randomised trial [16,19].
8. **Why does the *KIT* effect appear in adults but not consistently in children?** [42,43,46,47]

**Why CBF-AML is a cure paradigm — and where the paradigm stops.** It earns the label for four
reasons: a defining lesion with a mechanistically explained differentiation block [26,29]; a
chemotherapy dose-response shown in a randomised comparison specific to the subtype [51]; a
fusion transcript giving a quantitative, standardised, log-scale MRD readout usable in blood
[23,25,48]; and cure of roughly half of adults and ~80-85% of children with non-targeted therapy
[9,36,39]. The paradigm stops at the 30-40% who relapse. For them the lesion's druggability has
not translated: the CBFβ-SMMHC inhibitor remains preclinical after a decade [29], KIT inhibition
failed randomisation [20], and the one agent with a CBF-specific survival signal is an
untargeted CD33 conjugate resting on a ~250-patient meta-analysis subgroup [19]. A well-understood
driver plus a good MRD assay is enough to cure many patients — and not yet enough to cure the rest.

### Notes on evidence limits

- **Author strings** are abbreviated to the first three to six authors plus "et al." Journal,
  volume, pages, year, PMID and DOI were verified against NCBI E-utilities for every
  PubMed-indexed entry; NCT numbers were verified against the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API
  on the date of writing.
- **The ELN 2022 primary text could not be retrieved** (publisher returned HTTP 403 from
  three routes). The favorable-risk assignment of both CBF fusions is therefore cited to
  ref [8] as the authority but was *verified* against ref [9], which reproduces the ELN 2022
  favorable category, and ref [10].
- **The 2025 ELN-DAVID MRD consensus [24] full text could not be retrieved** (paywalled at
  both publisher routes). Its existence, authorship, journal, volume, pages, DOI and abstract
  were verified via PubMed. The statements attributed to it here (fusion-specific assays
  preferred over *WT1*/*MECOM*; optimal/warning/high-risk grading; the mast-cell explanation
  for persistent low-level positivity) come from indexed excerpts of the article, not from
  full-text reading. Numeric MRD-positivity ranges quoted in search excerpts of that article
  are deliberately **omitted** rather than reproduced.
- **The ESMO guideline [16]** recommendations quoted (7+3+GO in induction 1 [II, A]; 3 cycles
  intermediate-dose cytarabine [II, B]; GO in consolidation 1-2 optional [II, C]; chemotherapy
  consolidation for favourable risk [I, A]; the ALFA-0701 contribution of 9/251 favourable-risk
  patients) were read verbatim from the published PDF. Note ESMO 2020 predates ELN 2022 and
  uses ELN 2017 categories.
- **The 20.7% figure** for GO in favourable cytogenetics is the *6-year absolute* survival
  benefit from ref [19]'s own abstract. Absolute arm-level survival percentages (55.2% vs
  76.3%) were read from the full text; where the two differ slightly from ESMO's rendering
  (OS "to 75.5%"), the primary abstract figures are used.
- **Adult frequency** is anchored to ref [3] (n=1540, three AMLSG trials, intensively treated
  adults) because it is the largest cohort for which per-class counts were retrievable.
  Per-subgroup counts from the 5876-patient MRC cohort [35] could **not** be retrieved
  (publisher 403), so [35] is cited only for prognostic effects of additional abnormalities.
  Trial-enrolled cohorts systematically under-represent older and unfit patients, so these
  frequencies are not population incidence.
- **NCCN** listing of *KIT* mutation status as a prognostic modifier in CBF-AML was reported
  in secondary coverage; the **NCCN guideline document itself was not retrieved**, so no NCCN
  category is asserted here — **[unverified]**.
- **Paired diagnosis-relapse sequencing** in a large CBF cohort was searched for and not
  found; the resistance-mechanism section says so rather than substituting inference.
- **No CBF-AML data from 2025-2026 congresses (ASH/EHA) are cited.** Searches surfaced only
  peer-reviewed publications; abstract-only material was not retrieved and is therefore absent
  rather than paraphrased.
