# Intensive Chemotherapy and the Frontline Backbone in Acute Myeloid Leukemia

> Seven days of cytarabine with three days of an anthracycline — "7+3" — was first reported in 1973 [1] and is still the reference frontline regimen for adults judged fit for intensive therapy in 2026 [81]. Fifty-three years of randomized dose and schedule optimization have raised the complete remission rate to roughly 73–90% in younger adults, depending on trial and response definition [4,5,12] without solving the problem that actually kills patients: relapse from measurable residual disease. Every attempt to fix that by escalating cytotoxic dose has failed — daunorubicin 90 mg/m² is no better than 60 [4,5], induction cytarabine 2 g/m² is no better than 1 g/m² [9], consolidation cytarabine 3 g/m² is no better than 1.5 g/m² [12,15], a second induction in good early responders adds nothing to overall survival [5], and a fifth course is unnecessary [12]. The three additions that *did* improve survival in phase 3 trials — gemtuzumab ozogamicin [26,31], midostaurin [39], quizartinib [43] — are all biologically targeted, and each delivers a modest, subgroup-restricted gain layered onto an unchanged cytotoxic scaffold. Meanwhile the scaffold itself is being challenged: in 2025 a randomized phase 2 trial (PARADIGM) reported superior event-free survival for azacitidine–venetoclax over intensive induction in fit, transplant-eligible, FLT3-wild-type adults [73,74]. This domain matters for cure because the frontline backbone is where nearly all curative-intent AML therapy is still delivered, where most early treatment-related deaths occur, and where the field's central unresolved question now sits: whether cure requires maximal cytotoxic debulking at all.

## Key takeaways

- **Anthracycline dose is capped, not linear.** ECOG E1900 (N=657, ages 17–60) showed daunorubicin 90 mg/m² beat 45 mg/m² (CR 70.6% vs 57.3%, P<0.001; median OS 23.7 vs 15.7 months, P=0.003) [3], but the two trials that tested 90 against the *modern* 60 mg/m² both found no benefit: NCRI AML17 (N=1,206) found no CR difference (73% vs 75%, OR 1.07 [0.83–1.39], P=0.6) and higher 60-day mortality with 90 mg/m² (10% vs 5%, HR 1.98 [1.30–3.02], P=0.001) [4]; DaunoDouble (N=864, ages 18–65) found CRc 90% vs 89% (P=0.691) and 3-year OS 65% vs 58% (P=0.242) [5]. A cardiac substudy of 317 DaunoDouble patients found higher post-induction hs-troponin T with 90 mg/m² (median 9.5 vs 7 ng/L, P<0.01) [6].
- **Cytarabine dose is also capped.** HOVON/SAKK randomized 860 patients aged 18–60 to cumulative induction cytarabine ~10 g/m² vs ~26 g/m² and found the lower dose already produced maximal antileukemic effect [9]. In consolidation, the French BIG-1 trial (N=1,132 randomized, ages 18–60) established non-inferiority of intermediate-dose cytarabine 1,500 mg/m²/12h versus high-dose 3,000 mg/m²/12h: 5-year OS 59.3% vs 57.5% (adjusted HR 0.96 [0.80–1.15], non-inferiority P=0.0042) with *less* myelosuppression [15]. AML15 had already shown 1.5 g/m² equivalent to 3 g/m² [12].
- **A second induction cycle in good early responders is not beneficial for OS.** Among 389 DaunoDouble patients with day-15 marrow blasts <5%, single vs double induction gave 3-year RFS 51% vs 60% (HR 1.3, P=0.091) and 3-year OS 76% vs 75% (HR 1.0, P=0.937) [5].
- **CPX-351's pivotal win has not replicated.** The phase 3 registration trial (N=309, ages 60–75, high-risk/secondary AML) gave median OS 9.56 vs 5.95 months (HR 0.69 [0.52–0.90], one-sided P=0.003) and 5-year OS 18% vs 8% [17,18], supporting FDA approval on 3 August 2017 [19]. But NCRI AML18 (N=439, ≥60 years, non-adverse cytogenetics) found CPX-351 *inferior* to DA plus fractionated gemtuzumab ozogamicin (3-year OS 35% vs 52%, HR 0.62, P=0.001) [23]; NCRI AML19 (N=189, younger adverse-karyotype AML/high-risk MDS) found no OS difference versus FLAG-Ida (13.3 vs 11.4 months) [22]; and COG AAML1831 (N=721 FLT3-wild-type children/young adults) stopped its CPX-351 randomization for futility with 2-year EFS 51.2% vs 62.2% for DA (P=0.011) [24].
- **Gemtuzumab ozogamicin works, and the benefit is cytogenetically restricted.** The 3,325-patient individual-patient-data meta-analysis of five randomized trials found no CR gain (OR 0.91 [0.77–1.07], P=0.3) but reduced relapse (OR 0.81 [0.73–0.90], P=0.0001) and improved 5-year OS (OR 0.90 [0.82–0.98], P=0.01); absolute 6-year survival benefit was 20.7% in favourable cytogenetics, 5.7% in intermediate, and 2.2% (non-significant) in adverse [31]. Fractionated 3 mg/m² dosing caused fewer early deaths than 6 mg/m² at equal efficacy [31] — the pivotal distinction between the confirmatory trial that failed the drug's 2000 accelerated approval (SWOG S0106, single 6 mg/m² dose, negative on CR, RFS and OS) [29] and the trial that supported the 2017 re-approval (ALFA-0701, fractionated 3 mg/m²) [26,36].
- **Even in the "right" genotype, GO's effect can miss the primary endpoint.** AMLSG 09-09 randomized 588 NPM1-mutated patients and missed both co-primary endpoints (2-year OS 73% vs 69%, HR 0.90 [0.70–1.16], P=0.43) while significantly reducing 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse (25% vs 37%, cause-specific HR 0.65 [0.49–0.86], P=0.0028); treatment-related deaths were 6% vs 3% [33].
- **CD33 pharmacogenomics is a real but incompletely translated signal.** In COG AAML0531, the *CD33* splicing SNP rs12459419 determined GO benefit: patients with the CC genotype derived benefit; those carrying the T allele did not [35]. This has not been implemented in adult practice and no prospective genotype-stratified adult trial was located.
- **FLT3 inhibitor addition is the clearest modern gain, and it is small.** RATIFY (N=717 randomized of 3,277 screened, ages 18–59) gave OS HR 0.78 (one-sided P=0.009) with midostaurin [39]; QuANTUM-First (N=539, ages 18–75, FLT3-ITD only) gave median OS 31.9 vs 15.1 months (HR 0.78 [0.62–0.98], P=0.032) with quizartinib [43]. Both drugs are FDA-approved with intensive chemotherapy [41,44].
- **Not every FLT3 inhibitor helps.** Lestaurtinib added to UK AML15/AML17 chemotherapy in 500 randomized patients gave 5-year OS 46% vs 45% (HR 0.90 [0.70–1.15], P=0.3) [47]. Sorafenib in SORAML (N=267 analysed, ages 18–60, unselected for FLT3) improved 5-year EFS (41% vs 27%, HR 0.68, P=0.011) and 5-year RFS (53% vs 36%, HR 0.64, P=0.035) but not 5-year OS (61% vs 53%, HR 0.82, P=0.282) [48,49]. Crenolanib plus intensive chemotherapy remains phase 2 [51]. Gilteritinib versus midostaurin in HOVON 156 AML/AMLSG 28-18/PASHA (N=777 enrolled) **did not meet its primary endpoint of overall survival** [53,54,55].
- **Fitness instruments predict early death moderately well and long-term survival poorly.** The SIE/SIES/GITMO ("Ferrara") unfitness criteria [61] classified 30% of 655 intensively treated adults as unfit, with median OS 4.8 vs 36.8 months, and AUC 0.76/0.79 for day-28/day-100 mortality — better than a treatment-related-mortality score (0.66/0.62) but with OS c-statistic only ~0.75 even in augmented models [62]. The 2025 ELN expert panel explicitly reframes fitness as eligibility for *a specific therapy*, not a binary intensive/non-intensive verdict [68].
- **Early mortality has fallen in trials and registries but remains high in the real world.** SEER 1-month mortality among 9,380 treated patients ≤65 years fell from 18.7% (1973–77) to 5.8% (2008–10) [69]; but in 37,835 unselected non-M3 AML patients (median age 70) 1-month mortality was 33.4% overall, 52.8% in those >75, and 20.6% even among chemotherapy recipients [70]. In a Peruvian referral centre, 30-day mortality after 7+3 was 17.3% and 79.4% of early deaths were infectious [72].
- **The backbone itself is now under randomized challenge.** PARADIGM (phase 2, N=172, 86 per arm; fit, transplant-eligible, intermediate/adverse-risk, FLT3-wild-type) reported median EFS 14.5 vs 6.2 months favouring azacitidine–venetoclax over intensive induction (HR 0.57, P=0.0021), with median OS 21.5 vs 18.6 months (P=0.1873, not significant), transplant 61% vs 40% (P=0.009), and 30-day mortality 0% vs 3.5% [73,74,75]. Interpretation is limited by 67.6% crossover to venetoclax-based therapy in the chemotherapy arm and molecular imbalance between arms [74].

## 1. The 7+3 backbone: what 53 years of randomization settled

### 1.1 Origins and the shape of the dose–response curve

Cytarabine plus daunorubicin as a 7-day/3-day schedule was described by Yates and colleagues in 1973 [1]; the 1982 CALGB randomization of daunorubicin 45 vs 30 mg/m² versus doxorubicin 30 mg/m² established both an anthracycline dose effect in patients <60 years (CR 72% vs 59% vs 58%) and its reversal in patients >60 years (47% vs 31% vs 35%), with induction mortality shifting in parallel [2]. That single result contains the field's recurring lesson: cytotoxic dose intensity buys remission in the young and buys death in the old.

The modern anthracycline dose question was reopened by ECOG E1900, which randomized 657 patients aged 17–60 to daunorubicin 90 vs 45 mg/m² and found higher CR (70.6% vs 57.3%) and longer median OS (23.7 vs 15.7 months), with benefit concentrated in patients under 50 and in favourable/intermediate cytogenetics [3]. Because 60 mg/m² had by then become the common comparator, two subsequent trials asked the more relevant question, and both were negative (Table 1). NCRI AML17 randomized 1,206 mostly younger adults and found identical CR rates with doubled 60-day mortality in the 90 mg/m² arm [4]. DaunoDouble randomized 864 patients aged 18–65 and, after a preplanned interim analysis showed no response difference, moved all remaining patients to 60 mg/m²; final 3-year OS was 65% (60 mg/m²) vs 58% (90 mg/m²) [5]. A prospective cardiac substudy in 317 of those patients found a significant fall in median LVEF across both arms (65% → 61%, P<0.01) with dose-dependently higher post-induction hs-troponin T in the 90 mg/m² arm [6] — direct evidence that the extra dose is bought with myocardial injury and not survival.

Idarubicin versus daunorubicin has never resolved into a clear winner at modern doses. JALSG AML201 randomized standard-dose idarubicin against high-dose daunorubicin (50 mg/m² × 5) and found the regimens comparable [7]; a systematic review and meta-analysis of idarubicin versus high-dose daunorubicin reached the same conclusion [8].

### 1.2 Cytarabine dose, schedule, and the number of cycles

HOVON/SAKK randomized 860 patients aged 18–60 to two sequential induction cycles containing cytarabine 1,000 mg/m² q12h × 5 days then 2,000 mg/m² q12h × 4 days, versus the conventional 200 mg/m² continuous infusion × 7 days then 1,000 mg/m² q12h × 6 days. The lower-dose arm already achieved maximal antileukemic effect on all response endpoints — a plateau, not a gradient [9]. The two-cycle "double induction" standard against which later trials were measured was itself set by AMLCG randomization, which compared high-dose cytarabine plus mitoxantrone against standard-dose cytarabine, daunorubicin and 6-thioguanine as the second cycle [11]. Sequential high-dose cytarabine plus mitoxantrone (S-HAM), designed to shorten the interval between cycles, was later tested against standard double induction in a phase 3 trial and did not establish superiority [10].

The number of induction cycles is now addressed by direct randomization. In DaunoDouble, patients with day-15 marrow blasts <5% were randomized to a second 7+3 or not; CRc at end of induction was 87% (single) vs 85% (double), 3-year RFS 51% vs 60% (HR 1.3, P=0.091), and 3-year OS 76% vs 75% [5]. This is the strongest available evidence that response-adapted omission of a second induction is safe in good early responders.

Adding a nucleoside analogue is the one cytotoxic intensification with positive randomized data. The Polish PALG trial randomized daunorubicin+cytarabine ± cladribine ± fludarabine and found cladribine — but not fludarabine — prolonged survival [13]. In MRC AML15, FLAG-Ida produced a higher course-1 CR rate (77%), lower relapse (38% vs 55%, P<0.001) and better RFS (45% vs 34%, P=0.01) than ADE, at the cost of myelosuppression severe enough to reduce consolidation delivery; a fifth course provided no benefit, and MACE/MidAc was equivalent to high-dose cytarabine overall but superior in high-risk patients [12].

## 2. Consolidation: the dose question is now settled downward

CALGB 8525 established the modern high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC) consolidation paradigm: among 596 randomized patients in CR, 4-year continuous CR for patients ≤60 years was 24% (100 mg/m²), 29% (400 mg/m²) and 44% (3 g/m² q12h days 1/3/5), P=0.002, with no dose effect at all in patients >60 (≤16% in every arm) [14]. That trial has anchored practice for three decades — but it compared 3 g/m² against *low* doses, not against intermediate doses.

The gap was closed in 2025 by the French BIG-1 trial [15]. Patients aged 18–60 with newly diagnosed AML (excluding core-binding-factor, APL, Ph-positive and post-MPN AML) were randomized after induction to intermediate-dose cytarabine 1,500 mg/m²/12h or high-dose 3,000 mg/m²/12h; 1,132 randomized patients were analysed per protocol, adjusted for ELN 2022 risk, induction anthracycline, induction response and time-dependent transplant. Five-year OS was 59.3% (95% CI 55.0–63.3) with IDAC versus 57.5% (53.3–61.5) with HDAC, adjusted HR 0.96 (0.80–1.15), non-inferiority P=0.0042; no interaction with any prespecified subgroup was detected, and myelosuppression and related adverse events were *lower* after IDAC [15]. Together with the AML15 finding that 1.5 g/m² equals 3 g/m² [12], the consolidation cytarabine dose question is answered: higher is not better, and lower is safer. Whether specific genotypes (notably core-binding-factor AML, deliberately excluded from BIG-1) retain a HiDAC-specific benefit remains an open predictive-biomarker question [16].

## 3. CPX-351: a reformulation whose benefit has not replicated

CPX-351 is a liposome co-encapsulating daunorubicin and cytarabine at a fixed 1:5 molar ratio. Its registration trial randomized 309 patients aged 60–75 with newly diagnosed high-risk or secondary AML to CPX-351 or 7+3 and reported median OS 9.56 vs 5.95 months (HR 0.69 [0.52–0.90], one-sided P=0.003) [17]; 5-year follow-up gave median OS 9.33 vs 5.95 months (HR 0.70 [0.55–0.91]) and 5-year OS 18% vs 8% [18]. FDA approved it on 3 August 2017 for newly diagnosed therapy-related AML or AML with myelodysplasia-related changes [19], with pediatric extension in 2021. A post hoc analysis of the registration trial stratified by ELN 2017 risk subgroup was also published [21].

Three subsequent randomized comparisons have failed to reproduce a survival advantage, and one showed harm (Table 2). A 2026 re-analysis of the pivotal trial by MDS-related mutation status reported that the benefit was concentrated in patients with myelodysplasia-related mutations rather than in the clinically defined high-risk/secondary population as a whole [20] — a hypothesis that AML18 then contradicted, finding no CPX-351 OS benefit in MDS-related-mutation carriers (HR 1.40 [0.97–2.03]) and clear harm in NPM1-mutated (HR 2.83) and FLT3-mutated (HR 2.14) patients [23]. AML18 also documented slower count recovery (neutrophils 34 vs 31 days, P=0.033) and higher day-60 mortality (11% vs 4%, P=0.019) with CPX-351 [23].

The honest reading is that CPX-351 remains approved on one positive trial in one narrowly defined older population and has since lost to DA+GO in fit older adults without adverse cytogenetics [23], tied with FLAG-Ida in younger adverse-karyotype disease [22], and been shown inferior to DA in children [24]. Its RFS advantage in AML19 [22] and its possible enrichment in MDS-related genotypes [20] are the surviving signals, and they are contested by AML18 [23].

## 4. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin: the drug that came back

Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO) is a humanized anti-CD33 antibody conjugated to calicheamicin. Its history is the field's clearest case study in how dose and schedule, not target validity, decide whether a drug works.

ALFA-0701 randomized 280 patients aged 50–70 with de novo AML to standard DA ± fractionated GO 3 mg/m² on days 1, 4, 7 of induction plus day 1 of each of two consolidations. Two-year EFS was 40.8% vs 17.1% (HR 0.58 [0.43–0.78], P=0.0003), 2-year OS 53.2% vs 41.9% (HR 0.69 [0.49–0.98], P=0.0368), and 2-year RFS 50.3% vs 22.7% (HR 0.52 [0.36–0.75], P=0.0003); persistent thrombocytopenia was more common with GO (16% vs 3%, P<0.0001) [26]. The final blinded independent review in 271 analysable patients confirmed EFS (HR 0.66 [0.49–0.89], P=0.006) but final OS favoured GO non-significantly; veno-occlusive disease, including post-transplant, was observed [27]. SWOG S0106, using a single 6 mg/m² dose with a reduced daunorubicin backbone in 637 patients, was negative on CR (69% vs 70%), 5-year RFS (43% vs 42%) and 5-year OS (46% vs 50%), and post-consolidation GO did not improve DFS [29].

The 3,325-patient individual-patient-data meta-analysis reconciled these results [31]: GO does not increase CR (OR 0.91, P=0.3), does reduce relapse (OR 0.81, P=0.0001) and does improve 5-year OS (OR 0.90, P=0.01), with 6-year absolute survival benefit of 20.7% in favourable cytogenetics (OR 0.47 [0.31–0.73], P=0.0006), 5.7% in intermediate (OR 0.84, P=0.005) and 2.2% in adverse (OR 0.99, P=0.9); and 3 mg/m² caused fewer early deaths than 6 mg/m² at equal efficacy [31]. FDA re-approved GO on 1 September 2017 for newly diagnosed CD33-positive AML in adults on the basis of ALFA-0701 [36], and extended the newly diagnosed indication to pediatric patients ≥1 month on 16 June 2020 [37] on the basis of COG AAML0531 (N=1,063, EFS HR 0.84 [0.71–0.99], 5-year freedom from induction failure/relapse/death 48% vs 40%, no overall survival difference) [34].

Two more recent randomized results refine the picture. NCRI AML18 randomized 852 older adults to GO on day 1 versus days 1+4: CR after course 1 was 57% vs 63% (P=0.08) but MRD-negative CR (<0.1%) was significantly more frequent with the fractionated schedule (41% vs 50%, P=0.027), with the differential greatest in IDH-mutated disease; 5-year OS was 24% vs 29% (HR 0.89, P=0.14), reaching significance (26% vs 33%, HR 0.83, P=0.045) only when adverse cytogenetics and TP53 mutations were excluded — and the advantage disappeared when censoring at transplant, i.e. deeper remission mattered because it enabled transplant [32]. AMLSG 09-09 randomized 588 NPM1-mutated patients and missed both co-primary endpoints while halving relapse; CR rate was *lower* with GO (47% vs 58%, OR 0.63, P=0.0068) and treatment-related deaths were 6% vs 3% [33].

Safety is the recurring constraint: hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome/veno-occlusive disease, including after subsequent transplant [27,39], is the dose-limiting organ toxicity, and prolonged thrombocytopenia is near-universal at fractionated dosing [26].

## 5. FLT3 inhibitors on the backbone

RATIFY (Alliance CALGB 10603) screened 3,277 patients aged 18–59 to randomize 717 with FLT3 mutations to 7+3 plus midostaurin or placebo, with high-dose cytarabine consolidation, transplant permitted, and 12 cycles of maintenance. OS favoured midostaurin (HR 0.78, one-sided P=0.009) as did EFS (HR 0.78, P=0.002), consistently across ITD-high, ITD-low and TKD subtypes, with similar severe-adverse-event rates [39]. A post hoc analysis showed reduced cumulative incidence of relapse (HR 0.71 [0.54–0.93], P=0.01) when post-transplant events counted as events — but *no* significant difference when transplant was treated as a competing risk, and the maintenance phase's contribution was inconclusive [40]. Midostaurin was FDA-approved on 28 April 2017 in combination with standard cytarabine and daunorubicin induction and cytarabine consolidation [41]. AMLSG 16-10 provided single-arm confirmation extending to older patients with FLT3-ITD [42].

QuANTUM-First screened 3,468 patients to randomize 539 with FLT3-ITD (ages 18–75) to quizartinib or placebo with 7+3 induction, HiDAC consolidation and/or transplant, then up to 3 years of single-agent continuation. Median OS was 31.9 vs 15.1 months (HR 0.78 [0.62–0.98], P=0.032) at 39.2 months' median follow-up; CR rate was 55% in both arms and median CR duration 38.6 vs 12.4 months [43,44]. Quizartinib was approved 20 July 2023 with a boxed warning for QT prolongation, torsades de pointes and cardiac arrest and a REMS programme [44]. Subsequent analyses examined the interaction with transplant [45] and the contribution of the continuation phase [46].

The negative and equivocal results matter as much. Lestaurtinib added to AML15/AML17 chemotherapy in 500 randomized patients produced no OS or RFS benefit, although patients achieving sustained >85% FLT3 inhibition did better — a pharmacodynamic, not a randomized, finding [47]. SORAML randomized 267 analysable patients aged 18–60 (unselected for FLT3) and improved event-free survival (median 21 vs 9 months; 3-year 40% vs 22%, HR 0.64, P=0.013) at the cost of significantly more grade 3 or worse diarrhoea, bleeding, cardiac events and hand-foot-skin reaction [48]; at 78 months’ median follow-up, 5-year EFS was 41% vs 27% (HR 0.68, P=0.011) and 5-year RFS 53% vs 36% (HR 0.64, P=0.035), but 5-year OS was 61% vs 53% (HR 0.82, P=0.282), with 88% of relapsed patients receiving allogeneic transplant [49] — while SORMAIN showed benefit for sorafenib *maintenance after transplant* [50] — evidence that FLT3 inhibition may matter more post-remission than during induction. Crenolanib plus intensive chemotherapy remains at phase 2 [51], and gilteritinib plus intensive chemotherapy at phase 1b [52]. Most consequentially, HOVON 156 AML/AMLSG 28-18/PASHA — the only head-to-head phase 3 of two FLT3 inhibitors on the backbone, N=777 enrolled [53] — did not meet its primary endpoint of overall survival [54]; secondary reporting from EHA 2026 describes median OS not reached in either arm, a large numerical EFS difference (51.1 vs 19.9 months) that did not reach significance, and numerically higher early mortality with gilteritinib [55].

## 6. Salvage and maximally intensive regimens

FLAG-Ida is the reference high-intensity regimen in both frontline high-risk and relapsed/refractory settings. NCRI AML17 randomly compared FLAG-Ida with daunorubicin plus clofarabine in relapsed/refractory AML [56]. Adding venetoclax to FLAG-Ida in a phase 1b/2 study at a single institution [57] produced the highest reported remission depth for any intensive regimen: in the long-term report of 138 patients (77 newly diagnosed, 61 relapsed/refractory), newly diagnosed patients had ORR 97%, CRc 95%, flow-MRD-undetectable 90%, 3-year OS 66% and EFS 64% with outcomes similar across ELN 2022 risk groups and 64% transitioning to allogeneic transplant in CR1; relapsed/refractory patients had ORR 67% and CRc 41%, with first-salvage TP53-wild-type patients reaching CRc 74% and 3-year OS 51% [58]. These are single-institution phase 1b/2 data, not randomized — the survival figures are inseparable from patient selection and a 64% transplant rate.

Venetoclax has also been combined with dose-attenuated 7+3 (CAVEAT, a phase 1b in patients ≥65 including prior MDS/HMA exposure) [59] and with CLAG-M [25]. Quizartinib plus FLAG-IDA in relapsed/refractory FLT3-ITD AML reached phase I–II in the PETHEMA FLAG-QUIDA trial [60]. In a randomized phase 2 restricted to medically unfit adults (TRM score ≥13.1), CLAG-M outperformed CPX-351 on the primary 3-month OS endpoint and, in patients with proliferative disease, on OS (18.5 vs 3.9 months, P=0.02) [25].

## 7. Who is "intensive-eligible"? Fitness assessment

The SIE/SIES/GITMO consensus ("Ferrara") criteria define unfitness for intensive chemotherapy through organ-function and comorbidity thresholds rather than age [61]. Their most rigorous validation applied them retrospectively to 655 adults who actually received intensive therapy (7+3, CLAG-M or reduced-dose CLAG-M): 197 (30%) were unfit by at least one criterion, with median OS 4.8 months (95% CI 3.6–6.5) versus 36.8 months (27.4–73.0) for fit patients. AUC for day-28 and day-100 mortality was 0.76 and 0.79 — better than a previously developed treatment-related mortality score (0.66, 0.62) — improving to 0.84–0.85 when performance status and albumin were added, but with an OS c-statistic of at most 0.75 [62]. The criteria have also been validated to predict early mortality in patients receiving hypomethylating agent plus venetoclax [64], meaning "unfitness" identifies frail patients rather than patients specifically unsuited to intensive chemotherapy.

Complementary instruments capture different axes. The AML-Composite Model, developed in 733 and validated in 367 of 1,100 patients aged 20–89 across five centres, combines an augmented HCT-CI (adding hypoalbuminemia, thrombocytopenia and high LDH), age and cytogenetic/molecular risk to predict 1-year mortality with c-statistic 0.72 and AUC 0.76 [63]. Geriatric assessment adds independent prognostic information beyond performance status in older adults receiving induction [65]. The pretreatment TRM score predicts early death and was proposed as a treatment-assignment tool [66], though its authors later argued explicitly that available predictive models are too imprecise to assign initial therapy [67].

The 2025 ELN expert panel recommendations mark a conceptual shift: fitness should express eligibility for *a specific therapeutic programme* among several, not a binary intensive-versus-non-intensive verdict, and should integrate quality of life, patient-reported physical and social function, patient preference, social support and early palliative care [68]. This reframing follows from the 2026 reality that lower-intensity options include an all-oral regimen — decitabine/cedazuridine plus venetoclax, FDA-approved 13 May 2026 for adults ≥75 years or with comorbidities precluding intensive induction (ASTX727-07, N=101, CR 41.6% [95% CI 31.9–51.8]) [71].

## 8. Induction mortality

Early death after intensive induction has fallen substantially in selected, treated populations and remains high in the population as a whole. Among 9,380 SEER patients aged ≤65 treated with chemotherapy, 1-month mortality fell from 18.7% (1973–77) to 5.8% (2008–10) while median OS rose from 6 to 23 months [69]. But in an unselected SEER analysis of 37,835 non-M3 AML patients (median age 70), 1-month mortality was 33.4% and 2-month mortality 39.7%, reaching 52.8% and 62.3% respectively in patients >75; chemotherapy receipt was associated with 1-month mortality of 20.6% versus 61.2% without (adjusted OR 0.19 [0.18–0.20]), and era-wise 1-month mortality declined only from 37.1% (1975–2005) to 30.7% (2006–17) to 29.1% (2018–22) [70]. In a Peruvian national referral centre, 139 adults treated with 7+3 (2020–2024) had 30-day and 60-day mortality of 17.3% and 24.5%, with 79.4% of deaths infectious, ICU admission the strongest predictor (adjusted HR 12.88 [4.87–34.08]) and age ≥60 *not* an independent predictor [72]. Trial-derived induction mortality figures — 3.5–5% at 30 days in PARADIGM's chemotherapy arm [74], 4–11% at day 60 in AML18 [23] — therefore describe a highly selected population and should not be generalized.

## 9. Intensive versus lower-intensity therapy: the randomized challenge

PARADIGM is the first randomized trial to test venetoclax-based therapy against intensive induction in patients who were *fit* for intensive therapy. Presented at ASH 2025 (Abstract 6) [73], it randomized 172 previously untreated, transplant-eligible adults (86 per arm; median age 64, 51% <65) with intermediate- or adverse-risk, FLT3-wild-type AML — excluding core-binding-factor fusions, FLT3 mutations, and NPM1 mutations except in patients >60 — to azacitidine–venetoclax or intensive induction (7+3 or CPX-351) [74]; the registered protocol is NCT04801797 [75]. Reported results: median EFS 14.5 vs 6.2 months (HR 0.57, P=0.0021; adjusted HR 0.66, P=0.0231), 1-year EFS 53.4% vs 36.0%; median OS 21.5 vs 18.6 months (P=0.1873, not significant); overall response 88% vs 62% (P<0.0001); CR 59% vs 50% (P=0.066); transplant 61% vs 40% (P=0.009); 30-day mortality 0% vs 3.5% and 60-day 0% vs 4.7%; ICU admission 0% vs 9.8% (P=0.003); grade ≥3 infection 15.1% vs 20.8% and grade ≥3 bleeding 1.3% vs 6.3%; better quality of life and fewer hospitalizations [74]. The investigators' own stated caveats are essential: 67.6% of chemotherapy-arm patients who relapsed or were refractory received hypomethylating agent plus venetoclax off study, molecular profiles were imbalanced despite randomization (NPM1/IDH2 35% vs 16%; TP53 11% vs 19%), and the excluded subgroups — patients <60 with FLT3 or NPM1 mutations and core-binding-factor AML — are exactly those in whom intensive therapy has its best-documented benefit [74].

Supporting and conflicting non-randomized evidence exists. A propensity-score-adjusted cohort of 246 patients aged 60–75 (172 on 7+3, 74 on HMA+venetoclax) found composite CR lower with HMA+venetoclax (51.4% vs 66.9%, P=0.025; not significant in weighted regression, OR 0.65 [0.32–1.32]), adjusted median OS 9.5 vs 18.0 months (P=0.14) and adjusted median EFS 7.0 vs 7.2 months (P=0.91), concluding the analysis was probably underpowered and that a randomized trial is required [77]. In 1,357 NPM1-mutated patients from NCRI AML17/AML19, intensified FLAG-Ida chemotherapy was associated with improved outcomes across all molecular subgroups, with larger benefit in high-risk subgroups — a direct counterweight in the genotype most often proposed for de-escalation [78]. A German report describes VINCENT as a randomized comparison of venetoclax–azacitidine versus intensive chemotherapy in newly diagnosed NPM1-mutated AML [76].

## 10. What is being tested now (retrieved 2026-09-09)

The backbone is being modified in four directions simultaneously: menin inhibitors added to 7+3, venetoclax added to 7+3, MRD-adapted arm selection, and lesion-specific de-escalation.

- **Menin inhibitors + 7+3.** A review of ASH 2025 reports revumenib + intensive chemotherapy in a phase 1b (12 enrolled, 9 evaluable; CR in 8/9; one grade 5 typhlitis as DLT; no differentiation syndrome) and bleximenib + intensive chemotherapy in a phase 1b (safety n=44; efficacy n=24 at 100 mg twice daily; ORR 95.8%, composite CR 87.5%, CR/CRh 75%) [79]. Two phase 3 programmes are recruiting: ziftomenib with either venetoclax–azacitidine or 7+3 in untreated NPM1-mutated or KMT2A-rearranged AML (NCT07007312, estimated N=1,300, EFS primary for the intensive study), and revumenib + intensive chemotherapy in newly diagnosed NPM1-mutated AML (REVEAL-ND NPM1, NCT07211958, estimated N=468, co-primary EFS and MRD-negative CR rate) [80].
- **myeloMATCH.** The NCI platform includes a randomized phase 2 comparing 7+3 with CPX-351, 7+3+venetoclax, azacitidine+venetoclax and a further liposomal arm, with MRD response as primary endpoint (NCT05554406, estimated N=335), and a phase 2 comparing 7+3+GO with 7+3+venetoclax in core-binding-factor AML with MRD-negative CR as primary endpoint (NCT06917911, estimated N=162) [80].
- Evidence trail: **no phase 3 randomized trial of venetoclax added to standard-dose 7+3 with a reported primary result was located** as of 2026-09-09; the venetoclax-plus-intensive-chemotherapy literature remains phase 1b/2 and single-institution [25,57,58,59].

## What is established vs contested

**Established (randomized, replicated, or regulatory):**
1. Cytarabine plus an anthracycline for 7 and 3 days remains the reference intensive induction; daunorubicin 60 mg/m² is the appropriate dose and 90 mg/m² adds toxicity without survival benefit [4,5,6].
2. Induction and consolidation cytarabine exhibit a dose plateau: ~1 g/m² per dose in induction [9] and 1.5 g/m² per dose in consolidation [12,15] capture the full effect.
3. A second induction cycle is not required in patients with day-15 marrow blasts <5% [5]; a fifth course is unnecessary [12].
4. GO added to induction reduces relapse and improves survival in favourable and intermediate cytogenetics, not in adverse; 3 mg/m² fractionated dosing is the correct schedule [26,31,36].
5. Midostaurin (FLT3-mutated) and quizartinib (FLT3-ITD) added to 7+3 improve overall survival and are FDA-approved [39,41,43,44].
6. CPX-351 improves survival versus 7+3 in newly diagnosed high-risk/secondary AML aged 60–75 in its single registration trial [17,18,19].
7. Ferrara/SIE-SIES-GITMO unfitness criteria predict short-term mortality after intensive chemotherapy better than chance and better than a TRM score, but do not predict long-term survival well [62].
8. Early mortality after intensive induction in the general AML population is far higher than in trials, and is driven by age, infection and clinical severity at presentation [70,72].

**Contested or unresolved:**
1. Whether CPX-351 has any population in which it beats a modern comparator — it lost to DA+GO in AML18 [23], tied with FLAG-Ida in AML19 [22], and lost to DA in AAML1831 [24]; the MDS-related-mutation subgroup claim is supported by one re-analysis [20] and contradicted by another trial [23].
2. Whether GO should be given in NPM1-mutated AML: relapse falls but the primary endpoints failed and CR rate fell [33], while GO2 benefit in AML18 depended entirely on subsequent transplant [32].
3. Whether the FLT3 inhibitor benefit is an induction effect, a maintenance effect, or a transplant-enabling effect [40,45,46,50].
4. Whether type-2 (quizartinib) and type-1 (gilteritinib) FLT3 inhibitors differ clinically on the backbone — the only head-to-head phase 3 missed its OS endpoint [54,55].
5. Whether intensive induction should remain the default for fit adults with intermediate/adverse-risk, FLT3-wild-type AML [73,74] — and whether the PARADIGM EFS advantage survives crossover-adjusted, adequately powered OS analysis.
6. Whether CD33 genotype should gate GO use in adults [35].
7. Whether core-binding-factor AML retains a high-dose-cytarabine-specific consolidation benefit, since BIG-1 excluded it [15,16].

## Barriers to cure in this domain

1. **The cytotoxic dose–response curve is flat above modest doses, so intensification cannot deliver deeper remissions.** Four independent randomizations (E1900 vs AML17/DaunoDouble for anthracycline [3,4,5]; HOVON/SAKK for induction cytarabine [9]; CALGB 8525 vs AML15/BIG-1 for consolidation cytarabine [12,14,15]) converge on plateaus. The rate-limiting factor is not drug delivery but the intrinsic chemoresistance of the residual leukemic population.
2. **Normal hematopoietic and mucosal stem cells share the proliferative vulnerability being exploited, so the therapeutic index is set by neutropenia duration, not by leukemia biology.** AML18's day-60 mortality difference tracked count-recovery time (34 vs 31 days) rather than antileukemic potency [23]; infection caused 79.4% of early deaths in a real-world 7+3 cohort [72].
3. **Anthracycline cardiotoxicity is cumulative, dose-dependent, and already measurable during induction**, capping lifetime re-treatment and constraining salvage. Median LVEF fell 4 points and hs-troponin T rose within 15 days of a single induction, more so at 90 mg/m² [6].
4. **The backbone was optimized in, and its supporting evidence derives from, a population that excludes most patients with AML.** RATIFY enrolled ages 18–59 [39]; BIG-1 ages 18–60 and excluded CBF, APL, Ph-positive and post-MPN AML [15]; PARADIGM excluded CBF, FLT3-mutated and (under 60) NPM1-mutated disease [74]. Population 1-month mortality of 33.4% [70] describes patients these trials never enrolled.
5. **Genotype-specific resistance is not addressed by intensity.** TP53-mutated disease dominated AML19's adverse-karyotype cohort (43%) with median OS 11.4–13.3 months regardless of arm [22], and FLAG-IDA+venetoclax data explicitly identify TP53 mutation as the subgroup where deep remissions do not translate [57,58].
6. **Every successful addition to the backbone works by reducing relapse, not by increasing remission — and its survival value is frequently contingent on transplant.** GO increases MRD-negative CR without increasing CR [31,32]; AML18's GO2 survival advantage vanished on censoring at transplant [32]; RATIFY's relapse reduction disappeared when transplant was treated as a competing risk [40]. Intensive chemotherapy is functioning as a bridge, which means backbone improvements are bounded by transplant access and transplant outcomes.
7. **Fitness assessment cannot yet identify who benefits from intensity, only who dies early.** Ferrara criteria achieve AUC ~0.76–0.85 for day-28/day-100 mortality but c-statistic ≤0.75 for OS [62], and the same criteria predict early death under venetoclax-based therapy too [64] — so they measure frailty, not treatment-specific benefit [67,68].
8. **Approvals rest on single trials whose comparators have since changed.** CPX-351's approval comparator was 7+3 without GO; the fair modern comparator (DA+GO2) beat it [19,23]. This creates a standing risk that the approved backbone in a given subgroup is not the best available one.
9. **Reformulation-based strategies can be actively harmful in genotypes with good chemosensitivity.** CPX-351 was worse than DA in NPM1-mutated (HR 2.83) and FLT3-mutated (HR 2.14) older adults [23] and in low-risk pediatric AML (2-year CIR 39.9% vs 23.6%) [24], plausibly by altering dose intensity and delivery kinetics in disease that responds well to conventional scheduling.
10. **The single-agent-addition paradigm produces increments too small to close the gap to cure.** Each positive addition yields an OS hazard ratio near 0.78 [39,43] or an absolute 6-year subgroup survival gain of 2–21% [31], and the best current 3- to 5-year OS figures in fit patients remain roughly 52% (older adults, DA+GO2 [23]) to 59% (ages 18–60 after consolidation, BIG-1 [15]). Composing multiple increments requires factorial or platform designs, which the field has only just begun to run [80].

## Open research questions

1. In fit adults with intermediate/adverse-risk, FLT3-wild-type AML, does venetoclax–azacitidine achieve non-inferior or superior *crossover-adjusted overall survival* versus intensive induction in an adequately powered phase 3 with prespecified molecular stratification (NPM1/IDH2 and TP53), transplant as a planned covariate, and hospitalization/QoL as co-primary endpoints? (Hypothesis generated by PARADIGM [73,74]; contradicted for NPM1 by [78].)
2. Can a prospective MRD-adapted design formally test whether patients with day-15 marrow blasts <5% and MRD-negative CR after one induction can omit both a second induction and consolidation intensification without loss of RFS, using DaunoDouble's second randomization as the effect-size prior [5]?
3. Does core-binding-factor AML — excluded from BIG-1 [15] — retain a high-dose-cytarabine-specific consolidation benefit, and is that benefit modified by KIT mutation status? A CBF-restricted randomization of 1.5 vs 3 g/m² with molecular MRD endpoints would settle a 30-year-old assumption.
4. Should GO use in adults be gated by *CD33* rs12459419 genotype? A prospective genotype-stratified randomization is required to translate the AAML0531 pharmacogenomic finding into adult practice [35].
5. Why did CPX-351 harm NPM1- and FLT3-mutated older adults and low-risk children [23,24]? A pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic study comparing intramedullary drug exposure, DNA-damage response and MRD clearance kinetics between CPX-351 and 7+3 in matched genotypes would distinguish delivery-kinetic from dose-intensity explanations.
6. Is the FLT3 inhibitor survival benefit attributable to induction, consolidation, continuation, or transplant enablement? A factorial randomization of inhibitor exposure by phase (induction only vs induction+continuation vs post-transplant maintenance) is feasible given SORMAIN's positive maintenance result [50] and RATIFY's inconclusive maintenance analysis [40].
7. Can a fitness instrument be developed that predicts *differential benefit* between intensive and venetoclax-based therapy rather than frailty alone — for example, by fitting treatment-by-covariate interaction models on pooled randomized data, as the 2025 ELN panel's reframing implies is needed [62,64,67,68]?
8. Does reducing neutropenia duration by pharmacological means (rather than by reducing dose) improve survival after intensive induction, given that day-60 mortality differences in AML18 tracked count recovery [23] and that infection caused ~80% of real-world early deaths [72]?
9. What is the maximum tolerated *composition* of backbone additions — e.g. 7+3 + GO + FLT3 inhibitor + venetoclax + menin inhibitor in permissive genotypes — and can a platform trial with MRD-negative CR as an intermediate endpoint (as myeloMATCH is designed to do [80]) identify additive from redundant components?
10. Can trial eligibility be widened to the population that actually bears AML mortality — patients >75, ECOG 3–4, and those in low- and middle-income settings where 30-day mortality after 7+3 is 17.3% [72] — using pragmatic randomization with mortality and days-at-home endpoints, so that intensity decisions stop being extrapolated from cohorts that exclude the majority [70]?

- **Where the trail goes cold.** No phase 3 randomized trial of venetoclax added to *standard-dose* 7+3 with a reported primary endpoint was located as of 2026-09-09. No published full report of HOVON 156 AML was located — only the company press release [54] and secondary conference reporting [55]. No published full paper of PARADIGM was located; the ASH abstract page returned HTTP 403 and all PARADIGM numbers here derive from secondary coverage [74] cross-checked against the ClinicalTrials.gov record [75].
- **Discrepancies left visible rather than resolved.** (i) HOVON 156 OS hazard ratio is reported as 1.02 in one secondary source and 0.98 (95% CI 0.81–1.28, P=0.864) in another; the confidence interval is identical in both, so at least one figure is a transcription error, and neither could be checked against a primary report. (ii) PARADIGM composite remission is reported as 78% vs 54% in one secondary account and 81% vs 55% in another [74]; the figures quoted in the body of this file are from [74]. (iii) ALFA-0701 is described as 280 randomized (139 analysed per arm) in the primary publication [26] and as n=271 in the final update [27] and the FDA review [36]; both are stated.
- **Chronology documented through a regulatory-review publication, not a retrieved FDA notice.** Gemtuzumab ozogamicin received accelerated approval in 2000 for relapsed CD33-positive AML and was voluntarily withdrawn in 2010 after confirmatory data failed to verify benefit; that sequence is documented here through the FDA reviewers' 2018 approval paper [36] and the S0106 publication [29], not through a retrieved FDA withdrawal notice.
- **Not independently verified.** The 2017 midostaurin approval date (28 April 2017) is documented here through a *Blood* commentary [41] rather than a retrieved FDA approval notice; the FDA press page for that approval returned HTTP 404 on the URL located. The CPX-351 pediatric label expansion (30 March 2021) was described only in secondary coverage during this search and is therefore **not** cited as a reference. The bleximenib and revumenib plus-intensive-chemotherapy numbers in section 10 come from a review of conference abstracts [79], not from the abstracts themselves.
- **Cross-links.** Risk classification and ELN 2022/2024 stratification are covered in [`01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md`](01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md); disease biology underlying chemoresistance in [`02-disease-biology-genomics.md`](02-disease-biology-genomics.md). MRD methodology, transplant, and lower-intensity/venetoclax-based therapy are treated in their own domain files and are referenced here only where they bear directly on the intensive backbone.
