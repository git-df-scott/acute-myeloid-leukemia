# Allogeneic Transplantation, Post-Remission Strategy, and Maintenance: The Cure That Reaches a Fraction of a Fraction

> Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation is the only post-remission treatment in AML with a demonstrated survival benefit in a prospective meta-analysis — hazard ratio for death 0.90 overall, 0.73 in poor-risk and 0.83 in intermediate-risk disease, none in favourable-risk disease, across 24 trials and 6,007 patients [1] — and it remains the field's principal instrument for turning a remission into a cure. It is also a treatment that most people with AML never receive, that fails through relapse in a quarter to a half of those who do [8,9,26], that kills roughly one in ten recipients within a year through non-relapse causes even in the best modern series [21,22], and whose long-term survivors carry a mortality rate twice that of the general population fifteen years later [24]. The evidence base is strongest exactly where the disease is rarest: younger, fitter, sibling-donor patients in cooperative-group trials. Everything the field has learned since — that reduced-intensity conditioning trades toxicity for relapse [6,7], that haploidentical and mismatched donors with post-transplant cyclophosphamide now work about as well as matched ones [12,13,14], that age alone no longer excludes [15,16], that FLT3 inhibitor maintenance after transplant reduces relapse in randomized trials [29,30,32] while azacitidine and venetoclax maintenance have not [33,35] — has widened access and trimmed failure at the margins without changing the shape of the problem. This file quantifies each step of the attrition from "eligible" to "cured" and says where the evidence for each step is weak.

## Key takeaways

- **Transplant benefit is real, risk-restricted, and established by donor-versus-no-donor logic rather than randomization.** The 2009 meta-analysis of 24 prospective trials (N=6,007) found RFS HR 0.80 and OS HR 0.90 for allogeneic transplant in CR1, with benefit confined to intermediate-risk (OS HR 0.83) and poor-risk (OS HR 0.73) disease and none in good-risk disease (OS HR 1.07) [1]. In HOVON/SAKK, patients under 55 in CR1 with a sibling donor had cumulative relapse of 32% versus 59% without, at the cost of treatment-related mortality of 21% versus 4%, for DFS of 48% versus 37% [2]. In 760 HOVON patients aged 40–60, 5-year OS was 57% after allograft versus 40% after chemotherapy [3].
- **The evidence tier for the central decision is "genetic randomization by donor availability," and it is decades old.** No randomized trial has assigned patients to transplant versus no transplant, and the intermediate-risk question is now answered mainly by MRD (`09-measurable-residual-disease.md`): in AML17/AML19, transplant in CR1 improved 3-year OS from 24% to 61% in *NPM1*-MRD-positive patients and not at all in MRD-negative patients (79% vs 82%) [4].
- **Reduced-intensity conditioning buys lower toxicity with more relapse, and the trade is not favourable in fit patients with residual disease.** BMT CTN 0901 (N=272, ages 18–65) stopped early because relapse was 48.3% with RIC versus 13.5% with MAC; TRM was 4.4% versus 15.8%; RFS favoured MAC (47.3% vs 67.8%) and OS numerically did (67.7% vs 77.5%, P=.07) [6]. The MRD analysis of the same trial found that the entire RIC penalty fell on patients with detectable pre-transplant mutations (3-year relapse 67% vs 19%) [7]; in MDS, by contrast, *TP53* mutations predicted relapse equally after RIC and myeloablative conditioning in 1,514 CIBMTR patients, whereas RAS-pathway mutations predicted relapse only after RIC [10]. A German trial in patients ≤60 found no difference between RIC and standard conditioning (3-year OS 61% vs 58%), but was stopped early for slow accrual at 195 patients [5].
- **Intensifying a reduced-intensity regimen does not help.** FIGARO (N=244, median age 59) randomized fludarabine-based RIC against FLAMSA-Bu and found no difference in 2-year OS (HR 1.05) or relapse (HR 0.94), regardless of pre-transplant MRD status; acquisition of full donor T-cell chimerism at 3 months abrogated the adverse effect of MRD [8]. Treosulfan-fludarabine was non-inferior to reduced-dose busulfan-fludarabine in older/comorbid patients and superior on the primary endpoint (2-year EFS 64.0% vs 50.4%, HR 0.65) [9].
- **Donor type has stopped being the binding constraint.** Post-transplant cyclophosphamide raised 1-year GVHD-free, relapse-free survival from 34.9% to 52.7% in a randomized phase 3 (BMT CTN 1703, N=431) without changing OS, relapse or TRM [11]; in 10,025 CIBMTR patients, mismatched unrelated donor transplants with PTCy had the same OS and GRFS as matched unrelated donor transplants with PTCy (HR 0.96 and 0.90), independent of ancestry [12]. Haploidentical marrow beat double cord blood on OS (57% vs 46%) in the randomized BMT CTN 1101 [13]. In 1,295 EBMT patients in CR1 after two inductions, 10/10 unrelated and haploidentical donors matched sibling donors on relapse, LFS and OS [14].
- **Age is no longer an exclusion, but it remains a cost.** Among 1,321 CIBMTR patients ≥60 transplanted in CR1 (2007–2017), 3-year OS was 49.4%, 42.3% and 44.7% for ages 60–64, 65–69 and 70+, with residual disease and adverse cytogenetics accounting for most of the excess mortality [15]. In 701 EBMT patients aged ≥70 in CR1, 2-year OS was 48.1% and NRM 29.5% [16]. Genotype sets the ceiling: among 780 EBMT patients allografted in CR1, 2-year OS was 35.1% with a *TP53* mutation versus 64% without, and 24.6% when the mutation was accompanied by 17p loss or a complex karyotype [23]. CIBMTR growth in 2023 was concentrated in the 65–74 age group [17]; US referring physicians surveyed in 2024 reported an average maximum referral age of 73.5 years [18].
- **Most patients still do not get there.** Population-based Swedish data (1997–2006) show transplant rates falling rapidly above age 55, with 42% of AML patients under 60 transplanted, and a two-fold regional variation (two-thirds versus one-third of patients aged 40–59) that tracked 8-year survival (51% vs 30%) [19]. In HOVON/SAKK, only 45% of enrolled patients reached the eligible CR1 pool, 32% of those had a sibling donor, and 82% of those were transplanted [2].
- **Non-relapse mortality has fallen by more than half in thirty years and relapse has not.** EBMT 1-year NRM after sibling transplant fell from 29.7% (1980s) to 12.2% (2010–2016), and to 9.5% in matched-pair analysis of 2013–2016 [21]. Seattle reported a 52% reduction in NRM and a 21% reduction in relapse between 1993–97 and 2003–07 [20], and further reductions in day-200 NRM (HR 0.66) and relapse (HR 0.76) by 2013–17, concluding that "relapse of cancer remains the largest obstacle" [22].
- **Post-transplant relapse is the dominant failure and is mostly immunological.** Cumulative relapse after RIC transplant in CR was 32% in 2,815 EBMT patients, with 2-year OS from relapse of 14% [26]; DLI raised 2-year survival from 9% to 21% [25]; second transplant now yields 2-year OS of 35% (2015–2019) [28]. Relapsed blasts lose HLA class II expression (3–12-fold, in 17 of 34 patients) [39] and upregulate inhibitory ligands, without acquiring new mutations [39,40]; after haploidentical transplant, 5 of 17 relapses had lost the mismatched HLA haplotype outright [41].
- **Maintenance after transplant works for FLT3 inhibitors and not, so far, for anything else.** Sorafenib reduced relapse in two randomized trials (SORMAIN, N=83: 24-month RFS 85.0% vs 53.3%, HR 0.39 [29]; the Chinese phase 3, N=202: 1-year relapse 7.0% vs 24.5%, HR 0.25, with 5-year OS 72.0% vs 55.9% [30,31]). Gilteritinib in MORPHO (N=356) missed its primary endpoint (RFS HR 0.679, P=.0518) but benefited the 50.5% with detectable *FLT3*-ITD MRD (HR 0.515) and not those without (HR 1.213) [32]. Azacitidine maintenance versus observation (N=187) showed no RFS or OS difference [33]; VIALE-T (venetoclax + azacitidine after transplant, N=465) was terminated with a posted OS hazard ratio of 1.2 favouring supportive care [35]; midostaurin (RADIUS, N=60) was underpowered and not significant [34].
- **Maintenance without transplant has one positive phase 3.** QUAZAR AML-001 (N=472, age ≥55, CR1 after intensive chemotherapy, not transplant candidates): median OS 24.7 vs 14.8 months and RFS 10.2 vs 4.8 months with oral azacitidine versus placebo [36], independent of MRD status [37] and of *NPM1*/*FLT3* mutation status [38].

## 1. Who benefits: the donor-versus-no-donor era and what replaced it

The transplant indication in AML rests on intention-to-treat comparisons in which patients with an HLA-identical sibling were compared with those without — an approach that avoids selection bias at the price of dilution by non-compliance and by the ~18% of donor-available patients never transplanted [2]. The pooled result across 24 trials is unambiguous in direction and modest in size: RFS HR 0.80, OS HR 0.90, with the survival benefit entirely within intermediate- and poor-risk cytogenetics [1]. HOVON/SAKK's own analysis added that benefit was concentrated below age 40 [2], and the later time-dependent analysis of 760 patients aged 40–60 found allograft superior to chemotherapy (5-year OS 57% vs 40%) and equivalent to autograft on OS in intermediate-risk disease, though with less relapse [3].

These data predate ELN genetic risk and molecular MRD. The contemporary decision in intermediate-risk and *NPM1*-mutated disease is increasingly made on post-induction MRD: transplant benefit in AML17/AML19 was confined to *NPM1*-MRD-positive patients (HR 0.39) and absent in MRD-negative patients, including those with *FLT3*-ITD [4]; ALFA-0702 found the same interaction (`09-measurable-residual-disease.md`, section 4). There is no randomized trial of transplant versus no transplant in MRD-defined subgroups and, as that file argues, none is likely. The evidence tier for the field's most consequential post-remission decision is therefore: **prospective cohort with genetic randomization (1980s–2000s) plus MRD-stratified observational interaction analyses (2017–2024)**.

## 2. Conditioning intensity

BMT CTN 0901 is the decisive trial. Patients aged 18–65 with HCT-CI ≤4 and <5% marrow blasts were randomized to MAC (n=135) or RIC (n=137); accrual stopped at 272 of a planned 356 because relapse was 48.3% with RIC versus 13.5% with MAC. TRM was lower with RIC (4.4% vs 15.8%), RFS was higher with MAC (67.8% vs 47.3%), and the OS difference (77.5% vs 67.7% at 18 months) did not reach significance [6]. The genomic MRD substudy explains the result: patients with no detectable mutation before transplant had identical survival on either arm (3-year OS 56% vs 63%), whereas in NGS-positive patients RIC carried a relapse HR of 6.38 and OS HR of 1.97 [7]. The earlier German trial in patients ≤60 (RIC with 8 Gy TBI/fludarabine versus 12 Gy TBI/cyclophosphamide) found no difference in NRM, relapse or OS but stopped at 195 of the planned patients [5].

Attempts to close the RIC relapse gap by intensifying RIC have failed: FIGARO's FLAMSA-Bu arm produced no improvement in OS or relapse at any MRD level [8]. What did track outcome in FIGARO was immunological — full donor T-cell chimerism at 3 months abrogated the adverse effect of pre-transplant MRD — which the authors take as pointing toward interventions that accelerate donor immune reconstitution rather than more chemotherapy [8]. For older or comorbid patients, treosulfan-fludarabine was superior to reduced-dose busulfan-fludarabine on 2-year EFS (64.0% vs 50.4%) in a 476-patient phase 3 [9]. In the CIBMTR cohort of patients ≥60, conditioning intensity did not predict OS (3-year 46% RIC vs 49% MAC in the 60–69 group), and MAC was rarely used over 70 [15].

**Evidence tier:** conditioning intensity — phase 3 randomized (MAC superior for relapse in fit patients; RIC-intensification negative; treosulfan non-inferior/superior in older patients). Conditioning by MRD status — post hoc randomized-trial analysis only [7,8].

## 3. Donor, graft, and GVHD prophylaxis

The decade's largest practical change is post-transplant cyclophosphamide. BMT CTN 1703 randomized 431 adults undergoing RIC transplant from matched or 7/8-mismatched donors to PTCy/tacrolimus/MMF versus tacrolimus/methotrexate: 1-year GRFS 52.7% vs 34.9% (HR 0.64), with less severe acute and chronic GVHD and more immunosuppression-free survival, and no difference in OS, DFS, relapse or TRM [11]. Registry analysis of 10,025 CIBMTR patients (2017–2021) found that with PTCy, single-locus mismatched unrelated donors gave the same OS (HR 0.96) and GRFS (HR 0.90) as matched unrelated donors, and that adding MMUDs increased donor availability for every ancestry group [12]. BMT CTN 1101 randomized 368 patients to double cord blood or haploidentical marrow after RIC: 2-year PFS did not differ (35% vs 41%) but NRM (18% vs 11%) and OS (46% vs 57%) favoured haploidentical donors [13]. In 1,295 EBMT patients in CR1 after two inductions, 10/10 unrelated and haploidentical donors were comparable to sibling donors on relapse, LFS and OS, whereas 9/10 unrelated donors without PTCy had more chronic GVHD and worse GRFS [14]. In patients transplanted with active disease, 9/10 MMUD with PTCy gave at least comparable outcomes to haploidentical transplant (2-year LFS 42% vs 26%) [42]. The CIBMTR 2025 report records MMUD utilization doubling since 2020 on the back of PTCy, with matched unrelated donors at 45%, haploidentical 21%, matched related 18%, MMUD 12% and cord 3% of allografts [17].

The consequence is that "no donor" has largely ceased to be a reason for not transplanting in high-income systems. What remains are fitness, referral, disease control, and resources.

## 4. Age, fitness, referral, and how many patients actually reach transplant

Three kinds of evidence describe attrition. Population registries: the Swedish Acute Leukemia Registry (98% coverage, 3,899 patients, 1997–2006) found transplant rates and survival "decreased rapidly with age >55 years"; 42% of AML patients under 60 were transplanted, two-thirds in CR1; and the region that transplanted two-thirds of patients aged 40–59 had 8-year OS of 51% versus 30% where one-third were transplanted [19]. Cooperative-group trials: HOVON/SAKK's 2,287 enrolled patients yielded 1,032 (45%) eligible in CR1 under 55, of whom 32% had a sibling donor and 82% of those were transplanted — 268 patients, or 12% of enrolment, by sibling allograft in that era [2]; GIMEMA AML1310 delivered allograft to 71% of protocol-designated candidates using all donor sources (`09-measurable-residual-disease.md`) [43]. Referral surveys: US hematology-oncology physicians surveyed in 2015, 2019 and 2024 reported rising referral, earlier referral in CR1, falling concern about donor availability and age, and a 2024 average maximum referral age of 73.5 years [18].

The transplant literature has answered the age question on its own terms. Among 1,321 CIBMTR patients ≥60 in CR1, 3-year OS was 49.4% (60–64), 42.3% (65–69) and 44.7% (70+), and after adjustment age had "a small effect on OS and TRM"; pre-transplant MRD and adverse cytogenetics explained most of the adverse impact [15]. Among 701 EBMT patients ≥70 in CR1, 2-year OS was 48.1%, LFS 45.3%, relapse 25.2%, NRM 29.5% and GRFS 33.4%; haploidentical and 10/10 unrelated donors had lower relapse than siblings, and 9/10 unrelated donors the highest NRM [16]. These are outcomes in selected patients who reached transplant; the denominator of patients ≥70 with AML in CR1 who did not is not reported in either series, and no population-based estimate of transplant utilization in patients ≥65 was located (see evidence-trail notes). The SEER-Medicare finding that 31% of patients ≥65 received no treatment at all (`01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md`) bounds the problem from below.

## 5. Non-relapse mortality, GVHD, and late effects

NRM is the transplant literature's clearest success story. Seattle's comparison of 1993–97 with 2003–07 found a 60% reduction in day-200 NRM and 52% overall, with fewer severe GVHD, infections and organ injuries [20]; the subsequent decade (2003–07 vs 2013–17) brought further reductions in day-200 NRM (HR 0.66), relapse (HR 0.76) and overall mortality (HR 0.66), similar across conditioning intensity and donor type [22]. EBMT's 38,800-patient sibling-donor analysis found 1-year NRM of 29.7% in the 1980s, 24.4% in the 1990s, 14.8% in 2000–09 and 12.2% in 2010–16, and 9.5% in a 2013–16 matched-pair cohort [21]. Even so, 1-year NRM of roughly 10% in the best contemporary sibling-donor series, and 29.5% in patients ≥70 [16], is a mortality cost no other AML therapy carries at that point in the disease course.

Survival past the early period is not a return to normal. In the Bone Marrow Transplant Survivor Study (1,479 two-year survivors, median follow-up 9.5 years), conditional 15-year survival was 80.2%, relative mortality was 9.9-fold overall and remained 2.2-fold at 15 years; relapse (29%) and chronic GVHD (22%) led the causes of late death; survivors were 13.9 times more likely than siblings to report difficulty holding a job [24]. Chronic GVHD is simultaneously the transplant's principal long-term morbidity and, through the graft-versus-leukemia effect, part of its mechanism — the reason PTCy's reduction in GVHD without a change in relapse or OS [11] is a real but bounded advance.

## 6. Relapse after transplant: mechanisms and treatment

Relapse after allograft is the dominant mode of failure and its treatment record is poor. In 2,815 EBMT RIC transplants for AML in CR (1999–2008), cumulative relapse was 32%; among 263 relapsing patients, CR was re-induced in 32%, 2-year OS from relapse was 14%, and long-term survival "was achieved almost exclusively after successful induction of CR by cytoreductive therapy, followed either by donor lymphocyte infusion or second HSCT" [26]. In 399 patients relapsing after transplant, DLI raised 2-year survival from 9% to 21%, and to 56% when given in remission or with favourable karyotype versus 15% in aplasia or active disease [25]. Prophylactic DLI in CR improved OS only in high-risk AML in an 89-pair matched analysis (69.8% vs 40.2%) [27]. Second allograft has improved with time: across 1,540 EBMT recipients, 2-year OS rose from 22.5% (2000–04) to 35% (2015–19) and relapse fell from 64% to 50.7%, with NRM unchanged; myeloablative conditioning for the second transplant reduced relapse without raising NRM [28].

The mechanism is now well described and mostly non-genetic. Exome sequencing of 15 post-transplant relapses found no relapse-specific mutations in immune genes, but RNA sequencing showed 3–12-fold downregulation of MHC class II genes, confirmed by flow or immunohistochemistry in 17 of 34 patients and reversible by interferon-γ in vitro [39]. An independent analysis identified a post-transplant relapse transcriptional signature enriched for antigen presentation and T-cell costimulation, with loss of HLA-DR/DQ/DP via CIITA downregulation and upregulation of PD-L1, B7-H3, CD80 and PVRL2, each counteractable by interferon-γ or checkpoint blockade in vitro [40]. After haploidentical transplant with donor T-cell infusion, 5 of 17 relapses carried acquired uniparental disomy of 6p that deleted the mismatched HLA haplotype, rendering the leukemia invisible to donor T cells [41]. Relapse kinetics by NGS MRD after transplant, and the monthly monitoring they imply, are in `09-measurable-residual-disease.md`.

The therapeutic implication — that post-transplant relapse is an immune-evasion problem that chemotherapy cannot fix and that interferon-γ, checkpoint blockade, or second transplant from a different donor might — is mechanistically well founded and clinically unproven (`07-immunotherapy-antibodies-and-cellular.md`).

## 7. Maintenance after transplant

The randomized record is short and its positive results are all FLT3-directed. **Sorafenib**: SORMAIN (double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2, N=83) gave HR 0.39 for relapse or death and 24-month RFS of 85.0% versus 53.3%, with the greatest benefit in patients MRD-negative before and MRD-positive after transplant [29]; the Chinese open-label phase 3 (N=202, ages 18–60) gave 1-year relapse of 7.0% versus 24.5% (HR 0.25) [30] and at 5 years OS of 72.0% versus 55.9% (HR 0.55), LFS 70.0% versus 49.0%, relapse 15.0% versus 36.3%, with no excess NRM, GVHD or late effects [31]. **Gilteritinib**: MORPHO (BMT CTN 1506, double-blind phase 3, N=356) missed its RFS primary endpoint (HR 0.679, P=.0518) but in a prespecified analysis benefited the 50.5% of participants with detectable peri-transplant *FLT3*-ITD MRD (HR 0.515) and not those without (HR 1.213) [32]; the MRD analysis found relapse in 42.2% of MRD-positive placebo recipients versus 13.4% of MRD-negative, and multiclonal *FLT3*-ITD in 14.8% with worse survival on either arm [44]. **Midostaurin**: RADIUS (open-label, N=60) estimated 18-month RFS of 89% versus 76% (HR 0.46, P=.27) — underpowered [34].

**Hypomethylating agents**: the MD Anderson phase 3 of azacitidine versus observation (N=187) found median RFS 2.07 versus 1.28 years (P=.43) and OS 2.52 versus 2.56 years (P=.85), with 23 of 87 treated patients withdrawing for toxicity, preference or logistics [33]. Oral azacitidine after transplant reached phase 1/2 (N=30; 21% relapse) [45] and no randomized result was located. **Venetoclax**: VIALE-T (phase 3, N=465, venetoclax + azacitidine versus best supportive care after transplant) was terminated for "strategic considerations" with a posted Part 2 OS hazard ratio of 1.2 (95% CI 0.81–1.77) [35]; a phase 1 of venetoclax/azacitidine maintenance after venetoclax-containing RIC (N=22 maintained) reported 2-year OS 67% and relapse 41% and its authors state that "a randomized study is required" [46]. **Eprenetapopt + azacitidine** after transplant in *TP53*-mutant disease (phase 2, N=33) gave 1-year RFS 59.9% and OS 78.8% with no comparator [47]. A 2021 meta-analysis of five randomized trials (N=736; sorafenib ×2, midostaurin, decitabine, azacitidine) reported pooled OS HR 0.61 and RFS HR 0.51 [48] — an estimate dominated by the FLT3 trials and predating the negative azacitidine phase 3 and VIALE-T.

**Evidence tier:** sorafenib — randomized phase 2 and phase 3 positive (not approved for this indication); gilteritinib — phase 3 negative on primary endpoint, positive in prespecified MRD subgroup; azacitidine — phase 3 negative; venetoclax/azacitidine — phase 3 terminated, point estimate unfavourable; everything else — single-arm.

## 8. Maintenance without transplant

QUAZAR AML-001 is the only positive phase 3: 472 patients ≥55 in CR/CRi after intensive chemotherapy, ineligible for transplant, randomized to oral azacitidine 300 mg × 14 days or placebo; median OS 24.7 versus 14.8 months and RFS 10.2 versus 4.8 months, with preserved quality of life [36]. The benefit was independent of baseline MRD, and the drug converted 37% versus 19% of MRD-positive patients to negative [37]; among *NPM1*-mutated patients OS HR was 0.63 and among *FLT3*-mutated 0.63 (CI crossing 1), with survival improved regardless of post-induction MRD [38]. The venetoclax/azacitidine analogue, VIALE-M, had its randomized parts removed and reports only dose-finding data (`04-venetoclax-bcl2-lower-intensity.md`). Maintenance after intensive chemotherapy in transplant-eligible patients, and after venetoclax-based induction in the majority population, has no phase 3 evidence.

## What is established vs contested

**Established.**
- Allograft in CR1 improves RFS and OS in intermediate- and poor-risk but not favourable-risk AML (prospective donor-versus-no-donor meta-analysis) [1,2,3].
- Myeloablative conditioning reduces relapse relative to RIC in fit patients with residual disease, at the cost of TRM [6,7]; intensified RIC does not [8].
- PTCy improves GRFS without changing survival [11] and makes mismatched and haploidentical donors equivalent to matched ones on survival [12,13,14].
- NRM has fallen by more than half since the 1980s [20,21,22]; relapse has fallen far less [22,26].
- Post-transplant relapse is driven by loss or downregulation of HLA and upregulation of inhibitory ligands, not by new mutations [39,40,41].
- FLT3 inhibitor maintenance after transplant reduces relapse [29,30,31,32]; oral azacitidine maintenance after chemotherapy improves OS in non-transplant candidates [36].

**Contested or unresolved.**
- Whether MRD-negative intermediate-risk patients should be transplanted in CR1 at all [4].
- Whether transplant benefits patients ≥70 relative to non-transplant strategies (no comparative data; [15,16] report transplanted patients only).
- Whether any non-FLT3 maintenance after transplant helps: azacitidine negative [33], venetoclax/azacitidine terminated with an unfavourable point estimate [35], others single-arm [45,46,47].
- Whether gilteritinib maintenance should be given to all *FLT3*-ITD patients or only MRD-positive ones [32,44].
- The size and causes of the gap between transplant-eligible and transplanted patients in the venetoclax era; no contemporary population-level utilization study was located.

## Barriers to cure in this domain

The attrition from "has AML" to "cured by transplant," with the best available figure at each step and the quality of the evidence behind it:

1. **Most patients are not candidates, and the number is not measured.** Median age at diagnosis is about 70 (`01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md`); transplant rates "decreased rapidly with age >55" in a complete national registry [19]; 31% of US patients ≥65 receive no anti-leukemic therapy at all (`01`). The proportion of patients ≥65 in CR1 who are transplanted is not reported in any population-based series located here. **Evidence: registry (2011) plus absence.**
2. **Remission is a precondition and half of older patients do not reach it durably.** HOVON's eligible pool was 45% of enrolment in patients under 55 [2]. In the venetoclax era, composite remission is 66% and MRD-negative remission 41% of that (`04`, `09`), and relapse before transplant is common. **Evidence: trial and cohort data; no era-specific transplant-attrition analysis located.**
3. **Referral is discretionary and varies two-fold by geography with a survival cost.** Swedish regions transplanting two-thirds versus one-third of patients aged 40–59 had 8-year OS of 51% versus 30% [19]; US referral behaviour is improving but self-reported [18]. **Evidence: registry (one country, one era) and survey.**
4. **Donor availability has been solved technically but not organizationally.** PTCy makes MMUD and haploidentical donors equivalent [12,13,14], yet MMUD use is 12% of allografts [17], and ancestry-related disparities in reaching transplant persist (`14-access-equity-and-cost.md`). **Evidence: randomized and registry.**
5. **Of those transplanted, a quarter to a half relapse, and the fraction is set by residual disease and conditioning.** Relapse was 13.5% with MAC and 48.3% with RIC in fit patients [6], 32% after RIC in registry data [26], 25.2% in patients ≥70 [16], and about two-thirds in MRD-positive remission by any measure (`09`) [7]. The intervention that reduces it — myeloablation — is unavailable to the older majority [15], and the intensified-RIC alternative failed [8]. **Evidence: phase 3 and registry.**
6. **Relapse after transplant is an immune-escape event with no approved counter-measure.** HLA class II loss and checkpoint ligand upregulation [39,40] and HLA haplotype loss [41] are documented mechanisms; interferon-γ and checkpoint blockade reverse them in vitro; no randomized trial of a mechanism-directed post-transplant relapse therapy was located. Two-year survival after post-transplant relapse remains 14% overall, 21% with DLI, 35% with second transplant [25,26,28]. **Evidence: mechanistic (strong) and registry outcomes (strong); intervention (absent).**
7. **Non-relapse mortality of roughly 10% at one year, and 30% in patients ≥70, is a fixed cost that competes with the benefit in exactly the population where the benefit is smallest.** [16,21,22] **Evidence: registry, consistent across continents.**
8. **Maintenance can only be given after the transplant that most patients do not receive, and outside FLT3 it does not work.** The positive maintenance trials are FLT3-directed [29,30,32]; azacitidine [33] and venetoclax/azacitidine [35] are negative or unfavourable; and MORPHO shows that maintenance benefit may be confined to MRD-positive patients [32,44], which returns the problem to barrier 5. **Evidence: phase 3.**
9. **The cure that transplant delivers is a conditional one.** Fifteen-year survivors have twice the population mortality, with relapse and chronic GVHD as leading late causes, and substantial functional and economic sequelae [24]. Cure rates counted at 5 years overstate restored life expectancy.
10. **The evidence base for the central decision is old and unrepeatable.** The donor-versus-no-donor trials cannot be rerun; the transplant-versus-no-transplant randomized trial in MRD-defined subgroups will not be run (`09`); and every improvement since has been a within-transplant optimization (conditioning, donor, GVHD prophylaxis, maintenance) rather than a test of transplant's place in the modern treatment sequence. The comparison that now matters — transplant after venetoclax-based induction in older patients versus continued venetoclax-based therapy — has no randomized evidence, though PARADIGM's higher transplant rate after venetoclax/azacitidine (61% vs 40%) shows the question is live (`03-intensive-chemotherapy-backbone.md`).

## Open research questions

1. What fraction of patients aged 60–75 in CR1 after venetoclax-based induction are transplanted, in which health systems, and what determines it? A contemporary population-based utilization study is the single most useful missing dataset in this domain.
2. Can donor immune reconstitution be accelerated pharmacologically or by graft engineering, given FIGARO's finding that full donor T-cell chimerism at 3 months abrogates the effect of pre-transplant MRD [8]?
3. Does interferon-γ, checkpoint blockade, or HLA-mismatch-aware donor selection for second transplant reduce post-transplant relapse in patients with documented HLA class II loss or HLA haplotype loss [39,40,41]? A randomized trial has not been run.
4. Is gilteritinib maintenance justified in MRD-negative *FLT3*-ITD patients, and should MRD-directed maintenance be the model for other targeted classes [32,44]?
5. Why did azacitidine and venetoclax/azacitidine maintenance fail after transplant when oral azacitidine succeeded after chemotherapy [33,35,36]? Immune interference, drug exposure, and patient selection are all untested explanations.
6. What is the comparative value of transplant versus continued venetoclax-based therapy in older patients who reach MRD-negative remission — a question the field has now made answerable and has not yet asked in a randomized design.
7. What do transplant outcomes look like at population level in patients ≥70 when the untransplanted comparators are included [15,16]?

### Evidence-trail notes

- All references were retrieved on 2026-09-09 via PubMed E-utilities with abstracts read, except [35], which is the ClinicalTrials.gov registry record (results section) as also cited in `04-venetoclax-bcl2-lower-intensity.md`.
- Reference [10] (Lindsley 2017) is cited for pre-transplant genetics in MDS, where *TP53* predicted relapse regardless of conditioning intensity; the AML-specific transplant data for *TP53* ([23], 2-year OS 35.1% overall and 24.6% with 17p loss or complex karyotype) are developed in `10-tp53-adverse-risk-and-secondary-aml.md`.
- **Transplant utilization in patients ≥65.** No contemporary population-based analysis of the proportion of older patients in CR1 who undergo transplant was located; the Swedish registry figures [19] are from 1997–2006 and confined to patients <60 for the rate calculation. This is a documented gap, and barrier 1 depends on it.
- **Historical maintenance trials after chemotherapy** (pre-QUAZAR) were not retrieved and are not characterized here; the statement that QUAZAR is the sole positive phase 3 in that setting is limited to the trials retrieved.
- **Regulatory status** of sorafenib and gilteritinib as post-transplant maintenance was not retrieved from FDA or EMA sources and is not asserted; only the trial evidence tier is given.
- Chronic GVHD incidence, graft source (peripheral blood vs marrow), and ATG were not covered beyond their appearance in the cited trials.
