# Immunotherapy in AML: Antibodies, Engagers, and Cells, and Why the CD19 Result Has Not Happened Here

> Allogeneic transplant proves that a T-cell response can cure AML (`08-transplant-post-remission-and-maintenance.md`). Nearly two decades of trying to reproduce that effect pharmacologically has produced no approved T-cell-directed therapy for the disease, and this file is mostly a record of why. The proximate reason is a target problem that has been formally searched and not solved: an integrated proteomic and transcriptomic screen of the AML surfaceome concluded that no single antigen matches CD19's profile, and proposed combinatorial targeting instead [1]. Every antigen actually taken into patients is shared with normal myeloid cells, hematopoietic stem cells, or both, so the therapeutic window is set by marrow toxicity rather than by antileukemic potency. The consequences are visible in the trial record: a CD33 antibody-drug conjugate that raised composite remission to 70% with hypomethylating agents was stopped in phase 3 for excess deaths from infection [5,6,7]; the best-performing CD123 T-cell engager produced complete remission in about a quarter of chemotherapy-refractory patients and did not advance [9]; four CAR constructs across three antigens have reported overall response rates from 0% to 74% with almost no durability outside a transplant bridge [16,17,18,19,20,21]; checkpoint blockade works best in patients whose marrow was already immune-infiltrated before treatment [27,32]; and one randomized frontline checkpoint arm was closed after a toxicity signal [29]. Two things in the field are genuinely new: gene-edited grafts that delete the target from normal cells so it becomes leukemia-specific, now with 30 patients transplanted [23,24], and the recognition that AML's immune escape is largely transcriptional rather than mutational [33,34,35], which means it may be reversible.

## Key takeaways

- **The target problem is structural, not a search failure.** Integrating large proteomic and transcriptomic datasets across malignant and normal tissue, Perna and colleagues found no AML surface antigen with a CD19-like profile: expressed on leukemia stem cells and absent from normal CD34+CD38− cells, T cells, and vital tissues. They concluded that combinatorial targeting is required and identified candidate pairs [1]. An independent flow-cytometry profile of 356 primary AML and 34 normal marrows found CD33, CD123, CLL-1, TIM-3, and CD244 ubiquitously expressed on blasts irrespective of genetics, each with additional expression somewhere in normal hematopoiesis, and nominated CD33/TIM-3 and CLL-1/TIM-3 as the least-overlapping dual combinations [2]. In pediatric AML the answer differs again: CD33 and CLEC12A were the most upregulated, with CLEC12A absent from normal hematopoietic stem cells while CD33 and FLT3 are present on them [3].
- **The most active CD33 antibody-drug conjugate was stopped in phase 3 for deaths.** Vadastuximab talirine monotherapy gave CR+CRi of 28% at the recommended dose in relapsed AML, with 30-day mortality of 8% and prolonged count recovery [5]. Added to hypomethylating agents in 53 untreated older patients it produced composite remission of 70%, half of it MRD-negative, with median overall survival of 11.3 months and "increased hematologic toxicity" [6]. The randomized double-blind phase 3 (N=240) was terminated in June 2017 "due to safety; specifically a higher rate of deaths, including fatal infections, in the SGN33A arm versus the control arm" [7], and the sponsor suspended enrollment in every trial of the drug [8]. The company stated the phase 3 signal did not appear related to the hepatotoxicity that had earlier prompted an FDA hold on peri-transplant studies [8].
- **CD123 antibody-drug conjugation reached approval, but not in AML.** Pivekimab sunirine in relapsed/refractory AML (N=91) gave an overall response of 21% and composite complete remission of 17% at the recommended phase 2 dose, with veno-occlusive disease among the dose-limiting toxicities [10]. Its first approval, on 27 May 2026, is for blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm [11]. **Approved for BPDCN; phase 1–2 signal in AML.**
- **T-cell engagers reach roughly a quarter of refractory patients and stall there.** Flotetuzumab, a CD123×CD3 DART molecule, gave CR/CRh of 26.7% and overall response of 30.0% in 30 patients with primary induction failure or early relapse treated at the recommended dose, with median overall survival of 10.2 months among responders [9]. AMG 330 (CD33×CD3): 8 of 60 evaluable patients reached complete remission or a morphologic leukemia-free state, with cytokine release syndrome in 78% [12]. Vibecotamab (CD123×CD3) phase 1: overall response 9.0% across 111 evaluable patients, with responses only above a threshold dose and confined to patients with lower blast burden [13]; its phase 2 in AML with detectable MRD cleared MRD in 4 of 21 patients (19%) [14]. JNJ-63709178 produced "minimal clinical activity" and no recommended phase 2 dose could be identified [15].
- **CAR T cells in AML: high response in one construct, near-zero in another, durability in neither.** CLL-1 CAR T cells in 38 Chinese adults gave an overall response of 73.7% and MRD-negative complete response of 42.1%, with median progression-free survival of 9 months, median overall survival of 12.2 months, grade 3/4 cytokine release syndrome in 44.7%, and cytopenia in every patient with median neutrophil recovery at 45 days [18]. The industry-sponsored CLL-1 CAR (KITE-222) produced no clinically meaningful responses in 12 patients, and where blasts were depleted, CLL-1-negative blasts persisted [20]. Donor-derived CD33 CAR T cells after transplant (N=15) gave an overall response of 20% [16]. CD123 CAR T cells (N=19 AML) gave complete remission in 4 of 15 evaluable patients with limited persistence [17]. NKG2D-ligand CAR T cells gave objective responses in 3 of 12 evaluable patients "without durability outside of patients bridged to allogeneic HSCT" [21]; a second-generation construct with MICA/MICB knocked down raised the response rate from 0% to 17% [22].
- **Deleting the antigen from the graft is the one strategy that changes the arithmetic.** CD33-deficient human and non-human-primate hematopoietic cells engraft and differentiate normally and are impervious to CD33-directed CAR T cells, converting a shared antigen into a leukemia-specific one [23]. In a phase 1/2a trial, all 30 patients receiving a CRISPR-Cas9 CD33-deleted allograft engrafted by day 28 with a median of 10 days; 19 went on to gemtuzumab ozogamicin maintenance at up to 2 mg/m² without prolonged high-grade cytopenias; three transplant-related deaths occurred and the trial was stopped early [24]. Multiplex base editing extends the same principle and protected engrafted human cells from gemtuzumab in vivo [25].
- **Checkpoint blockade has activity where the marrow is already inflamed, and a randomized frontline arm was closed for toxicity.** Azacitidine plus nivolumab in 70 patients with relapsed/refractory AML gave an overall response of 33%, complete remission or CRi in 22%, and grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events in 11%; response was 58% in hypomethylating-agent-naive patients versus 22% in pretreated patients, and pre-treatment marrow CD3 and CD8 predicted response [27]. Pembrolizumab after high-dose cytarabine in relapsed/refractory AML gave composite complete remission of 38% and median overall survival of 11.1 months, with responders showing more progenitor-exhausted CD8 T cells before treatment [28]. Ipilimumab for relapse after allogeneic transplant produced complete responses in 5 of 22 patients at 10 mg/kg, concentrated in extramedullary disease, with graft-versus-host disease precluding further dosing in 14% and one treatment-related death [30].
- **Vaccines have never been tested against a concurrent control in AML that reported a survival result.** A WT1 mRNA-electroporated dendritic-cell vaccine given as post-remission therapy to 30 patients at very high risk produced an antileukemic response in 13, with 5-year overall survival of 53.8% in responders versus 25.0% in non-responders [36]. Galinpepimut-S, a multivalent WT1 peptide vaccine, gave median disease-free survival of 16.9 months in a 22-patient single-arm phase 2 [37]. Its phase 3 in second remission remains blinded, with 72 of the 80 events required for final analysis reached as of 26 December 2025 [38]. **Phase 3 result pending; no efficacy data released.**
- **NK-cell approaches are safe and, so far, transient.** A systematic review of 29 trials of non-genetically-modified NK-cell transfer found a pooled response of 35% in relapsed/refractory AML with 20.2% proceeding to transplant, 1-year disease-free survival of 26% in high-risk remission patients ineligible for transplant, graft-versus-host disease in 0–8%, and consistently short NK persistence [39]. CD33 CAR-NK cells in 10 heavily pretreated patients produced MRD-negative complete remission in 6 by day 28 without neurotoxicity or graft-versus-host disease [40]. An IL-15 superagonist after transplant showed a threefold reduction in 2-year relapse among patients receiving more than four doses, at P=.06 in 20 patients [41].
- **AML's immune escape is largely transcriptional, which is the field's main reason for optimism.** Relapse after transplant involves no new mutations in immune genes but 3- to 12-fold downregulation of MHC class II, reversible by interferon-γ in vitro (`08-transplant-post-remission-and-maintenance.md`) [33,34]. Interferon-γ-related expression profiles predict both chemotherapy resistance and response to flotetuzumab across 442 primary samples [32], and *TP53*-abnormal marrows, which are chemoresistant, are immune-infiltrated and enriched among flotetuzumab responders, 7 of 15 of whom achieved complete responses [26].

## 1. The target problem

Every clinical AML antigen is a lineage antigen. CD33 is expressed on the majority of AML blasts and on normal myeloid progenitors, and in a substantial fraction of people on hematopoietic stem cells [2,3]. CD123 is expressed on blasts and leukemia stem cells with lower but real expression on normal progenitors [11]. CLL-1/CLEC12A is the most favourable of the three because it is absent from normal hematopoietic stem cells [3,20], which is why it has attracted the most CAR development. TIM-3 is attractive in combination because its normal-tissue overlap is complementary to CD33 and CLL-1 rather than coincident [2]. CD70 is close to leukemia-restricted, with little or no expression in normal marrow, and CD70-directed CAR T cells did not impair normal colony formation [42].

Two systematic attempts to find better have both concluded that no single antigen suffices. The proteomic-transcriptomic integration explicitly reports that its investigations "did not uncover candidate targets with a profile as favorable as CD19" and pivots to combinatorial design [1]. The flow-cytometric coexpression profile of 356 AML samples reaches the same conclusion from the other direction, finding each of six candidate targets expressed somewhere in normal hematopoiesis and recommending dual targeting [2]. The pediatric profile shows the target set is age-specific: known adult targets including *IL3RA* (CD123) were not overexpressed relative to healthy precursors in 36 pediatric samples, while CD33 and CLEC12A were, and *KMT2A*-mutated infant AML overexpressed FLT3 [3].

Antigen loss is not hypothetical. In the CLL-1 CAR trial where blasts were depleted, CLL-1-negative blasts persisted and total blast counts did not fall [20]. This is the AML analogue of CD19-negative escape, arriving earlier in development because the starting antigen was less uniformly expressed.

## 2. Antibody-drug conjugates beyond gemtuzumab

Gemtuzumab ozogamicin itself, its withdrawal, and its 2017 return on fractionated dosing are covered in `03-intensive-chemotherapy-backbone.md`; the cytogenetically restricted benefit and the *CD33* splicing polymorphism belong there.

**Vadastuximab talirine (SGN-CD33A)** is the most instructive failure in this domain because its efficacy signal was strong. Monotherapy in 131 patients (median age 73) gave CR+CRi of 28% at the recommended 40 µg/kg with MRD negativity in half of responders, but count recovery took a median of 6.4 weeks for neutrophils and 10.6 weeks for platelets [5]. Combined with azacitidine or decitabine in 53 untreated older patients, composite remission was 70%, MRD-negative in 51%, with 30- and 60-day mortality of 2% and 8%, median relapse-free survival 7.7 months and overall survival 11.3 months [6]. Against that background the randomized double-blind phase 3 in the same population (N=240) was terminated for "a higher rate of deaths, including fatal infections, in the SGN33A arm versus the control arm" [7], with enrollment suspended across the entire program [8]. The pyrrolobenzodiazepine payload's marrow toxicity, layered onto the myelosuppression of a hypomethylating agent in older patients, is the most plausible reading. **Evidence tier: phase 3 terminated for harm.**

**Pivekimab sunirine (IMGN632)** carries an indolinobenzodiazepine payload on a CD123 antibody. In 91 patients across dose escalation and expansion, the recommended phase 2 dose was 0.045 mg/kg every three weeks; at that dose the overall response was 21% and composite complete remission 17%, with febrile neutropenia and infusion reactions the main grade 3 events and reversible veno-occlusive disease among the dose-limiting toxicities [10]. Development moved to combination with azacitidine and venetoclax; the drug's first regulatory approval, in May 2026, is for BPDCN rather than AML [11].

## 3. T-cell engagers

The class has produced consistent, modest, and non-durable activity, with cytokine release syndrome as a manageable but universal companion.

**Flotetuzumab** is the best-characterized. In 88 patients, with a recommended dose of 500 ng/kg/day and step-up dosing plus dexamethasone and tocilizumab to control infusion reactions, 30 patients with primary induction failure or early relapse treated at that dose achieved CR/CRh in 26.7% and overall response in 30.0%; responders had median overall survival of 10.2 months with 6- and 12-month survival of 75% and 50% [9]. A 10-gene bone-marrow signature predicted complete response better than the ELN classifier [9]. In the subset with *TP53* abnormalities, 7 of 15 (47%) achieved complete responses, and responders had higher baseline tumour-inflammation, *FOXP3*, CD8 and PD-1 scores [26]. This is the strongest immune signal in a genotype where nothing else works (`10-tp53-adverse-risk-and-secondary-aml.md`), and it comes from 15 patients.

**AMG 330** (CD33×CD3, continuous infusion) treated 77 patients without reaching a maximum tolerated dose; cytokine release syndrome occurred in 78% with 10% grade 3/4, and 8 of 60 evaluable patients achieved complete remission or a morphologic leukemia-free state, with a further 37% of non-responders having marrow blasts halved [12]. **Vibecotamab** (XmAb14045) in 120 patients gave an overall response of 9.0%, rising to 11.5% above a target dose of 0.75 µg/kg, with responses associated with lower baseline blast burden [13] — a finding the sponsor pursued into a phase 2 in AML with MRD only, where 4 of 21 patients (19%) cleared MRD and median overall survival was 13.1 months, alongside an overall response of 67% in the separate MDS/CMML cohort [14]. **JNJ-63709178** was evaluated intravenously and subcutaneously in 62 patients; step-up dosing did not mitigate cytokine production, exposure was suboptimal, activity was minimal, and no recommended phase 2 dose was identified [15].

Two mechanistic findings frame the class. *TP53*-deficient AML cells suppress T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion under AMG 330 through a secretome containing TGF-β1, driving co-cultured T cells toward a senescent profile [43]. Conversely, STING agonists amplify AMG 330-mediated killing through an interferon-γ feedback loop in primary cells and xenografts [44]. Both are preclinical.

## 4. CAR T and CAR NK cells

Response rates across constructs span the full range, and durability is uniformly poor without a transplant.

| Construct | Target | N treated | Response | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VCAR33 [16] | CD33 | 15 | ORR 20% | Donor-derived, post-transplant; ended for non-safety reasons |
| CD123 CAR [17] | CD123 | 21 (19 AML) | CR 4/15 evaluable | Limited persistence; 89% prior transplant |
| CLL1 CAR [18] | CLL-1 | 38 | ORR 73.7%, MRD-CR 42.1% | Median PFS 9 months; all patients cytopenic |
| Anti-CLL1 4SCAR [19] | CLL-1 | 4 (pediatric) | 3 CR | Caspase-9 safety switch |
| KITE-222 [20] | CLL-1 | 12 | No meaningful response | CLL-1-negative blasts persisted |
| CYAD-01 [21] | NKG2D ligands | 16 | 3/12 evaluable | No durability outside transplant bridge |
| CYAD-02 [22] | NKG2D ligands | 12 | ORR 17% | MICA/B knockdown improved persistence |
| CD33 CAR-NK [40] | CD33 | 10 | 6/10 MRD-negative CR | No ICANS or GVHD |

The gap between the 73.7% response in one CLL-1 CAR trial [18] and the absence of response in another [20] is the domain's sharpest unresolved question. Construct design, lymphodepletion, manufacturing, and patient selection all differ, and no head-to-head comparison exists. Prolonged cytopenia is the shared toxicity: every patient in the 38-patient CLL-1 series had it, with median neutrophil recovery at 45 days [18], which is the on-target myeloablation the target problem predicts.

**Gene-edited grafts** address that directly. Deleting CD33 from donor hematopoietic cells leaves engraftment and myeloid function intact in mice and rhesus macaques while rendering the graft resistant to CD33-directed CAR T cells, so CD33 becomes leukemia-specific after transplant [23]. The clinical version, a CRISPR-Cas9 CD33-deleted allograft, achieved day-28 neutrophil engraftment in all 30 patients with a median of 10 days; 19 received gemtuzumab maintenance up to 2 mg/m² without prolonged high-grade cytopenias, with three transplant-related deaths from renal failure, sepsis and sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, and the trial was stopped early [24]. A case report describes remission of *TP53*-mutant AML after the edited graft followed by donor-derived CD33 CAR T cells [45]. Base editing achieves the same protection at more than 70% efficiency and can be multiplexed [25]. **Evidence tier: phase 1/2a, engraftment endpoint met, stopped early; efficacy unproven.**

## 5. Checkpoint blockade

The randomized record is thin and the single-arm record is interpretable only with immune biomarkers.

Azacitidine plus nivolumab in relapsed/refractory AML (N=70) gave overall response 33% with CR/CRi 22%, and the response split sharply by prior exposure: 58% in hypomethylating-agent-naive patients versus 22% in pretreated patients. Grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events occurred in 11%. Pre-treatment marrow and blood CD3 and CD8 predicted response, and CTLA-4 rose on CD4 effector T cells in non-responders [27]. The frontline randomized test in older, less-fit patients within a platform trial was reported for the azacitidine-nivolumab comparison [29]; the arm was closed after a toxicity signal in this population (see evidence-trail notes for what could and could not be retrieved).

Pembrolizumab after high-dose cytarabine (N=37) gave overall response 46%, composite complete remission 38% and median overall survival 11.1 months, with grade ≥3 immune-related events in 14% and complete responders showing higher pre-treatment frequencies of progenitor-exhausted TCF-1-positive CD8 T cells [28]. Pembrolizumab with 10-day decitabine in 10 patients was feasible with stable disease or better in 6, and clonal CD8 effector-memory expansions tracked immune-related adverse events rather than antileukemic response [31]. Checkpoint exposure before transplant did not increase severe graft-versus-host disease in a small comparison, particularly with post-transplant cyclophosphamide [46].

After transplant, CTLA-4 blockade is the more active axis: ipilimumab at 10 mg/kg produced complete responses in 5 of 22 patients, four of them in extramedullary AML, with four responses durable beyond a year, alongside immune-related adverse events in 21% and graft-versus-host disease precluding further dosing in 14% including one death [30]. Responses tracked local CD8 infiltration and peripheral T-cell receptor turnover [47].

CD47 blockade with magrolimab and TIM-3 blockade with sabatolimab both reached randomized testing and both failed; the trials and their numbers are in `10-tp53-adverse-risk-and-secondary-aml.md`. CD70 blockade with cusatuzumab, which had a leukemia-stem-cell rationale and produced 8 complete remissions in 12 patients in phase 1 [48], gave complete remission in 12% and 27% of the two dose arms of a randomized phase 2 dose-optimisation study that stopped at the end of part one because the treatment landscape had moved [49].

## 6. Vaccines and adoptive NK cells

Vaccination has produced immunological responses that correlate with survival in single-arm studies and no randomized survival result. In 30 patients vaccinated with WT1 mRNA-electroporated dendritic cells as post-remission therapy, 13 had a demonstrable antileukemic response, 9 achieved molecular remission, 5-year overall survival was 53.8% in responders versus 25.0% in non-responders, and long-term clinical response correlated with circulating polyepitope WT1-specific CD8 T cells [36]. The comparison group was a national registry, not a concurrent control [36]. Galinpepimut-S gave median disease-free survival of 16.9 months from first remission in 22 patients with immune responses in 9 of 14 tested [37]; its phase 3 in second remission is an overall-survival study that had reached 72 of 80 required events by late December 2025 with the sponsor blinded to all efficacy data [38].

NK-cell transfer is the safest modality in this file and the least durable. Across 29 trials and roughly 600 patients, pooled response in relapsed/refractory AML was 35% with a fifth of patients bridged to transplant, 1-year disease-free survival was 82% in low/intermediate-risk remission and 26% in high-risk remission without transplant, graft-versus-host disease was 0–8%, and NK persistence was "typically short, improving with multiple infusions" [39]. CD33 CAR-NK cells produced MRD-negative complete remission in 6 of 10 patients by day 28 with no neurotoxicity and reversible marrow suppression [40]. An IL-15 superagonist given from day 60 after transplant expanded NK cells without inducing exhaustion, with a threefold reduction in 2-year relapse in patients receiving more than four doses at P=.06 [41].

## 7. Why the immune system fails here

Three mechanisms are documented and none requires a mutation.

**Antigen presentation is turned down, not lost genetically.** Paired sequencing of 15 post-transplant relapses found no relapse-specific mutations in immune genes but 3- to 12-fold downregulation of *HLA-DPA1*, *HLA-DPB1*, *HLA-DQB1* and *HLA-DRB1*, confirmed by flow or immunohistochemistry in 17 of 34 patients and reversible by interferon-γ in vitro [33]. An independent cohort found the same loss driven by *CIITA* downregulation, alongside upregulation of PD-L1, B7-H3, CD80 and PVRL2, each counteractable by interferon-γ or checkpoint blockade [34]. Outright genomic loss of the mismatched HLA haplotype occurs too, in 5 of 17 relapses after haploidentical transplant [35].

**The starting immune state predicts everything.** Across 442 primary samples from children and adults, immune-infiltrated and immune-depleted classes could be defined, and interferon-γ-related profiles predicted both chemotherapy resistance and flotetuzumab response [32]. That is the same axis in both directions: inflamed marrow resists cytarabine and responds to a T-cell engager. It also explains why *TP53*-abnormal disease, the most chemoresistant subgroup, is enriched among flotetuzumab responders [26].

**T cells in AML are exhausted and senescent before treatment starts**, and oncogenic signalling in the blast reduces its own susceptibility to killing [4,50]. The same review notes that AML subgroups with heightened interferon-γ signalling benefit from CD123×CD3 engagers and checkpoint inhibitors while responding less well to cytotoxic chemotherapy [4]. The *TP53*-deficient secretome finding [43] is one concrete instance.

## What is established vs contested

**Established.**
- No single AML surface antigen has a CD19-like therapeutic window; combinatorial or conditional targeting is required [1,2,3].
- Vadastuximab talirine increased deaths in a randomized phase 3 despite high remission rates [6,7].
- T-cell engagers produce complete remission in roughly 20–27% of refractory patients and no durable disease control [9,12,13,14,15].
- Post-transplant immune escape is predominantly transcriptional and interferon-γ-reversible in vitro [33,34].
- CD33-deleted allografts engraft normally and tolerate CD33-directed maintenance in humans [23,24].

**Contested or unresolved.**
- Why CLL-1 CAR T cells produced a 73.7% response in one series and none in another [18,20].
- Whether the flotetuzumab signal in *TP53*-abnormal AML [26] is real at scale.
- Whether checkpoint blockade has any role outside immune-infiltrated marrow or extramedullary post-transplant relapse [27,28,30,32].
- Whether vaccination adds anything a concurrent control would confirm [36,37,38].
- Whether short NK persistence [39] is fixable by engineering or is intrinsic.

## Barriers to cure in this domain

What specifically prevents the CD19-CAR-T-style result, and what evidence would show it solved:

1. **The target is the patient's myeloid system.** Every clinically tested antigen is shared with normal progenitors or stem cells [1,2,3], so the dose-limiting toxicity is marrow aplasia, not tumour lysis. Universal cytopenia with 45-day neutrophil recovery in the most active CAR series [18] and fatal infections in the phase 3 ADC [7] are the same toxicity at different intensities. **Solved when:** a therapy achieves deep remission with count recovery comparable to a hypomethylating agent, or when the normal compartment is edited to be resistant [23,24,25].
2. **The window that gene editing opens has not yet been shown to close the relapse gap.** The CD33-deleted graft met its engraftment endpoint in 30 patients and tolerated maintenance [24], but the trial stopped early and no relapse or survival comparison against a standard graft exists. **Solved when:** a randomized comparison of edited-graft-plus-maintenance versus standard transplant shows lower relapse.
3. **Antigen heterogeneity and loss defeat single-target constructs before durability can be assessed.** CLL-1-negative blasts persisted after CLL-1-directed depletion [20]; the field's own screens predicted this [1,2]. **Solved when:** a dual-target construct shows blast clearance without an antigen-negative residual population.
4. **AML has few neoantigens, so there is little for a released T cell to see.** The disease carries about 13 coding mutations per genome (`02-disease-biology-genomics.md`), which is why checkpoint blockade behaves as a marrow-inflammation-dependent therapy [27,28,32] rather than a broadly active one. **Solved when:** an interferon-γ or STING-based conditioning strategy converts immune-depleted marrow to immune-infiltrated and that conversion produces responses [32,44].
5. **Escape is transcriptional and therefore fast and reversible in both directions.** MHC class II downregulation needs no mutation and reverses with interferon-γ in vitro [33,34]; that means it can also recur. **Solved when:** a trial demonstrates that reversing class II loss in patients with documented downregulation produces remissions.
6. **The immunosuppressive contribution of the blast is genotype-specific and untargeted.** *TP53*-deficient AML actively suppresses engager-redirected T cells through TGF-β1 [43], in the same genotype whose inflamed marrow predicts engager response [26]. These findings are not reconciled and neither has been tested prospectively.
7. **Every positive signal in this domain sits in a bridge-to-transplant frame.** NKG2D CAR responses were durable only in transplanted patients [21], NK transfer bridged a fifth of responders to transplant [39], and the gene-edited graft strategy is definitionally post-transplant [24]. Immunotherapy in AML is currently a way to reach the curative modality, not a substitute for it, which returns the field to the access constraints in `08-transplant-post-remission-and-maintenance.md`.
8. **The comparator has moved while the class developed.** Cusatuzumab's randomized phase 2 stopped because venetoclax-azacitidine had made the enrolment question obsolete [49]; the vadastuximab phase 3 comparator was a hypomethylating agent alone [7]. Any new immunotherapy must now beat venetoclax-azacitidine in unfit patients, which no agent in this file has been tested against.

## Open research questions

1. What explains the discrepancy between CLL-1 CAR series [18,20], and does a head-to-head or pooled analysis of construct, lymphodepletion and manufacturing resolve it?
2. Does the CD33-deleted allograft plus CD33-directed maintenance reduce relapse relative to a standard graft, and can the approach be extended to CLL-1 or CD123 [23,24,25]?
3. Is the 47% complete response rate to flotetuzumab in *TP53*-abnormal AML [26] reproducible in a prospective, genotype-selected cohort, and how does it coexist with TGF-β1-mediated engager resistance in the same genotype [43]?
4. Can an immune-infiltration signature [32] be used prospectively to allocate T-cell-engaging therapy, and does allocating on it improve response?
5. Does interferon-γ, a STING agonist [44], or a hypomethylating agent restore MHC class II in patients with documented post-transplant downregulation, and does that produce remissions [33,34]?
6. Can NK persistence be extended beyond days without the toxicity that limits T-cell products [39,40,41]?
7. Would a vaccine trial with a concurrent control and an MRD-conversion endpoint be feasible in first remission, given that the only survival comparisons available are registry-based [36]?
8. What is the minimum durability an AML immunotherapy must achieve to be worth its marrow toxicity if the patient is not a transplant candidate?

### Evidence-trail notes

- All journal references were retrieved on 2026-09-09 through PubMed E-utilities with abstracts read; [7] is the ClinicalTrials.gov registry record, [8] and [38] are secondary and company sources cited as such.
- **The CASCADE phase 3 has no located peer-reviewed publication.** Its termination and stated reason come from the registry record's `whyStopped` field [7], which is the primary source, corroborated by trade reporting of the sponsor's announcement [8]. The sponsor's own press release could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so [8] is secondary coverage and is labelled as such. Results were posted to the registry in December 2018 but were not retrieved in detail here.
- **SWOG S1612's nivolumab arm.** The publication [29] exists and its title states what was compared, but PubMed carries no abstract and the full text was not retrieved, so no efficacy or toxicity figure from it is quoted. Web reporting describes the arm being closed in late 2019 after a toxicity signal with 15 patients accrued; that account is **[unverified]** here and is deliberately not stated as fact in the body beyond "closed after a toxicity signal", which the publication title alone does not establish. This should be resolved against the full text.
- **Vibecotamab phase 2 [14] and the CLL-1 CAR series [18]** are recent and were read from abstracts only; the CLL-1 adult series is registered on the Chinese registry (ChiCTR2000041054) rather than ClinicalTrials.gov and that identifier was not independently dereferenced.
- **Magrolimab, sabatolimab and cusatuzumab** are covered here only where this file's argument needs them; their trial numbers live in `10-tp53-adverse-risk-and-secondary-aml.md` and `02-disease-biology-genomics.md`.
- Gemtuzumab ozogamicin's own trial record is deliberately not repeated; see `03-intensive-chemotherapy-backbone.md`.
- Pediatric immunotherapy is touched only through [3]; the pediatric trial record belongs in `12-pediatric-aml.md`, which is not yet written.
- No AML immunotherapy has FDA or EMA approval as of this file's writing other than gemtuzumab ozogamicin; pivekimab sunirine's May 2026 approval [11] is for BPDCN. Regulatory status beyond that was not independently retrieved from agency sources.
