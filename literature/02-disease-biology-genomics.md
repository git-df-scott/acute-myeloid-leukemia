# Disease Biology: Genomics, Clonal Evolution, and Leukemia Stem Cells

> AML is not one disease and not one cell. Two decades of sequencing have shown it to be a small number of driver mutations arranged into a modest number of recurrent co-mutation classes [1,2,5], acquired over decades in long-lived hematopoietic stem cells whose clonal expansions are detectable years to decades before diagnosis [65,66,67,68,70,71]. Superimposed on that genetic architecture is a *cellular* architecture: a stem-cell-like compartment that is quiescent, metabolically distinct, niche-protected, and demonstrably the reservoir from which relapse arises [21,43,44,46]. Any credible campaign to cure AML has to solve both problems at once — eradicate every genetically fit subclone *and* eradicate the stem-cell state that regenerates disease after every cytoreduction. This document assembles the primary evidence for that two-axis model, records where LSC-directed and metabolism-directed translation has failed as prominently as where it has succeeded, and flags the specific biological gaps that currently make cure impossible for most adults.

## Key takeaways

- AML has an unusually low mutation burden for an adult cancer — ~13 coding mutations per genome, ~5 in recurrently mutated genes — which is why targeted therapy is plausible and also why so few neoantigens exist [1].
- 1,540 intensively treated patients yielded 5,234 driver mutations in 76 genes/regions; ≥2 drivers in 86% of patients; co-mutation patterns partition the disease into 11 classes, of which chromatin–spliceosome (18%) and TP53/aneuploidy (13%) are the poor-risk workhorses [2]. A later 3,653-patient effort extended this to 16 classes covering 100% of patients [5].
- Prognosis is a property of mutation *combinations*, not individual genes: the prognostic effect of a mutation is frequently reversed by co-mutation, most starkly in NPM1-mutant AML [2].
- The ELN 2022 genetic risk classification [6] was derived in intensively treated patients and does **not** stratify outcome usefully under venetoclax–azacitidine; in pooled VIALE-A/phase 1b data (n=279 ven-aza) only TP53, FLT3-ITD, NRAS and KRAS status separated median OS (26.5 vs 12.1 vs 5.5 months) [90]. Independent Beat-AML data in 595 older LIT-treated patients likewise failed to separate favorable from intermediate risk (P=.71) [91].
- Relapse follows two genomic patterns — founding-clone persistence with new mutation acquisition, or expansion of a pre-existing minor subclone — established by paired diagnosis/relapse whole-genome sequencing [18], and functionally traced to therapy-resistant cells within the stem/progenitor compartment [21].
- Pre-leukemic HSCs carrying DNMT3A (and other epigenetic-regulator) mutations but lacking NPM1 survive induction, persist in remission, and hold a multilineage repopulation advantage — a bona fide relapse reservoir that current therapy does not target [19,20].
- CHIP is common and strongly premalignant: detectable in 10% of persons >65 vs 1% of those <50, HR 12.9 (95% CI 5.8–28.7) for subsequent hematologic cancer, with ~42% of hematologic cancers in that cohort arising in people already clonal >6 months before diagnosis [66]; an independent cohort found mutations in 9.5% of those aged 70–79 with increased hematologic-cancer risk and all-cause mortality (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.1–1.8) [65]. Clones expand at mutation-specific exponential rates from ~5%/yr (DNMT3A, TP53) to >50%/yr (SRSF2 P95H) [70], and positive selection, not drift, dominates [69].
- Risk of progression is now quantifiable: the CHRS separates a high-risk minority from a majority with minimal progression risk using only a CBC plus sequencing [73]; TP53, IDH1/2 and spliceosome mutations, and high VAF, are the dominant adverse features in prospective pre-diagnostic cohorts [67,68].
- The first mechanistically rational *interception* target has emerged: DNMT3A-R882 HSPCs are OXPHOS-dependent, complex I and SLC25A1 inhibition suppresses mutant (not WT) clonal expansion in vivo, and metformin users in the UK Biobank (n=412,234) had markedly lower DNMT3A-R882 CH prevalence [80]. A single-arm phase 1 interception study is now recruiting (NCT07188740, n=32 planned) [81].
- The LSC17 score is the most clinically mature stemness biomarker: derived from functionally validated LSC fractions [36], it has now passed prospective real-world validation in 276 newly diagnosed patients (190 intensively treated: CR 63% vs 94%, MRD+ 46% vs 10%, OS HR 2.16), independent of ELN 2022 [37,38].
- LSC-directed metabolic and surface-antigen strategies have a poor clinical track record to date: the complex I inhibitor IACS-010759 was terminated for neurotoxicity with no RP2D [55]; devimistat failed phase 3 (ARMADA 2000, CR 20.4% vs 21.6%) [56]; cusatuzumab (anti-CD70) reached only 12–27% CR in CULMINATE and part two was abandoned [100]; sabatolimab (anti-TIM-3) phase 3 was terminated after missing OS [102]; magrolimab (anti-CD47) phase 3 ENHANCE-3 was terminated for futility with excess deaths [98].
- Venetoclax exposed a differentiation-state axis of drug sensitivity: primitive AML is BCL-2-dependent and OXPHOS-vulnerable [46,48], whereas monocytic AML loses BCL2, becomes MCL1-dependent, and is selected for at relapse [51]; relapsed LSCs additionally upregulate nicotinamide metabolism [49] and fatty-acid oxidation [50].
- The niche is an active accomplice, not scenery: AML remodels endosteal vasculature [59], induces sympathetic neuropathy [60], reprograms mesenchymal cells (STC1) [61], and — new in 2025 — draws on osteolineage-derived taurine via TAUT to drive glycolysis, with TAUT inhibition synergizing with venetoclax [57].
- Single-cell multi-omics is now the resolution at which AML biology is being rewritten: clone-specific differentiation routes [24], clone-specific drug responses in complex-karyotype disease [25], clone-specific IDH-mutant programs [26], hard-wired genotype–immunophenotype coupling in NPM1-mutant AML [27], and convergent genetic *and* non-genetic RAS activation as the resistance route to ven/gilteritinib [28].

## 1. The genomic landscape and the reference cohorts

(For a current clinical synthesis of how this genomic architecture maps onto diagnosis, risk-stratification and management, see the 2025 review by Shimony, Stahl and Stone [116].)

The foundational census came from TCGA: 200 clinically annotated adult de novo AML cases (50 whole genomes, 150 exomes) with RNA, miRNA and methylation profiling, establishing that AML carries far fewer mutations than most adult cancers — an average of 13 mutated genes, of which ~5 are recurrently mutated in AML — and grouping drivers into functional categories (transcription-factor fusions, NPM1, tumor suppressors, DNA methylation, signaling, chromatin modifiers, myeloid transcription factors, cohesin, spliceosome) [1].

Papaemmanuil and colleagues then converted the census into a classification. Across 1,540 patients enrolled in three prospective intensive-therapy trials, 111 cancer genes were interrogated; 5,234 drivers were found in 76 genes or genomic regions, with ≥2 drivers in 86% of patients. Co-mutation patterns compartmentalized the cohort into 11 classes. Beyond the already-defined entities, three new categories emerged: chromatin/RNA-splicing-mutated AML (18%), TP53/aneuploidy AML (13%), and provisionally IDH2-R172 AML (1%). Critically, gene–gene interactions repeatedly reversed the prognostic meaning of single mutations, most notably within NPM1-mutant disease [2]. Tazi et al. later extended this framework to 3,653 patients and 16 molecular classes covering 100% of patients, with "secondary AML-2" emerging as the second-largest class (24%) and prognostically adverse irrespective of flow MRD [5].

Beat AML added the missing functional dimension, linking mutational status to ex vivo drug response including combinatorially specific sensitivities [3], and its harmonized expansion showed that drug sensitivity is governed broadly by AML differentiation state with genomics acting as conditional modifier [4]. Whole-genome sequencing is a viable replacement for conventional cytogenetics, detecting all standard-of-care events plus new risk-relevant structural variants [15], and consensus guidance exists on how genomic profiling should drive decisions [16]. Pediatric AML is genomically distinct — fewer SNVs, more structural alterations, age-specific mutational interactions — so adult biology should not be assumed to transfer [17].

## 2. Ontogeny, secondary-type mutations, and TP53 biology

Lindsley et al. defined AML ontogeny genetically: in 194 rigorously defined secondary or therapy-related AML plus 105 unselected cases, a mutation in SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR or STAG2 was >95% specific for secondary AML; these lesions arise early, often persist in clonal remission, and identify a poor-outcome subset (lower CR, more reinduction, shorter EFS) hiding inside "de novo" elderly AML [9]. This concept is now embedded in both the ICC [7] and WHO 5th edition [8] as myelodysplasia-related gene mutations, though the interaction between those mutations and clinical ontogeny is not identical and has been dissected separately [10]. CEBPA has been re-carved by function: in 4,708 patients, bZIP in-frame mutations — not TAD mutations — carry the favorable prognosis [11].

TP53-mutant AML is the hardest biological problem in the field. It clusters with complex karyotype and aneuploidy [2], with dismal outcomes largely independent of allelic burden in AML [12], whereas in MDS the multi-hit/allelic state is the key discriminator of genome instability and outcome [13] — a principle carried into IPSS-M [14]. Therapy-related disease often arises not from chemotherapy-induced TP53 mutation but from expansion of pre-existing TP53-mutant clones under cytotoxic selection [77,78,79]. Patient-derived iPSC models show that TP53 mutation *plus* del(5q) — not TP53 mutation alone — is what licenses stepwise aneuploid evolution toward complex karyotype, with the resulting aneuploid HSPC state marked by BCL2 upregulation and venetoclax sensitivity, and resistant clones switching to alternative BCL2-family dependencies [29].

## 3. Clonal architecture and clonal evolution at relapse

Paired diagnosis/relapse whole-genome sequencing in 8 patients established the two canonical patterns: the founding clone always survives chemotherapy, and either (a) acquires new mutations and expands, or (b) a pre-existing minor subclone survives, expands, and then acquires new mutations [18]. Single-cell DNA sequencing of 146 samples from 123 patients (740,529 cells) refined the picture: AML is dominated by a small number of clones, frequently with co-occurring epigenetic-regulator mutations; clonal complexity rises with progression; and specific mutation combinations achieve dominance under therapeutic pressure while others do not [22]. Single-cell sequencing of secondary AML had already shown that the clonal architecture of the antecedent MDS is carried forward largely intact into the leukemic phase [23]. Clonal architecture itself — not just mutation content — predicts outcome and drug sensitivity [105], and transcriptional hierarchy composition can be read out from the same specimens by single-cell RNA-seq with genotyping, which resolves malignant cell types whose abundance varies both between patients and between subclones of the same tumor [30]. Under frontline venetoclax combinations, bulk molecular profiling of response and failure identified FLT3-ITD, biallelic TP53, and emergent RAS/kinase-pathway and BAX mutations as the recurrent routes to treatment failure [92].

Contemporary single-cell work has made this actionable. In NPM1-mutant AML (43 samples, 32 patients), diagnosis and relapse show similar architectural patterns, but *signaling* mutations drive increased clonal complexity specifically at relapse in a way that correlates with OS; genotype–immunophenotype relationships appear hard-wired, so mutation content constrains the lineage trajectory the leukemia can take [27]. In complex-karyotype AML, 75% of cases harbor multiple subclones with ongoing karyotype remodeling via breakage-fusion-bridge cycles and chromothripsis; PDX experiments showed subclone-specific LSC drug responses, including BCL-xL dependence [25]. In IDH-mutant AML, clone-specific programs track NPM1, NRAS and SRSF2 co-mutation, and relapse after IDH-inhibitor monotherapy is marked by upregulated stemness, inflammation, mitochondrial metabolism and anti-apoptotic factors with *down*regulated MHC class II [26].

Resistance is not only genetic. In a phase 1b ven/gilteritinib cohort (NCT03625505), single-cell DNA/protein and RNA/protein profiling showed FLT3-mutant clones were effectively eliminated, but resistance converged on RAS pathway activation through three routes — selection of RAS-mutant clones, non-mutational upregulation of RAS transcriptional programs, and a shift to RAS-associated monocytic differentiation — with RAS inhibition resensitizing in vitro [28]. Menin inhibition provides the cleanest example of on-target genetic resistance: MEN1 mutations at the drug–menin interface (M327, T349, G331, S160) arise under revumenib, are conserved across xenograft and base-editor screens, and were absent pre-treatment [93,94].

Immune-mediated escape is a parallel evolutionary axis. After allogeneic transplant, relapse is frequently accompanied by downregulation of MHC class II (via CIITA-axis repression) rather than by new driver mutations [103,104]. This has direct implications for LSC-directed immunotherapy: the target cell can change its visibility without changing its genotype.

## 4. Clonal hematopoiesis, CCUS, and pre-leukemia

Two 2014 NEJM papers established age-related clonal hematopoiesis as a population-scale premalignant state. Genovese et al. detected clonal hematopoiesis in 10% of persons >65 but only 1% of those <50, most often DNMT3A, ASXL1 or TET2, with HR 12.9 (95% CI 5.8–28.7) for subsequent hematologic cancer; ~42% of hematologic cancers in the cohort arose in individuals already clonal more than 6 months before diagnosis, and in two AML cases the leukemia demonstrably arose from the earlier clone [66]. Jaiswal et al. found mutations in 9.5% of those aged 70–79 and 11.7% of those aged 80–89, associated with increased hematologic-cancer risk and with all-cause mortality (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.1–1.8) plus incident coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke [65]. Two 2018 papers moved this to prediction: in the Women's Health Initiative, 212 women who later developed AML (median 9.6 years) were compared with matched controls, and IDH1, IDH2, TP53, DNMT3A, TET2 and spliceosome mutations significantly raised the odds of AML [67]; in a parallel effort, pre-diagnostic CH was shown to differ from benign CH by mutation identity, number, and VAF, permitting risk prediction in healthy individuals years ahead [68].

The evolutionary parameters are now measured, not assumed. Watson et al. used ~50,000 blood sequences to show positive selection rather than drift shapes CH, bounding HSC numbers and quantifying per-variant fitness effects [69]. Fabre et al. tracked 697 clones in 385 individuals ≥55 over a median 13 years: 92.4% expanded at a stable exponential rate, from ~5%/yr (DNMT3A, TP53) to >50%/yr (SRSF2 P95H); DNMT3A clones expanded early in life and slowed in old age, splicing-gene clones expanded only later, TET2 clones across all ages [70]. Lifespan phylogenetics showed marked loss of clonal diversity after age ~70 [71], consistent with earlier HSC population-dynamic estimates [72]; formal evolutionary modeling of CH was extended in 2026 [82].

Clinically, the CHRS (derived and validated in 438,890 UK Biobank exomes) uses eight laboratory and molecular features from a CBC plus sequencing to separate a high-risk minority from a large majority with minimal progression risk [73]. Real-world CCUS series confirm and refine this: multicenter risk prediction for clonal cytopenia [74] and clone-metric-based outcome models [75] both show that VAF, mutation number, and specific genes (spliceosome, TP53) drive progression, building on the earlier demonstration that driver mutations define distinct entities within myelodysplasia [76].

Two pre-leukemic-HSC papers anchor the cell biology. Shlush et al. found recurrent DNMT3A mutations at high VAF in purified HSC and mature fractions of AML patients *without* the NPM1 mutation present in blasts, and showed these DNMT3A-mutant HSCs outcompete wild-type HSCs in xenografts and survive chemotherapy, persisting in remission [19]. Corces-Zimmerman et al. independently showed preleukemic mutations preferentially affect epigenetic regulators and persist in remission [20]. A counterpoint worth recording: in DNMT3A-mutant/NPM1-mutant AML, retention of functional normal *and* preleukemic HSCs at diagnosis has been associated with **good** prognosis, so preleukemic-HSC burden is not monotonically bad [107].

Interception is the frontier. Gozdecka et al. performed a genome-wide CRISPR screen in primary Dnmt3a^R882H/+ mouse HSPCs, found 640 vulnerability genes enriched for mitochondrial metabolism, confirmed enhanced OXPHOS flux, and showed that CTPI2 (SLC25A1), IACS-010759 and metformin suppressed post-transplant expansion of mutant but not WT LT-HSCs; metformin's effect was recapitulated in a primary human DNMT3A-R882 CH sample, and in 412,234 UK Biobank participants metformin use was associated with markedly lower DNMT3A-R882 CH prevalence after adjustment for HbA1c, diabetes and BMI [80]. A prospective single-arm phase 1 study of metformin for DNMT3A-R882 CH in remission acute leukemia (VAF ≥5%, 6 months of therapy to 2,000 mg/day, primary endpoint VAF response at 6 months, n=32 planned) began recruiting in 2026 [81]. Older interception concepts — restoring TET2 function with vitamin C [83,84] — remain preclinical for AML prevention; no positive randomized prevention data were located as of 2026-09-09.

## 5. Germline predisposition

Germline predisposition is no longer a pediatric footnote. DDX41 is the most common adult germline predisposition to myeloid neoplasia: pathogenic germline variants are found in ~3% of adult MDS/AML, are autosomal dominant with high lifetime penetrance, comprise truncating alleles (~two-thirds), DEAD-box-domain missense variants and deletions, and >95% of truncating alleles detected on tumor NGS are germline — so a truncating DDX41 variant on a somatic panel should trigger germline testing [85,86]. DDX41-mutant MDS progresses rapidly to AML, largely confined to truncating variants [86]. Beyond DDX41, ANKRD26, CEBPA, ETV6, GATA2, RUNX1 and TP53 constitute the core adult-relevant list; two 2025 reviews synthesize pathogenesis and lifespan-spanning clinical implications [87,117]. Familial platelet disorder with germline RUNX1 progresses to myeloid malignancy through acquisition of additional clonal lesions [109]. Note the practice gap: a 2025 series documented low rates of germline investigation when somatic profiling flags variants of suspected germline origin [unverified — retrieved as a secondary summary only; primary article not fully verified].

## 6. Leukemia stem cells: functional definition and hierarchy

The LSC concept is functionally, not phenotypically, defined. Lapidot et al. showed a rare cell in AML patient blood initiates human AML on transplantation into SCID mice at a frequency of ~1 in 250,000 [32]; Bonnet and Dick localized this activity to a CD34+CD38− compartment and established the hierarchical model [33]. Two 2011 papers complicated the phenotype: Goardon et al. showed CD34+ AML commonly contains two functional LSC populations resembling LMPPs and GMPs [35], and Eppert et al. showed LSC-derived gene-expression programs — not immunophenotype — predict clinical outcome [34]. Transcriptional regulators of the LSC state have also been identified genotype-specifically — for example hepatic leukemia factor (HLF) in DNMT3A/NPM1/FLT3-ITD triple-mutant AML [106]. Markers subsequently proposed include CD47 [97], CD70 [99], TIM-3, GPR56 [113], and CD123; a 2026 review summarizes TIM-3's dual role as immune checkpoint and LSC marker [114] (title/metadata verified; the review's individual claims were not independently checked).

Persistence mechanisms are multiple and partly non-overlapping. Chemotherapy-resistant human AML stem cells home to and engraft within the endosteal region [43]. Cytarabine-residual AML cells are, importantly, *not* enriched for LSCs but are enriched for high-OXPHOS, high-mitochondrial-mass, CD36+, FAO-active cells — a high-OXPHOS gene signature predicting treatment response in PDX and patients [52]. Chemotherapy also induces a reversible senescence-like state, ATR-dependent, with downregulated MYC and LSC genes yet superior colony-forming and engraftment potential on recovery — meaning relapse-initiating capacity can be *induced* rather than pre-existing [45]. A transient post-chemotherapy "leukemic regenerating cell" state has been defined with its own vulnerability window [44]. Finally, functional proof that relapse originates in the stem/progenitor compartment came from combined genetic and functional analysis of purified subpopulations and xenografts from paired diagnosis/relapse samples [21].

Two 2025–2026 additions: LSC biomechanical properties can be targeted to suppress stemness and enhance NK-mediated killing [preclinical: 115]; and AML LSCs selectively downregulate heme biosynthesis enzymes to support self-renewal, such that HBE inhibition collapses complex IV, dysregulates copper chaperones and triggers cuproptosis, with glycolysis synthetic-lethal [preclinical: 58].

## 8. Bone marrow microenvironment and niche

AML does not passively occupy marrow; it rebuilds it. Duarte et al. showed AML progression remodels the endosteal vascular niche and that blocking this remodeling rescues normal HSC loss [59]. Hanoun et al. showed AML induces sympathetic neuropathy that alters the niche in a leukemia-permissive direction [60]. Mesenchymal niche remodeling via stanniocalcin 1 impairs normal hematopoiesis [61], and exosome secretion converts the niche to a leukemia-permissive state [62]. Metabolically, LSCs exploit an adipose-tissue niche: CD36+ LSCs induce lipolysis in gonadal adipose tissue, fuel FAO, and are protected from chemotherapy there [53].

The most consequential recent niche finding is metabolic cross-feeding. Temporal scRNA-seq of the stromal niche integrated with human LSC RNA-seq and in vivo CRISPR screens identified taurine–TAUT as a critical dependency: CDO1-driven taurine biosynthesis is restricted to osteolineage cells and rises with myeloid disease progression; blocking CDO1 in osteolineage cells impairs LSC growth and improves survival; TAUT loss impairs leukemia progression and, because TAUT is elevated in venetoclax-resistant AML, TAUT inhibition synergizes with venetoclax on primary human AML cells via RAG-GTP/mTOR-dependent glycolysis [57]. Spatial approaches are now mapping niche-specific inflammatory signaling and differentiation hierarchies [64] and immunotherapy-driven niche remodeling [63], and single-cell profiling shows TP53-mutant AML carries a distinctively reprogrammed hierarchy and disrupted immune–stromal ecosystem [110].

## 9. Metabolic dependencies — and the clinical record, including failures

Preclinically the case is strong. ROS-low functionally defined LSCs overexpress BCL-2 and depend on OXPHOS, and BCL-2 inhibition reduces OXPHOS and selectively eradicates quiescent LSCs [46]; de novo LSCs are aberrantly reliant on amino-acid uptake/catabolism to fuel the TCA cycle, with exogenous amino acids rescuing them from ven/aza [47]; and in patients ven/aza disrupted the TCA cycle (↓α-ketoglutarate, ↑succinate) via reduced glutathionylation of succinate dehydrogenase [48]. Relapsed/refractory LSCs escape through elevated nicotinamide metabolism, which activates both amino-acid metabolism and FAO (NAMPT inhibition selectively eradicates R/R LSCs preclinically) [49], and through fatty-acid metabolism itself [50]; AML cells additionally have low spare respiratory reserve [54]. Layered on top is the developmental-state axis: primitive AML is ven-sensitive while monocytic AML loses BCL2, becomes MCL1-dependent, and is selected at relapse [51].

Clinically, the translation record is largely negative so far. This must be stated plainly.

The interpretive lesson: differentiation-state biology (menin inhibition of NPM1/KMT2A-driven stemness programs) has produced approvals, whereas broad LSC-surface targeting (CD47, CD70, TIM-3) and broad mitochondrial inhibition (complex I, lipoic-acid analog) have not. Both approved menin inhibitors nonetheless deliver CR+CRh only ~21% with median response durations of 5–6 months in R/R disease, and on-target MEN1 mutations arise [93] — the stemness axis is real but not yet curative.

## 10. MRD, LSC, and the measurement problem

ELN consensus MRD guidance (2021, updated 2025) is the operational bridge between clonal biology and clinical decisions [88,89]. The 2025 ELN-DAVID update issued 56 recommendations (53 with ≥90% consensus), aligned MRD to ELN 2022 genetic risk, introduced a qualitative MRD response category (optimal / warning / high risk of treatment failure), and newly recommended ultrahigh-sensitivity NGS-based MRD for FLT3-ITD-mutated AML after intensive chemotherapy and before alloHCT [89]; NPM1 monitoring criteria are codified separately [112]. Two structural problems remain. First, MRD assays are calibrated to bulk clonal burden, not to the LSC compartment, even though flow LSC frequency adds independent prognostic information [40,41] and LSC17 itself predicts MRD positivity [37,39]. Second, persisting DNMT3A/TET2/ASXL1 mutations in remission commonly reflect residual clonal hematopoiesis rather than residual leukemia — an interpretive trap that ELN guidance addresses [88,89] and that pre-leukemic-HSC biology explains [19,20].

## What is established vs contested

**Established**

1. AML has a low coding mutation burden (~13 mutated genes/genome) for an adult cancer [1].
2. Driver co-mutation patterns define a limited set of reproducible genomic classes with distinct outcomes, and the prognostic effect of any single mutation is context-dependent on its co-mutations [2,5].
3. Secondary-type (myelodysplasia-related) mutations are highly specific for secondary ontogeny, arise early, and persist in remission [9].
4. Relapse arises either from the persisting founding clone or from a pre-existing minor subclone, with new mutations acquired in both patterns [18,22].
5. Pre-leukemic HSCs with epigenetic-regulator mutations survive chemotherapy and persist in remission [19,20].
6. CHIP is age-associated, common, and confers measurable myeloid-malignancy risk; clone growth reflects positive selection at mutation-specific rates [65,66,69,70], and progression risk is quantifiable from routine labs plus sequencing [73,74,75].
7. Germline DDX41 variants account for a substantial share of adult germline myeloid predisposition; truncating alleles on somatic panels warrant germline testing [85,86].
8. AML contains functionally defined stem cells detectable by xenotransplantation, and stemness gene programs — not surface phenotype alone — predict outcome [32,33,34,36,37].
9. ELN 2022 genetic risk does not transfer to venetoclax-based lower-intensity therapy [90,91].
10. Venetoclax response tracks differentiation state; monocytic AML is less BCL-2-dependent and is selected at relapse [51].
11. The marrow niche is actively remodeled by AML and contributes to LSC protection [53,57,59,60,61,62].
12. Immune escape via MHC class II downregulation is a major, largely non-mutational post-transplant relapse mechanism [103,104].

**Contested or unresolved**

1. Whether a single canonical LSC immunophenotype exists. Goardon's dual LMPP/GMP-like populations [35], CloneTracer's finding that dormant stem compartments are dominated by *residual healthy and preleukemic* cells while active LSCs resemble healthy counterparts and retain erythroid capacity [24], and the 2026 hrLSC2/CD34+CD90−CLL1−CD69+CD53− proposal [42, preprint] are not fully reconcilable.
2. Whether chemoresistant residual disease is LSC-enriched. Farge et al. explicitly found it is not, and is instead high-OXPHOS [52]; Shlush et al. traced relapse to stem-cell-compartment cells [21]. Both may be true in different patients or at different timepoints.
3. Whether relapse-initiating capacity is pre-existing or induced. Duy et al.'s ATR-dependent senescence-like resilient state [45] and Boyd et al.'s leukemic-regenerating cells [44] argue for therapy-induced plasticity, not just selection.
4. Whether pre-leukemic-HSC burden is uniformly adverse. Donato et al. report *better* prognosis in DNMT3A^mut/NPM1^mut AML retaining functional normal and preleukemic HSCs [107].
5. Whether OXPHOS dependence is a druggable LSC vulnerability in humans. Compelling preclinical and correlative human data [46,47,48,52] versus a failed complex I inhibitor [55] and a failed phase 3 mitochondrial-metabolism agent [56].
6. Whether LSC-surface antigens are viable targets. CD47, CD70 and TIM-3 all had strong LSC rationale [97,99] and all failed or underperformed in randomized/expansion settings [98,100,101,102].
7. Whether CHIP interception can prevent AML. The DNMT3A-R882/metformin data are mechanistically strong and epidemiologically supported [80], but the only registered interception trial has no results [81].
8. The correct blast threshold and definition for NPM1-mutant AML (ICC ≥10% vs WHO no threshold) remains discordant between the two 2022 classifications [7,8,112].
9. Whether taurine–TAUT, heme biosynthesis/cuproptosis, or LSC biomechanics represent tractable clinical targets — all are preclinical only [57,58,115].

## Barriers to cure in this domain

1. **The relapse reservoir is genetically and phenotypically distinct from the bulk disease that all response criteria measure.** CR is defined by blast morphology and increasingly by bulk MRD, but the cells that regenerate AML are rare, quiescent, sometimes preleukemic rather than leukemic, and sometimes only exist as a therapy-*induced* state [19,20,21,44,45]. We therefore cannot currently measure whether a therapy has done the thing that matters.
2. **Preleukemic HSCs are functionally normal enough that no available agent distinguishes them from wild-type HSCs.** DNMT3A/TET2/ASXL1-mutant HSCs sit inside a normal-looking multilineage compartment with a competitive advantage [19,20,70], so eradication risks marrow failure. The metabolic-vulnerability approach [80] is the only mechanistically selective lead and is unproven in humans.
3. **TP53-mutant/complex-karyotype AML has no biologically rational therapy.** The clone is chemoresistant, expands under cytotoxic selection [77,78,79], and evolves ongoing aneuploidy licensed by del(5q) plus TP53 loss [29]. The one immune approach with LSC rationale (CD47) killed patients [98].
4. **Resistance converges phenotypically from multiple genetic and non-genetic routes.** RAS-pathway activation arises via mutation, transcriptional upregulation, or monocytic differentiation shift in the same trial cohort [28]; MEN1 interface mutations arise on menin inhibition [93]; monocytic subclones with MCL1 dependence are selected by venetoclax [51]. Single-target strategies are therefore intrinsically time-limited.
5. **Differentiation state, not genotype, is often the dominant determinant of drug response** [4,31,51], yet clinical stratification remains genotype-based and ELN risk classification demonstrably fails under the most widely used lower-intensity regimen [90,91].
6. **The niche supplies metabolites and survival signals that in vitro and even xenograft models under-represent.** Osteolineage-derived taurine [57], adipose-derived fatty acids [53], and remodeled endosteal vasculature [59] mean that LSC metabolic dependencies measured ex vivo may not be the ones that matter in patients.
7. **Therapeutic-window failure for metabolic targets.** Complex I is essential in neurons and other high-OXPHOS tissues; IACS-010759 could not maintain target plasma levels without lactic acidosis and neuropathy [55]. Any OXPHOS-directed program needs either tissue-selective delivery or a leukemia-restricted node (e.g., SLC25A1, NAMPT, TAUT, heme biosynthesis) [49,57,58,80].
8. **Immune escape is decoupled from genotype.** Loss of MHC class II at post-transplant relapse [103,104] and downregulated MHC class II at IDH-inhibitor relapse [26] mean LSC-directed T-cell approaches can be defeated without any change in the target antigen.
9. **Assay and reference-standard gaps.** LSC quantification (xenotransplantation limiting dilution) is slow, expensive, patient-sample-limited, and not standardized; surrogate signatures (LSC17, hierarchy composition, hrLSC2) are not cross-calibrated, and only LSC17 has prospective real-world validation [37,42].
10. **Cohort composition bias.** The reference genomic cohorts that define classification are dominated by patients fit for intensive therapy [2,5], while the majority of incident AML is older and treated with lower-intensity regimens — precisely the population where existing risk models break [90,91] and where global epidemiology is shifting [111].

## Open research questions

1. Can a prospectively isolatable LSC phenotype (e.g., CD34+CD90−CLL1−CD69+CD53−/hrLSC2 [42]) be cross-validated against limiting-dilution xenotransplantation in an independent multicenter cohort and then used as the primary pharmacodynamic endpoint in an early-phase trial, replacing bulk CR?
2. Does adding LSC-compartment measurement (flow LSC frequency [40] plus LSC17 [37]) to ELN-2022-aligned MRD [89] improve prediction of relapse over MRD alone, and can an LSC17-high/MRD-negative group be randomized to intensified post-remission therapy?
3. In patients receiving venetoclax-based therapy, does a differentiation-state-based classifier (Primitive/GMP/Mature hierarchy composition [31]) outperform ELN 2022 and the TP53/FLT3-ITD/RAS signature [90] for treatment assignment, tested prospectively?
4. Can pre-emptive RAS-pathway inhibition, added at the point of monocytic/RAS-transcriptional shift detected by single-cell profiling, prevent rather than treat resistance to ven/FLT3-inhibitor therapy [28]?
5. Does metformin (or a selective SLC25A1/complex I inhibitor) reduce DNMT3A-R882 clone VAF durably in humans, and does VAF reduction translate into reduced progression to AML — the design question NCT07188740 [81] only begins to address?
6. In CHRS-high CHIP/CCUS [73,74], can a randomized interception trial with a molecular-response primary endpoint be powered feasibly, and what surrogate (VAF trajectory, clonal growth rate per Fabre kinetics [70], or incident cytopenia) is acceptable to regulators?
7. Is the TAUT–taurine axis [57] druggable in humans without impairing normal osteolineage or cardiac/retinal taurine biology, and does TAUT inhibition restore venetoclax sensitivity in venetoclax-refractory patients?
8. Does heme-biosynthesis inhibition-induced cuproptosis [58] spare normal HSPCs in humanized models, and what is the synthetic-lethal partner (glycolysis inhibition?) that gives a therapeutic index?
9. Can del(5q)+TP53-mutant preleukemic HSPCs be intercepted before aneuploid evolution, using the BCL2-dependence of the aneuploid HSPC state identified in iPSC models [29], and does that translate to venetoclax-based prevention in TP53-mutant CH after cytotoxic therapy [78,79]?
10. What is the mechanistic basis for MHC class II silencing at relapse [103,104] and at IDH-inhibitor failure [26], and can it be pharmacologically reversed to rescue LSC-directed immunotherapy?
11. Why do DNMT3A^mut/NPM1^mut AMLs retaining functional normal and preleukemic HSCs do *better* [107], and does preleukemic-HSC burden define a subgroup for whom stem-cell-sparing strategies are preferable?
12. Can ATR inhibition, timed to the chemotherapy-induced senescence-like resilient state [45] or the leukemic-regenerating-cell window [44], reduce relapse in a randomized post-induction consolidation design?
13. Do the 16-class unified molecular taxonomy [5] and the WHO/ICC discordances (notably NPM1 blast threshold [7,8,112]) materially change treatment assignment, and can a single harmonized classification be validated prospectively in lower-intensity-treated older patients?
14. What LSC and clonal-evolution biology is specific to pediatric AML [17, and 108 preprint], and to what extent can adult LSC-directed strategies be transferred?
