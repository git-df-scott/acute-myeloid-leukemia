# Epidemiology, Classification, and Risk Stratification of Acute Myeloid Leukemia

> AML is a disease of older adults whose population-level outcome has improved only modestly: in the United States roughly 22,700 people are projected to be diagnosed and 11,500 to die of AML in 2026, with 5-year relative survival of 33.4% overall and approximately 5% for patients aged ≥70 years across four decades of SEER follow-up [1,4]. Any credible campaign to *cure* AML must therefore begin from three uncomfortable epidemiologic facts: (i) the median patient is 70 years old and frequently never receives antileukemic therapy at all [1,5]; (ii) the disease is not one disease but a federation of genetically defined entities that two competing 2022 classification systems still describe with different names and different blast thresholds, so that trial cohorts are not interchangeable across systems [11,12,14,15]; and (iii) the risk-stratification instruments used to allocate transplant, intensity, and trial entry were derived in intensively treated younger adults and demonstrably degrade in the older, venetoclax-treated majority [22,23,26,28]. Classification and risk stratification are not bookkeeping: they determine who is offered curative-intent therapy, which biology is enriched in trials, and whether an apparent advance is real or an artefact of cohort drift.

## Key takeaways

- SEER projects 22,720 new AML cases and 11,500 AML deaths in the US in 2026 (1.1% of new cancers, 1.8% of cancer deaths); incidence 4.4/100,000/yr (2019–2023) was stable 2014–2023 while death rates fell 0.9%/yr (2015–2024); 5-year relative survival is 33.4% (2016–2022); median age at diagnosis 70, at death 74 [1].
- Global absolute burden is rising steeply on demographic grounds: GBD 2021 analyses report incident AML rising from 79,372 (1990) to 144,645 (2021), a +82.25% increase, with deaths +73.77% (74.9k → 130.2k) and DALYs +23.70%; burden is concentrated in high-SDI regions and in males and the elderly [2,3].
- Survival gains are age-stratified almost to the point of two diseases: in 29,107 SEER de novo AML patients, 5-year OS rose 9% → 15% → 22% → 28% across 1980–89, 1990–99, 2000–09, 2010–17, reaching 63% in ages 15–39 but remaining 5% in patients ≥70; 4-week mortality remained 20–45% in modern eras [4].
- Undertreatment is a first-order epidemiologic problem, not a footnote: among 7,665 SEER-Medicare AML patients aged ≥65 (2008–2015), 31% received neither antileukemic treatment nor supportive care; mean age 80.4 years, and 95.3% were dead by 180 days [5].
- Secondary and therapy-related AML are a growing, genetically distinct fraction: t-AML incidence in Sweden rose 4.5%/yr (95% CI 2.8–6.2), nearly doubling, and t-AML made up 686/6,779 (10%) of all AML over 1997–2015 [7]; in Denmark tAML was 6.6% and sAML 19.8% of 3,055 unselected patients [6]; eight "secondary-type" mutations (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR, STAG2) are >95% specific for secondary AML [9].
- WHO-HAEM5 and ICC 2022 disagree in ways that change diagnoses in ~1 in 8 patients: reclassification versus WHO 2016 was 22.8% (WHO 2022) and 23.7% (ICC), with a 13.1% difference in patient distribution *between* the two 2022 systems in 1,001 real-world AML cases [18].
- The central structural divergences are the AML blast threshold (WHO-HAEM5 removes the 20% floor for most genetically defined AML, retaining it only for AML with BCR::ABL1 and AML with CEBPA mutation; ICC keeps a 10% floor and creates a distinct "MDS/AML" category for 10–19% blasts), the handling of TP53 (a standalone ICC entity requiring ≥20% blasts and VAF ≥10%; no separate WHO entity), and the myelodysplasia-related gene list (ICC counts RUNX1; WHO does not, but WHO adds del(11q) and −13/del(13q) as MR cytogenetics) [11,12,13,14].
- ELN 2022 works for intensively treated younger adults and fails for the older/less-intensive majority: it reclassified 15% of 1,118 patients (12% into more adverse groups) with *slightly worse* OS discrimination than ELN 2017 [24]; in an Alliance cohort of 1,637 patients the intermediate and adverse groups were not separable in patients ≥60 years [23]; in Beat AML, 78% of patients ≥60 given lower-intensity therapy were ELN 2022 adverse [26].
- ELN 2024 supplies a purpose-built less-intensive classification driven by four gene groups (favorable: NPM1/IDH2 without signaling comutation, IDH1 with AZA+IVO, DDX41; adverse: mutated TP53; intermediate: everything else with FLT3-ITD/NRAS/KRAS), with reported median OS ~39, 37, 29, >24 months for the favorable strata versus 5–8 months for TP53-mutated [21].
- Model comparisons are treatment-specific, not absolute: in 426 real-world patients, C-index for OS was ELN 2022 0.680 vs Beat AML 2024 0.664 vs ELN 2024 0.601 with intensive therapy, but ELN 2022 failed to separate favorable from intermediate risk in less-intensive therapy where ELN 2024 and Beat AML 2024 did [28]; in 669 PETHEMA HMA-treated patients ELN 2024 stratified outcomes but 24-month AUC improved from 0.71 to 0.76 only after adding cytogenetic risk and secondary AML [29].
- Machine learning improves discrimination modestly and inconsistently: a 2026 meta-analysis of 24 studies/137 model cohorts found a pooled *validation* AUC of 0.769 (95% CI 0.742–0.795), with non-genetic models (0.776) no worse than gene-centric models (0.741) and little prospective validation [33]; the HARMONY model beat ELN 2022 for 5-year OS (AUC(t) 0.741 vs 0.700) in 2,550 patients with external validation in 714 [34].
- Diagnostic turnaround is a solvable rate-limiting step, and waiting is safe: median time from diagnosis to treatment was 3 days (IQR 2–7) in 2,263 intensively treated patients with no OS association across strata up to >15 days (2-year OS 51/48/44/50%, P=.211) [43]; clinical whole-genome sequencing returned results in a median of 5 days and changed ELN risk category in 16.2% of 117 prospective patients [41].
- Genotype now gates approved therapy, which makes classification an access issue: FDA approved revumenib for R/R NPM1-mutant AML on 24 Oct 2025 (AUGMENT-101, CR+CRh 23.1%) and ziftomenib on 13 Nov 2025 (KO-MEN-001, N=112, CR+CRh 21.4%, median duration 5.0 months) — both requiring an NPM1 result [49,50].

## 1. Burden of disease

### United States

The NCI SEER program projects 22,720 new AML diagnoses and 11,500 AML deaths in the United States in 2026, representing 1.1% of all new cancer cases and 1.8% of all cancer deaths [1]. Age-adjusted incidence was 4.4 per 100,000 per year (5.2 in males, 3.7 in females; 2019–2023 cases) and was statistically stable over 2014–2023; the death rate was 2.7 per 100,000 (3.4 male, 2.1 female; 2020–2024 deaths) and declined by an average 0.9% per year over 2015–2024 [1]. Five-year relative survival for 2016–2022 was 33.4%. Median age at diagnosis is 70 and median age at death 74; the peak incidence decade is 65–74 years, accounting for 26.6% of new diagnoses. Lifetime risk is approximately 0.5%, and an estimated 83,311 Americans were living with AML in 2023 [1].

The single most important structural feature of AML epidemiology is the divergence of outcome by age. In the largest population-based series, 29,107 SEER patients with *de novo* AML diagnosed 1980–2017, median survival by treatment decade was 4, 5, 7, and 11 months, and 5-year survival 9%, 15%, 22%, and 28%; patients aged 15–39 reached 63% 5-year survival in 2010–2017 while patients aged ≥70 remained at 5%, and 4-week mortality — 37% in 1980–89 overall — remained 20% to 45% in modern eras depending on age stratum [4]. Real-world improvement in older adults after venetoclax approval is documented but partial: a US cohort comparing 2014–2018 with 2019–2022 found higher overall survival in the post-approval era, with the improvement particularly evident in non-Hispanic Black patients receiving venetoclax-based induction [52].

A large share of the population-level mortality is attributable to patients who are never treated. Among 7,665 SEER-Medicare patients aged ≥65 diagnosed 2008–2015, 2,373 (31%) received neither active antileukemic therapy nor supportive care; their mean age was 80.4 years, and 79.7% and 95.3% had died by 60 and 180 days respectively [5]. This is a population that essentially never appears in the trial literature.

### Global

GBD 2021-based analyses put global incident AML at 144,645 cases in 2021, up 82.25% from 79,372 in 1990, with deaths rising 73.77% (74.92 × 10³ → 130.19 × 10³) and DALYs rising 23.70% (3,342.91 × 10³ → 4,135.06 × 10³) [2,3]. Age-standardized incidence, death, and DALY rates are higher in high-SDI than low-SDI regions, and males and the elderly are disproportionately affected [2]. Modelled projections in GBD-derived analyses forecast continued growth in absolute case and death counts through 2040 (reported figures approximately 184,000 incident cases and 166,000 deaths) — *[unverified: this specific projection appeared in secondary summaries of GBD analyses and was not confirmed against a retrieved abstract]*. Because most of the rise is demographic (population ageing and growth), absolute AML burden will increase even if age-specific risk stays flat — a planning fact for any cure campaign.

Pediatric AML is epidemiologically and biologically separate. In a SEER-based analysis, childhood AML age-adjusted incidence rose from 5.766 to 7.607 per 1,000,000 across 1975–2005, while 5-year relative survival rose from 22.40% (1975–84) to 68.30% (2005–14), with widening disparities by race and socioeconomic status [54]. A 2026 international expert panel has now issued pediatric-specific consensus recommendations, explicitly because children's AML carries distinct cytogenetic and molecular features requiring different diagnostics, response criteria, and supportive care [47].

## 3. Classification: WHO-HAEM5 versus ICC 2022

### Architecture

Both 2022 systems moved AML from a morphology-first to a genetics-first hierarchy, and both split AML into (a) entities defined by genetic abnormality and (b) a residual set defined by differentiation. WHO-HAEM5 lists AML with PML::RARA, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, CBFB::MYH11, DEK::NUP214, RBM15::MRTFA, BCR::ABL1, KMT2A rearrangement, MECOM rearrangement, NUP98 rearrangement, NPM1 mutation, CEBPA mutation, AML myelodysplasia-related (AML-MR), and a deliberately open "AML with other defined genetic alterations" category, plus eight differentiation-defined types (minimal differentiation, without maturation, with maturation, acute basophilic, acute myelomonocytic, acute monocytic, acute erythroid, acute megakaryoblastic) and myeloid sarcoma as a tissue-based manifestation [11]. The ICC uses a parallel genetic hierarchy but applies ontogeny (progression from MDS/MPN, prior cytotoxic therapy, germline predisposition) as *qualifiers* appended to the genetic diagnosis rather than as separate entities [12,13].

### Empirical consequences

Three independent cohorts have quantified the impact. In 1,001 real-world AML patients, reclassification relative to WHO 2016 was 22.8% under WHO 2022 and 23.7% under ICC, with a 13.1% difference in patient distribution *between* the two 2022 systems; of 397 ICC MDS-related AML cases, 55.9% were defined by MDS-related karyotype [18]. In a 717-MDS/734-AML sequencing cohort, morphologically defined AML entities collapsed from 13% to 5% of cases, while myelodysplasia-related AML expanded to 28% (WHO 2022) and 26% (ICC) [17]. In a multicentre Chinese cohort of 568 AML and 75 MDS/AML with independent validation (n=524), AML-MR and ICC MDS/AML were molecularly and clinically convergent — ASXL1 25.0% vs 22.7%, TP53 19.4% vs 12.0%, complex karyotype 24.4% vs 21.3%; median OS 10.3 vs 13.3 months (P=0.425) — arguing that the WHO/ICC split at this interface is largely nosological rather than biological, while AML-MR itself carried a genuinely worse prognosis than non-AML-MR (median OS 10.3 vs >22 months, P<0.001) [19]. The practical corollary is that patients in the 10–19% blast zone can be scored by either an AML instrument (ELN) or an MDS instrument — the IPSS-M, built on 2,957 patients and 31 genes and restratifying 46% of cases relative to IPSS-R [48] — with no consensus on which applies.

### Reconciliation status as of September 2026

There is no unified successor classification. Reviews through 2025 and 2026 continue to frame the two systems as convergent in concept and divergent in operational criteria, and explicitly identify standardization of blast thresholds, TP53 handling, and MDS–AML interface definitions as the remaining harmonization targets [14,15,16,17]. **No published harmonized WHO/ICC AML classification, and no WHO 6th edition or formal ICC revision, was located in searches through September 2026.** Practically, this means every AML cohort assembled after 2022 must state which system it used, and meta-analyses that pool WHO-2022 and ICC-2022 cohorts are pooling non-identical populations.

## 4. Genetic risk stratification

### ELN 2022 (intensive therapy)

The 2022 ELN recommendations define three genetic risk categories at diagnosis [20]: **Favorable** — t(8;21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1; inv(16) or t(16;16)/CBFB::MYH11; mutated NPM1 without FLT3-ITD; bZIP in-frame mutated CEBPA. **Intermediate** — t(9;11)/MLLT3::KMT2A; wild-type NPM1 with FLT3-ITD (without adverse lesions); mutated NPM1 with FLT3-ITD; and any cytogenetic/molecular abnormality not classified as favorable or adverse. **Adverse** — t(9;22)/BCR::ABL1; t(6;9)/DEK::NUP214; t(v;11q23.3)/KMT2A-rearranged; t(8;16)/KAT6A::CREBBP; inv(3)/t(3;3)/GATA2, MECOM(EVI1); t(3q26.2;v)/MECOM-rearranged; −5 or del(5q); −7; −17/abn(17p); complex karyotype; monosomal karyotype; mutated ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, RUNX1, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, U2AF1 and/or ZRSR2; mutated TP53. The major changes from 2017 were abandonment of the FLT3-ITD allelic ratio (all FLT3-ITD now intermediate irrespective of ratio or NPM1 status), restriction of favorable CEBPA to in-frame bZIP mutations regardless of mono- or biallelic status, and promotion of myelodysplasia-related gene mutations to adverse risk.

The pattern is unambiguous: ELN 2022 compresses the older, less-intensively treated population into the adverse category (72.8–78% in two independent datasets [22,26]), which destroys its clinical utility exactly where the epidemiologic mass of the disease sits.

### ELN 2024 for less-intensive therapy

ELN 2024 is a purpose-built genetic classification for newly diagnosed AML receiving HMA monotherapy, HMA/venetoclax, or azacitidine + ivosidenib (IDH1-mutated); it explicitly **does not apply** to patients previously treated with an HMA [21].

| ELN 2024 risk category | Genetic definition | Reported median OS |
|---|---|---|
| Favorable | Mutated NPM1 (FLT3-ITDneg, NRASwt, KRASwt, TP53wt) | 39 months |
| Favorable | Mutated IDH2 (FLT3-ITDneg, NRASwt, KRASwt, TP53wt) | 37 months |
| Favorable | Mutated IDH1 (TP53wt) — applies specifically to AZA+IVO, irrespective of signaling mutations | 29 months |
| Favorable | Mutated DDX41 (≈two-thirds germline origin) | >24 months |
| Favorable | Other cytogenetic/molecular abnormalities (FLT3-ITDneg, NRASwt, KRASwt, TP53wt); incl. AML with MR gene mutations in this state | 23 months (MR-gene subgroup) |
| Intermediate | Other cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities with FLT3-ITDpos and/or NRASmut and/or KRASmut; TP53wt (incl. MR-gene mutations in this state) | 12–13 months |
| Adverse | Mutated TP53 | 5–8 months |

Two details deserve emphasis. First, the classification is essentially a *signaling-comutation* classifier layered on a small favorable gene set: NPM1-mutated AML without signaling mutations had median OS 39 months versus 9.9 months with co-occurring FLT3-ITD/KRAS/NRAS; the same pattern held for IDH2 (36.9 vs 12.2 months), RUNX1 (32.5 vs 9.3 months), and MR-gene AML (22.9 vs 12.9 months) [21]. Second, cytogenetically adverse AML *without* TP53 mutation had median OS 23.4 months on azacitidine/venetoclax — i.e. adverse cytogenetics is not the dominant adverse variable in this setting, TP53 is [21]. ELN 2024 also flags that a DDX41 mutation at near-heterozygous VAF should prompt germline evaluation [21].

### Non-genetic, composite, and clinical models

Genetics alone does not predict early death. The **AML composite model (AML-CM)** added comorbidity burden (HCT-CI), age, albumin, platelet count, and LDH to cytogenetic/molecular risk in 1,100 patients aged 20–89 across five academic centres (training 733 / validation 367), predicting 1-year mortality with C statistic 0.72 and AUC 0.76 and outperforming HCT-CI, augmented HCT-CI, and Karnofsky score [35]; it was subsequently revised onto the ELN 2017 backbone [36]. A 12-factor MD Anderson model in 3,728 intensively treated patients (adding therapy-related myeloid neoplasm, performance status, cardiac comorbidity, and laboratory variables to NPM1/TP53/PTPN11 genotype) separated four groups with 5-year OS from 69% to 3% [37]. A 2026 LASSO-Cox nomogram in a Japanese cohort selected nine clinical variables, achieving C-index 0.721 (training) and 0.678 (validation) with 3-year OS 84.1% / 57.2% / 11.9% across three strata [38]. These models exist because fitness, not only biology, decides who can be cured — and they are systematically under-used relative to ELN.

## 5. Diagnostic workup, technology, and turnaround time

ELN 2022 requires integrated morphology, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and targeted molecular testing at diagnosis, with results for therapeutically actionable lesions available rapidly enough to inform the initial treatment decision [20]. Contemporary reviews frame this as the direct consequence of a therapeutic landscape in which at least twelve agents have been approved in AML since 2017, most of them biomarker-selected, and in which MRD is being folded into longitudinal risk assessment rather than used once at diagnosis [44,45,46]. *(A frequently quoted 3–5 day target for NPM1, FLT3, IDH1/IDH2 appears in secondary summaries of ref [20]; the exact figure was not confirmed against the retrieved primary text — [unverified].)* The clinical stakes of turnaround have risen sharply because genotype now gates approved drugs: revumenib was approved 24 Oct 2025 for R/R NPM1-mutant AML (AUGMENT-101, NCT04065399; CR+CRh 23.1%, 95% CI 13.5–35.2; median duration of response 4.5 months) [50], and ziftomenib on 13 Nov 2025 for R/R NPM1-mutant AML (KO-MEN-001, NCT04067336, N=112; CR+CRh 21.4%, 95% CI 14.2–30.2; CR 17.0%; median duration of CR+CRh 5.0 months) [49] — in both cases with NPM1 status determined by NGS or PCR.

Crucially, the evidence says it is safe to wait for those results in a clinically stable patient. In the German SAL-AML registry, 2,263 non-APL patients given intensive induction had a median time from diagnosis to treatment of 3 days (IQR 2–7), and 2-year OS across TDT strata of 0–5, 6–10, 11–15 and >15 days was 51%, 48%, 44%, and 50% (P=.211), with no association between TDT and OS [43]. This is a genuinely practice-changing negative result: it removes the main clinical argument against waiting for molecular stratification.

Two technologies are displacing classical cytogenetics. Clinical **whole-genome sequencing** in 263 patients with myeloid cancers detected all 40 recurrent translocations and 91 copy-number alterations identified by standard cytogenetics, produced new clinically reportable genomic events in 40/235 (17.0%), and in a prospective arm of 117 consecutive patients returned results in a median of 5 days, providing new genetic information in 29 (24.8%) and changing risk category in 19 (16.2%) [41]. **Optical genome mapping** in 100 newly diagnosed adults detected 91.4% of abnormalities found by chromosome banding, added information in 37% (95% CI 28–47%), reclassified 14% to unfavorable cytogenetics and 7.7% to ELN 2022 adverse risk, and uncovered cryptic KMT2A and NUP98 lesions — directly targetable with menin inhibitors — in 10% of cases [42].

**Measurable residual disease** is now the principal dynamic risk stratifier. A meta-analysis of 81 publications and 11,151 patients found MRD negativity associated with HR 0.36 for OS (95% bayesian CrI 0.33–0.39) and 0.37 for DFS (0.34–0.40), corresponding to estimated 5-year OS 68% vs 34% and DFS 64% vs 25% [40]. The 2025 ELN-DAVID MRD consensus (published 2026) issued 56 recommendations, 53 with ≥90% consensus, aligned MRD assessment to the ELN 2022 genetic risk framework, introduced qualitative MRD response categories, recommended ultrahigh-sensitivity NGS-based MRD for specified AML subtypes, and specified multiparameter flow cytometry during the first year after allogeneic transplant with qPCR or UHS-NGS beyond two years [39].

Finally, classification and stratification are not globally available. Reviews of AML care in low- and middle-income countries identify absent or unreliable access to cytogenetics and molecular profiling — and to the targeted agents those tests select for — as central obstacles, compounded by supply-chain and expertise barriers in flow cytometry [53]. A risk classification that requires a 20-gene NGS panel within days is, for much of the world, a classification that cannot be applied.

## 6. Representativeness and disparities

Trial-derived risk models inherit trial populations. A systematic review and meta-analysis of AML randomized trials found that only 23.3% reported race/ethnicity and 28.9% reported the proportion aged ≥65; among trials reporting race, participants were 80.8% White, 9.8% Asian/Pacific Islander, 4.7% Black, 3.4% Hispanic, and 0.5% Native American/Alaska Native, with enrollment incidence ratios of 0.28 (Hispanic) and 0.16 (Asian) versus 1.23 (White) [51]. Reporting improved from 19.5% to 46.2% of trials over the last decade [51]. The Alliance validation of ELN 2022 additionally found that risk-group separation behaved differently within African-American and Hispanic subgroups [23] — i.e. the classifier's calibration is not demonstrably race-invariant. Real-world data do show narrowing of some gaps in the venetoclax era [52], but this is measured against a baseline of substantial disadvantage.

## What is established vs contested

**Established**

1. AML is a disease of ageing: median age at diagnosis 70; 5-year relative survival 33.4% overall, ~5% at age ≥70 across four SEER decades [1,4].
2. Absolute global burden is rising and will continue to rise on demographic grounds [2,3].
3. A large minority of older patients receive no antileukemic therapy and die within months [5].
4. Secondary-type mutations (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR, STAG2) are highly specific markers of secondary AML biology [9]; t-AML incidence is rising [7].
5. Both 2022 classifications are genetics-first, and both substantially reclassify patients relative to WHO 2016 [17,18].
6. ELN 2022 discriminates outcome in intensively treated adults <60 years [23,25].
7. ELN 2022 does not usefully stratify older or less-intensively treated patients, assigning 72.8–78% to adverse risk [22,26].
8. TP53 mutation is the dominant adverse genetic variable in the less-intensive setting, with median OS 5–8 months [21,22].
9. MRD negativity is strongly associated with survival across methods and subgroups [40].
10. Delaying induction by up to ~2 weeks to obtain genetics does not compromise survival in stable patients [43].
11. Genome sequencing and optical genome mapping reproduce conventional cytogenetics and add clinically actionable information in a substantial minority [41,42].
12. AML trial populations under-represent Black, Hispanic, Asian, and older patients [51].

**Contested**

1. Whether the ICC "MDS/AML" (10–19% blasts) category is a biologically real entity or an administrative convenience — evidence of molecular and survival convergence with WHO AML-MR argues against distinctness [19].
2. The correct blast threshold, if any, for genetically defined AML (0% vs 10% vs 20%) [11,12,14,15].
3. Whether TP53-mutated myeloid neoplasia should be defined by VAF ≥10%, by higher VAF cutoffs, by multihit status, or by karyotype context [30] — and whether low-VAF TP53 should carry adverse risk at all.
4. Whether RUNX1 mutation belongs in the myelodysplasia-related gene set [12,14].
5. Whether ELN 2022 or ELN 2017 discriminates better in intensively treated adults; at least one large cohort found ELN 2022 slightly *worse* for OS [24].
6. Which less-intensive-therapy model to adopt: ELN 2024, the VIALE-A 4-gene signature, Beat AML 2024, or refinements adding cytogenetics and secondary AML — head-to-head C-indices differ by treatment and cohort [22,26,28,29].
7. Whether machine-learning models add clinically actionable discrimination over ELN plus clinical variables; pooled validation AUC ~0.77 with non-genetic ≥ genomic models argues for caution [33,34].
8. Whether "secondary/therapy-related" ontogeny retains independent prognostic value after genetic adjustment [8,10] versus in HMA-treated cohorts where sM-AML improved model fit [29].
9. Whether composite fitness models (AML-CM) should formally gate intensity decisions [35,36,37].
10. Whether NPM1-mutated disease with <20% blasts should be treated as AML [14, and the divergent thresholds in 11,12].

## Barriers to cure in this domain

1. **The classification schism creates a measurement problem that propagates into every trial.** With 13.1% of patients distributed differently between WHO 2022 and ICC 2022 [18], eligibility criteria, historical control arms, and reported subtype-specific outcomes are not comparable across studies; apparent efficacy differences can be generated purely by which nosology a protocol used.
2. **Risk classifiers are derived under one therapy and applied under another.** ELN 2022 was built from intensively treated cohorts, so its adverse category absorbs 72.8–78% of the venetoclax-era population and loses resolution precisely where most patients are [22,26]. ELN 2024 fixes this for HMA-based therapy but is explicitly inapplicable to HMA-pretreated patients and performs worse in HMA monotherapy and secondary AML [21,29].
3. **The adverse category is a heterogeneity sink.** Within ELN 2022 adverse risk, patients reclassified solely by MR gene mutations had 5-year OS 26% versus 12% for other adverse genotypes [24], and cytogenetically adverse TP53-wild-type AML reached 23.4 months median OS on azacitidine/venetoclax [21]. Treating "adverse" as a single indication for transplant or for experimental therapy mis-allocates both.
4. **TP53 is defined by an operational threshold rather than by biology.** The ICC's VAF ≥10% cutoff was chosen to exclude non-leukemic clonal variants, not because 10% marks a biological transition [12,30]; multihit status, clone size, and karyotype context are not integrated, and low-VAF cases remain unclassifiable in practice.
5. **The 10–19% blast interval is a diagnostic no-man's-land.** ICC MDS/AML and WHO AML-MR converge molecularly and in survival (10.3 vs 13.3 months, P=0.425) [19], yet the two labels route patients to different trial portfolios and different reimbursement pathways.
6. **Ontogeny is a proxy for exposure history that classification erases.** Because ELN 2022 accounts for the prognostic effect of secondary ontogeny [10], the *causal* signal — prior cytotoxic therapy, antecedent clonal hematopoiesis — disappears from risk models, removing the variable that a prevention strategy would target, even as t-AML incidence rises 4.5%/yr [7].
7. **Fitness is not in the genetic classifiers.** Early mortality is driven by comorbidity, albumin, platelets, LDH, and performance status [35,37]; genetic risk models silently assume the patient survives long enough for biology to matter, which for a 31%-untreated ≥65 population is not a safe assumption [5].
8. **Diagnostic latency and diagnostic access are both rate-limiting, in opposite directions.** In well-resourced centres, molecular results arrive in ~2–5 days and waiting is safe [41,43]; in much of the world cytogenetics and NGS are unavailable at all [53], so the same classification cannot be operationalized, and menin-inhibitor-eligible NPM1 or KMT2A lesions — 10% cryptic by OGM alone [42] — go undetected.
9. **Prognostic models are validated retrospectively and almost never prospectively.** The 2026 meta-analysis found limited prospective validation across 137 model cohorts [33]; no model in this domain has been shown in a randomized design to improve survival by changing treatment allocation.
10. **Trial populations do not match the disease population.** Under-representation of Black (4.7%), Hispanic (3.4%), Asian (9.8%) and older patients [51], plus differential classifier behaviour by ancestry group [23], means risk thresholds calibrated on trials may mis-assign therapy in the patients with the worst outcomes.
11. **Modest per-decade survival gains compound too slowly to reach cure.** Absolute 5-year survival rose 9% → 28% across 1980–2017 in de novo AML but did not move at all in patients ≥70, and 4-week mortality remained 20–45% [4]; incremental risk-adapted intensification has plateaued in the population that carries most of the mortality.
12. **Post-cure disease persists as clonal hematopoiesis.** Among 373 long-term AML survivors, 61.9% carried CH variants, with ≥10% VAF clones linked to secondary neoplasms and diabetes [56] — cure defined as leukemia-free survival is not the same as restoration of a normal hematopoietic system.

## Open research questions

1. Does prospective randomization of treatment allocation by a validated less-intensive-therapy classifier (ELN 2024, with or without cytogenetics and sM-AML added as in [29]) versus ELN 2022 improve 2-year overall survival in patients ≥65 receiving HMA/venetoclax?
2. Can a single harmonized WHO/ICC AML classification be prospectively tested against both parent systems for prognostic discrimination and treatment-assignment concordance in a multinational cohort of ≥5,000 patients, with the blast threshold treated as a continuous variable rather than a cut point?
3. Is the ICC MDS/AML (10–19% blasts) category prognostically or therapeutically separable from WHO AML-MR when treatment intensity is randomized, or should the two be merged as [19] implies?
4. What is the optimal operational definition of TP53-driven AML — VAF threshold, multihit status, clone dominance, or TP53 plus complex karyotype as a "very adverse" stratum [24] — as judged by discrimination for early death and by response to TP53-agnostic versus TP53-directed therapy?
5. Does RUNX1 mutation carry independent adverse prognosis after adjustment for co-mutations and ontogeny in the intensive and less-intensive settings separately, and should it therefore be in or out of the MR gene list?
6. Can a prognostic model that combines genetics, comorbidity (AML-CM-type variables), and early MRD kinetics outperform ELN plus MRD alone for prediction of 1-year mortality, and does using it to select intensity reduce early death in a randomized trial?
7. Do machine-learning models with externally validated AUC ~0.74–0.77 [33,34] change treatment decisions in a way that improves survival, tested as a randomized decision-support trial (model-guided vs guideline-guided transplant allocation in CR1)?
8. What is the incremental clinical yield and cost-effectiveness of front-line whole-genome sequencing or optical genome mapping versus karyotype + FISH + targeted NGS, measured as ELN risk reclassification, menin-inhibitor-eligible lesion detection, and time to correct therapy [41,42]?
9. Can a diagnostic package deliverable in resource-limited settings (targeted PCR/NGS for NPM1, FLT3, IDH1/2, TP53, plus flow cytometry) reproduce ELN 2024 risk assignment with acceptable concordance against full cytogenetics-plus-panel testing, and does deploying it improve survival in LMIC cohorts [53]?
10. Given that treatment delay up to ~2 weeks is prognostically neutral [43], can a randomized strategy trial of "genotype-first induction" (wait for full molecular results, then assign) versus "immediate empiric induction" demonstrate superiority in event-free survival?
11. What is the population-attributable fraction of AML caused by specific prior therapies (PARP inhibitors, radioligand therapy, autologous transplant conditioning, CAR-T lymphodepletion) in the current treatment era, and can pre-treatment clonal hematopoiesis screening identify patients in whom leukemogenic regimens should be avoided?
12. Does prospective clonal-hematopoiesis surveillance in AML long-term survivors [56] identify a population in whom intervention prevents second myeloid neoplasms, and what is the natural history of persistent CH after cure?
13. Why does the ELN classifier behave differently across ancestry groups [23] — germline modifiers, co-mutation frequencies, treatment access, or all three — and can ancestry-aware recalibration reduce outcome disparities?
14. Can the ~5% 5-year survival in patients ≥70 [4] be moved by a strategy trial in which risk stratification explicitly includes untreated-patient pathways (i.e. randomizing supportive care versus low-intensity therapy in patients whom current practice would not treat)?
