# Menin Inhibitors in NPM1-mutated and KMT2A-rearranged AML

> Menin inhibition is the clearest demonstration in AML that a chromatin-level transcriptional dependency can be drugged, and it is also the clearest demonstration that drugging one is not the same as curing anything. Twenty years elapsed between the discovery that MLL fusion proteins require physical interaction with menin [1] and the first FDA approval of a menin inhibitor (revumenib, 15 November 2024) [14]; two more approvals followed within twelve months (revumenib for *NPM1*-mutated AML, 24 October 2025 [15]; ziftomenib, 13 November 2025 [19]). Together *NPM1* mutations (~30% of adult AML) and *KMT2A* rearrangements (~10% of adult AML, ~20% of pediatric AML) define the largest genetically actionable slice of the disease [44,69,47]. Yet in the registration trials, single-agent menin inhibition produced complete remission plus CR with partial hematologic recovery (CR+CRh) in only 21–23% of heavily pretreated patients, with median response durations of 4.5–6.4 months [12,13,16,18,20] — and roughly 40% of relapses after revumenib carry acquired *MEN1* binding-site mutations [25,38]. The clinical question this domain now poses is therefore not whether menin is a real target but whether combination and frontline use can convert a short differentiation-driven remission into durable disease eradication. Five randomized phase 3 trials, enrolling an estimated 3,691 patients, are testing exactly that; none has reported [58].

## Key takeaways

- **Target validation is unusually clean.** Menin is required for MLL/KMT2A-fusion leukemogenesis and for its maintenance, and acute genetic ablation of menin reverses aberrant *HOX* expression and the differentiation block [1]. The same dependency exists in *NPM1*-mutant AML, where mutant NPM1 binds chromatin and co-occupies KMT2A targets [8], organizing phase-separated nuclear condensates that sustain *HOX/MEIS1* transcription [9], and in *NUP98*-rearranged AML [10].
- **Single-agent activity in relapsed/refractory disease is real but modest and short-lived.** AUGMENT-101 *KMT2A*r: CR+CRh 22.8% (95% CI 12.7–35.8) in 57 centrally confirmed efficacy-evaluable patients, ORR 63.2%, P=.0036 [12]; the FDA-reviewed population of 104 treated patients gave CR+CRh 21.2% (13.8–30.3) with median duration 6.4 months [16]. AUGMENT-101 *NPM1*m: CR+CRh 23.4% (one-sided P=.0014) in 64 efficacy-evaluable adults, ORR 46.9%, median CR+CRh duration 4.7 months [13]. KOMET-001 phase 2 (ziftomenib, *NPM1*m): CR/CRh 22% (14–32), ORR 33%, median duration of response 4.6 months, median OS 6.6 months [18].
- **The two approved drugs have materially different toxicity profiles, and the labels say so.** REVUFORJ carries a boxed warning for **both** differentiation syndrome (25% of 241 patients; grade 3/4 in 12%; 2 deaths) and QTc prolongation/torsades (36%; QTcF >500 ms in 10%; one fatal cardiac arrest, one non-sustained torsades) [16]. KOMZIFTI carries a boxed warning for differentiation syndrome only (26% of 112; grade 3 in 13%; 2 deaths), with QTc prolongation in 12% [20]. Ziftomenib population PK shows flat exposure–response and no clinically meaningful azole interaction, supporting a fixed 600 mg once-daily dose [71].
- **Ziftomenib is not approved for *KMT2A*r AML, and the reason is a safety signal, not indifference.** Enrollment of *KMT2A*r patients in KOMET-001 phase 1 was halted because of the rate and severity of differentiation syndrome [17]; the label records **4 fatal differentiation-syndrome cases among 39 *KMT2A*r AML patients** treated with ziftomenib [20]. This is the single most important negative finding in the domain.
- **Combination therapy raises response rates far above monotherapy — in single-arm phase 1 studies.** Revumenib + azacitidine + venetoclax in newly diagnosed adults ≥60 years (BEAT AML, N=43): ORR 88.4%, composite CR 81.4%, CR 67.4%, and 37/37 evaluable patients MRD-negative by central flow cytometry [32]. Revumenib + oral decitabine/cedazuridine + venetoclax in relapsed/refractory disease (SAVE, N=42): CRc 71%, CR/CRh 60%, MRD-negative in 80% of those, median CR/CRh duration 10.5 months [33]. Ziftomenib + venetoclax/azacitidine in R/R *NPM1*m AML at 600 mg (N=48 evaluable): CRc 46%, median DOR 8.6 months [34]. None of these is randomized.
- **Prior venetoclax exposure roughly halves combination benefit.** In KOMET-007, CRc was 70% (16/23) in venetoclax-naive versus 24% (6/25) in venetoclax-exposed R/R *NPM1*m patients [34].
- **Resistance is dominated by *MEN1* binding-site mutations, and it is inhibitor-specific.** Somatic *MEN1* mutations at the drug–menin interface were identified in patients with acquired resistance to revumenib and reproduce resistance in xenografts and base-editor screens [25]; up to 39% of post-revumenib relapses carry them [38]. CRISPR base-editor profiling across five clinical menin inhibitors identified shared (M327I/V/T, G331D) and inhibitor-specific (C334R, E368K/V, V372A) resistance alleles [26]. Ziftomenib retains activity against T349M and G331R but not M327I or G331D [22]; bleximenib retains activity against M327I and T349M via a distinct binding mode [23]. One patient has been reported to respond to menin-inhibitor switching (bleximenib → revumenib) [27,28].
- **Outcomes after menin-inhibitor failure are poor and should anchor expectations.** In 84 adults across 4 US centers, 40% received supportive care only after failure; among 50 treated, non-trial CR/CRi was 19% and ORR 32%; median OS from next therapy 4.4 months (15.4 months if CR/CRi achieved vs 3.4 months if not, P=.048). No *FLT3*-ITD patient responded to gilteritinib (0/6 gilteritinib-naive). *FLT3*-ITD, *WT1*, and *MEN1* mutations associated with resistance [27].
- **Two menin inhibitors have already failed.** DS-1594b was stopped at phase 1 in 17 patients with **no responses** (4 patients had >25% marrow blast reduction), no recommended phase 2 dose established, and differentiation syndrome in 29% [35]. The BMF-219/icovamenib acute leukemia trial was terminated after 55 patients; the sponsor's stated reason was withdrawal from oncology, explicitly not safety or efficacy [37]. Revumenib in colorectal and other solid tumors was terminated after phase 1b for failing prespecified efficacy [58].
- **Transplant remains the only demonstrated route from a menin-induced remission to potential cure, and access to it is limited.** HSCT followed treatment in 24/104 (23%) of the *KMT2A*r registration cohort but only 7/65 (11%) of the *NPM1*m cohort [16] and 4/112 (3.6%) of the ziftomenib cohort [20]. Depth of remission matters: in 48 revumenib-treated patients, median OS was 23.6 months for morphologic CR with undetectable MRD versus 20.8 months with detectable MRD versus 3.2 months in non-responders [49].

## 1. Biology and preclinical proof of concept

Menin, encoded by *MEN1*, is a nuclear scaffold with no catalytic activity. Yokoyama and colleagues showed in 2005 that oncogenic MLL (KMT2A) fusion proteins retain a high-affinity, amino-terminal menin-binding motif; that this interaction is required for initiation of MLL-mediated leukemogenesis; that menin is required for *maintenance* of MLL-transformed but not other oncogene-driven myeloid transformation; and that acute genetic ablation of menin reverses *HOX* dysregulation and abrogates the differentiation block [1]. That paper defined a druggable protein–protein interaction and, unusually, a therapeutic window: the dependency is oncogene-specific rather than lineage-general.

Chemical proof of concept followed in stages. Grembecka and colleagues reported the first nanomolar-affinity small-molecule menin–MLL inhibitors in 2012, showing downregulation of fusion target genes with selective differentiation and apoptosis of MLL-translocated cells [2]. Borkin et al. developed the orally bioavailable MI-463 and MI-503, with survival benefit in murine MLL models and activity in primary patient samples [3]. Critically, He et al. showed the effect is **fusion-partner independent** — the inhibitors block transformation by MLL-AF9, MLL-AF6, MLL-ENL and others alike [4], which is the mechanistic basis for enrolling all *KMT2A* rearrangements in a single trial cohort. Krivtsov et al. then reported VTP50469, the direct precursor of revumenib, showing displacement of menin from protein complexes, loss of MLL chromatin occupancy at select genes, and long-term disease-free survival in multiple *KMT2A*r ALL patient-derived xenografts [6].

The *NPM1* arm of the story is independent and equally important. Kühn et al. used a CRISPR/Cas9 domain screen to show that *NPM1*-mutant AML is exceptionally dependent on the menin-binding site of MLL1, and that pharmacologic menin–MLL1 disruption has profound antileukemic activity in human and murine *NPM1*mut models — with further synergy from combined DOT1L inhibition [5]. Uckelmann et al. later demonstrated that mutant NPM1 itself binds specific chromatin targets co-occupied by KMT2A, and that targeted NPM1c degradation collapses gene expression and RNA polymerase II occupancy at those targets, providing the direct mechanistic explanation for why menin inhibitors work in *NPM1*c leukemia [8]. The current model frames mutant NPM1 as an organizer of phase-separated nuclear transcriptional condensates that concentrate regulators at active chromatin [9]. Separately, Uckelmann et al. showed that VTP-50469 reverses aberrant self-renewal in *Npm1c/Dnmt3a* mutant mice **before** leukemia develops — the only preclinical evidence in this domain for a preventive, pre-leukemic application [7].

The dependency extends beyond the two approved indications. Menin–MLL1 is a molecular dependency in *NUP98*-rearranged AML, with VTP50469 IC50 values comparable to *KMT2A*r and *NPM1*c cells and survival prolongation in NUP98-NSD1 and NUP98-JARID1A PDX models [10]; combining menin inhibition with CDK6 or FLT3 inhibition is synergistic in NUP98-r models [55]. Menin dependency has also been proposed for *UBTF* tandem duplication and other HOX-driven entities, and menin inhibition upregulates MHC class I and II via CIITA activation, sensitizing blasts to T-cell cytotoxicity [24] — an immunologic mechanism not yet tested clinically. Menin is a dependency in proliferative megakaryocytes as well, which both explains the 15–20% rate of falling platelet counts in treated patients and raises myelofibrosis as a candidate indication [53].

## 3. Differentiation syndrome and QTc: the two class toxicities

Differentiation syndrome is the on-target consequence of forcing blast maturation and was recognized in the first-in-human revumenib study as a hallmark of drug activity [11]. Across menin inhibitors it occurs in roughly 10–25% of patients [50], but the label data show the rate is disease-context dependent, not uniform.

For revumenib, pooled across 241 patients (207 adult, 34 pediatric) treated at the recommended dose, differentiation syndrome occurred in 60 (25%), was grade 3/4 in 12%, and was fatal in 2. Median time to onset was 9 days (range 3–41). Incidence by disease: 33% in *KMT2A*r AML, 33% in *KMT2A*r MPAL, 9% in *KMT2A*r ALL, and 18% in *NPM1*m AML. Treatment interruption was required in 7%; withdrawal in 1% [16]. For ziftomenib, differentiation syndrome occurred in 29/112 (26%) of *NPM1*m patients, grade 3 in 13%, fatal in 2, with median onset 15 days (range 3–46) and interruption in 13% [20]. The *KMT2A*r experience with ziftomenib is the outlier: 4 fatal cases among 39 patients [20], the finding that stopped that cohort in phase 1 [17] and that keeps *KMT2A*r off the ziftomenib label.

Both labels operationalize the same mitigation: reduce white cell count to <25 × 10⁹/L before starting; treat suspected differentiation syndrome immediately with systemic corticosteroids (dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 12 hours in adults; 0.25 mg/kg/dose in children <40 kg for revumenib) for a minimum of 3 days and until resolution; add hemodynamic monitoring; use hydroxyurea and, if needed, leukapheresis for non-infectious leukocytosis; interrupt the drug if severe signs persist beyond 48 hours of steroids or earlier for life-threatening pulmonary involvement; restart steroids promptly if symptoms recur on taper [16,20]. Efforts to find a predictive biomarker are early — high-throughput plasma proteomic profiling during revumenib treatment in pediatric AML is the only such approach located [51].

QTc prolongation separates the agents. Revumenib: reported in 86/241 (36%), grade 3 in 15% and grade 4 in 2%, QTcF >500 ms in 10%, increase >60 ms from baseline in 24%, dose reduction in 7%; incidence rose with age (21% under 17 years, 35% at 17–<65 years, 46% at ≥65 years), and there was one fatal cardiac arrest and one non-sustained torsades de pointes [16]. A published case report documents revumenib-associated QTc prolongation with multiple episodes of torsades and ventricular arrhythmia [52]. The label therefore requires an ECG before initiation (do not start if QTcF >450 ms), weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly, with electrolyte correction and specified interruption/reduction thresholds at >480 ms and >500 ms [16]. Ziftomenib's QTc rate was 12% (grade 3 in 8%, QTcF >500 ms in 9%), the threshold for not initiating is QTcF >480 ms, and no QTc boxed warning applies [20]; the once-daily fixed 600 mg dose with no azole adjustment is supported by flat population exposure–response modeling [71]. In practical terms revumenib is a twice-daily, CYP3A4-interacting, ECG-intensive drug (270 mg twice daily without a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, 160 mg twice daily with one) and ziftomenib is a once-daily drug whose principal constraint is differentiation syndrome plus avoidance of acid-reducing agents [16,20].

One further monitoring hazard is specific to differentiation therapy: menin inhibition changes blast immunophenotype. In sequential marrows from 48 revumenib-treated patients, 16/31 (52%) showed dynamic immunophenotypic shifts — myeloid/stem-like to monocytic or the reverse, or substantial antigen-intensity changes — meaning flow-cytometric MRD after menin inhibition can be misread if the shift is not recognized [49].

## 4. Combination and frontline strategies

Because monotherapy remissions are shallow and brief, essentially the entire field has moved to combinations. The preclinical rationale for pairing menin inhibition with BCL2 inhibition is direct: menin inhibition changes expression of anti-apoptotic regulators, and menin inhibitor plus venetoclax is synergistic for differentiation and proliferation arrest in vitro and prolongs survival in *NPM1*-mutant PDX models beyond either agent [54,73]. Bleximenib synergizes with venetoclax plus azacitidine and with gilteritinib in *KMT2A*r cells [23], and a RARα agonist (tamibarotene) synergizes with revumenib in *RARA*-high *KMT2A*r/*NPM1*c models [56].

## 5. Pediatric and infant disease

*KMT2A* rearrangements are enriched in children, occurring in roughly one fifth of pediatric AML and the majority of infant ALL [47,46]. Revumenib's label covers patients 1 year and older for both indications, with weight- and BSA-based dosing down to 0.4 m² [16], and 25/104 (24%) of the *KMT2A*r registration cohort and 34/241 (14%) of the pooled safety population were under 17 years [16]. Two pediatric-specific safety observations matter: QTc prolongation was *less* frequent in children (21% under 17 years versus 46% at ≥65 years) [16], while differentiation syndrome in *KMT2A*r AML and MPAL (33% each) is a pediatric-weighted risk given the age distribution of those diseases [16].

The pediatric development pathway is now substantial: a Children's Oncology Group phase 2 of revumenib plus lymphoid- or myeloid-directed chemotherapy in **R/R *KMT2A*-rearranged infant leukemia** (NCT05761171, n=78 estimated, MRD-negative remission rate as a primary endpoint), St. Jude's phase 1 of revumenib plus azacitidine and venetoclax in pediatric/young-adult R/R AML (NCT06177067), and PedAL's phase 1 of ziftomenib plus fludarabine/cytarabine in pediatric R/R *KMT2A*r, *NUP98*r or *NPM1*m acute leukemia (NCT06376162) [58,60]. A published multinational adult-and-pediatric consensus initiative argues explicitly for integrating pediatric populations into adult menin-inhibitor trials rather than running them serially [45]. Post-transplant revumenib maintenance in high-risk pediatric *KMT2A*r acute leukemia has been reported in case-series form [48], and a small pediatric post-HSCT maintenance series (n=10, *KMT2A*r or *NUP98*r) with 100% 1-year event-free survival was presented at ASH 2025 [40,63].

AUGMENT-102 (NCT05326516), which combined revumenib with fludarabine/cytarabine in a predominantly pediatric R/R population, completed with 30 patients [58,61]; efficacy figures circulating in secondary coverage (composite CR ~50–56% across two dose levels) could not be verified against a retrieved primary abstract or publication and are therefore recorded here as **[unverified]**.

## 6. Resistance

Three distinct resistance mechanisms are documented, with very different evidentiary status.

**On-target *MEN1* binding-site mutation — established.** Perner et al. identified somatic *MEN1* mutations at the revumenib–menin interface in patients with acquired clinical resistance and showed that these mutants attenuate drug–target binding without disrupting the natural MLL1 interaction, preventing inhibitor-induced eviction of menin and MLL1 from chromatin; the same alleles reproduce resistance in xenografts and in an unbiased base-editor screen [25]. This was the first demonstration that a chromatin-targeting drug exerts enough selection pressure in patients to drive escape-mutant evolution. Frequency estimates: up to 39% of post-revumenib relapses [38]; 13% of patients in SAVE developed emergent menin-binding-site mutations [33]; mutations may emerge within 2–3 months of therapy [38]. Detection in cell-free DNA is feasible [30].

Cross-resistance is only partial, which is the field's main therapeutic opening. CRISPR base-editor profiling against five clinical menin inhibitors, with co-crystal structures for each, distinguished shared alleles (M327I/V/T, G331D) from inhibitor-specific ones (C334R, E368K/V, V372A), and orthogonal in vitro and in vivo mutagenesis under therapeutic pressure indicated the identified alleles are likely to arise against all menin inhibitors [26]. Drug-specific data align: ziftomenib retains antileukemic activity against T349M and low-nanomolar potency (GI50 ≤25 nM) against G331R, while M327I and G331D are resistant, with co-crystal structures rationalizing the pattern [22]; bleximenib retains potent activity against engineered *MEN1* M327I and T349M cells and binds menin in a mode distinct from revumenib [23]. Clinical proof that switching works is currently a single reported patient (bleximenib → revumenib) [28] plus 1/2 responders in a retrospective failure cohort [27].

**Off-target signaling and co-mutation — established as prognostic, unsolved therapeutically.** In 1,750 children on Children's Oncology Group trials, RAS pathway mutations were present in 43% of AML cases, and RAS pathway mutation in *KMT2A*r AML was associated with lower CR rate, worse EFS and OS, and early relapse. Revumenib plus the MEK1/2 inhibitor selumetinib was synergistic in cell lines and PDX-derived cultures and reduced leukemia burden in vivo — **but did not improve overall survival compared with menin inhibition alone** [31]. This is an important negative preclinical result and is reported here with the same weight as the positive synergy data. In the clinical failure cohort, *FLT3*-ITD, *WT1* and *MEN1* mutations were associated with resistance, and no *FLT3*-ITD patient responded to gilteritinib after menin-inhibitor failure (0/6 gilteritinib-naive) [27].

**Fusion-partner and lesion-type effects — mixed.** Preclinically, menin–MLL inhibition is fusion-partner independent [4]. Clinically, the AUGMENT-101 registration cohort spanned t(9;11) (22%), t(11;19) (19%), t(6;11) (10%), t(10;11) (10%), t(4;11) (7%) and others, and CR+CRh is not reported by translocation in the label [16]. Fusion partner does, however, carry prognostic weight independent of menin inhibition: in 490 newly diagnosed *KMT2A*r patients from the East China Leukemia Alliance, KMT2A::MLLT3 was most frequent (31.6%), followed by MLLT4 (25.1%), ELL (20%) and MLLT10 (12.7%), with KMT2A::MLLT4 conferring inferior prognosis; *KRAS* (20.1%), *NRAS* (19.0%), *TET2* (10.2%), *WT1* (8.6%) and *PTPN11* (7.4%) were the commonest co-mutations, and median OS was 30.9 months overall [57]. An earlier 172-patient MD Anderson series found translocation subtype not predictive in multivariate analysis while age and allogeneic HSCT were [42]. Lineage matters more than partner in the menin data: CR+CRh in the *KMT2A*r registration cohort was 21% in AML, 19% in ALL and 50% in MPAL (n=2) [16], and differentiation syndrome was three-fold less common in *KMT2A*r ALL (9%) than AML (33%) [16]. Finally, *KMT2A* partial tandem duplication was **excluded** from AUGMENT-101 [16] — a real evidence gap, since KMT2A-PTD accounted for 18.2% of 303 KMT2A-associated AML cases in one series and responded well to venetoclax-based rather than intensive therapy [74].

## What is established vs contested

**Established**

1. Menin is a required cofactor for KMT2A-fusion leukemogenesis and maintenance, and its genetic or pharmacologic disruption reverses *HOX*/*MEIS1* dysregulation and the differentiation block [1,2,3,6].
2. *NPM1*-mutant AML shares this dependency through direct chromatin binding by mutant NPM1 at KMT2A-co-occupied targets [5,8,9].
3. Revumenib produces CR+CRh in ~21–23% of R/R *KMT2A*r acute leukemia and ~23% of R/R *NPM1*m AML, with median response duration under 7 months; ziftomenib produces CR/CRh in ~21–22% of R/R *NPM1*m AML with median duration ~5 months [12,13,16,18,20].
4. Differentiation syndrome (10–26%, occasionally fatal) and QTc prolongation are class toxicities; their relative severity differs between agents, and revumenib's QTc liability is age-dependent and label-restricted [16,20,50].
5. Acquired *MEN1* binding-site mutations are a genuine, patient-derived, on-target resistance mechanism, detectable in cell-free DNA, arising within months, and only partially cross-resistant between inhibitors [25,26,30,33].
6. Outcomes after menin-inhibitor failure are poor (median OS 4.4 months from next therapy) [27].
7. Not every menin inhibitor works: DS-1594b produced no responses in 17 patients and was discontinued [35].
8. Adding a menin inhibitor to venetoclax/azacitidine or to 7+3 markedly increases response rate and MRD-negativity in single-arm frontline cohorts [32,64,62].

**Contested or unresolved**

1. Whether any of these response gains translate into an overall survival benefit. No randomized menin-inhibitor trial has reported [58].
2. Whether menin inhibitors belong in frontline therapy for all *NPM1*m/*KMT2A*r patients or only in defined subgroups (older, unfit, MRD-persistent, transplant-ineligible).
3. Whether the *KMT2A*r differentiation-syndrome hazard is drug-specific (as the ziftomenib label's 4/39 fatal cases suggest [20]) or dose/schedule-dependent and manageable across the class.
4. Whether menin-inhibitor switching is a reproducible strategy or an anecdote. Current clinical evidence is one to two patients [27,28].
5. Whether the correct sequencing is menin inhibitor before or after venetoclax. KOMET-007 shows prior venetoclax exposure cuts CRc from 70% to 24% [34], which argues for earlier menin use — but this is a non-randomized subgroup comparison in a heavily pretreated population.
6. Whether combining menin inhibition with RAS/MAPK blockade helps. The one systematic preclinical test improved leukemia burden but not survival [31].
7. Whether menin inhibition should be used as post-transplant maintenance. Evidence is case series and small phase 1/2 trials only [48,58].
8. Whether the indication should extend to *NUP98*r, *UBTF*-TD, KMT2A-PTD and other HOX-driven disease. Preclinical support is strong for *NUP98*r [10,55]; clinical data are confined to the 21% *NUP98*r subgroup of SAVE (median CR/CRh duration 5.9 months, the shortest of the three genotypes) [33] and single case reports [36].

## Barriers to cure in this domain

1. **Response depth is decoupled from response durability.** Menin inhibitors achieve MRD-negativity in a majority of responders — 68.2% of evaluable responders in AUGMENT-101 *KMT2A*r [12], 80% in SAVE [33], 37/37 in BEAT AML [32] — yet median monotherapy response duration remains 4.5–6.4 months [16,20]. Flow-cytometric MRD negativity after differentiation therapy therefore does not certify eradication of the self-renewing clone, and interpretation is further confounded by treatment-induced immunophenotypic switching in ~52% of patients [49].
2. **On-target escape is fast and structurally predictable.** Because the drug occupies a defined pocket on a scaffold protein whose *physiological* ligand (MLL1) binds the same site differently, single amino-acid substitutions can abolish drug binding while preserving oncogenic function [25]. Base-editor screening shows the mutational escape space is shared across the entire current class at key residues (M327, G331) [26]. No allosteric or degrader-based menin therapeutic has reached clinical proof of concept in leukemia.
3. **The differentiation mechanism generates its own dose-limiting toxicity.** Differentiation syndrome is not an off-target effect that better chemistry can remove; it is the therapeutic mechanism expressed systemically. Its incidence tracks with the disease subsets most in need of the drug (*KMT2A*r AML and MPAL, 33% each [16]), it forced abandonment of a whole genotype cohort for one agent [17,20], and it caps the dose intensity and combination density achievable.
4. **QTc liability restricts eligibility and combination partners.** Revumenib requires QTcF ≤450 ms to start, weekly ECGs for a month, and interruption thresholds that in practice exclude many older patients with cardiac comorbidity or QT-prolonging concomitant drugs [16] — precisely the population in which *NPM1*m AML is most common (median age 65 in the registration cohort [16]).
5. **Co-mutations that drive proliferation are not addressed by differentiation therapy.** RAS pathway mutations occur in 43% of pediatric AML and worsen every outcome in *KMT2A*r disease [31]; *FLT3*-ITD and *WT1* mutations mark menin-inhibitor resistance [27]. The one rationally designed combination against RAS-co-mutant disease reduced tumor burden without extending survival [31].
6. **Venetoclax exposure appears to erode subsequent menin-inhibitor benefit.** With venetoclax/azacitidine established frontline for unfit patients, most R/R patients reaching a menin inhibitor are already venetoclax-exposed (75% in AUGMENT-101 *NPM1*m [13]; 55% in KOMET-007 [34]) — the subgroup with CRc 24% rather than 70% [34]. The class may therefore be being tested in the sequence least favorable to it.
7. **Transplant, the only established consolidation, is reached by a minority.** HSCT rates after menin monotherapy were 23% (*KMT2A*r), 11% (*NPM1*m) and 3.6% (ziftomenib cohort) [16,20]. Without transplant, a 5-month remission is a delay, not a cure.
8. **The *KMT2A*r population is heterogeneous in ways the trials have not resolved.** Fusion partner (KMT2A::MLLT4 worse [57]), *EVI1* co-expression, therapy-related versus de novo origin (median OS 0.7 vs 1.4 years [42]), and lineage (AML vs ALL vs MPAL, with CR+CRh 21% vs 19% vs 50% [16]) all modify outcome, and KMT2A-PTD was excluded from registration altogether [16,74].
9. **Evidence for cure-relevant endpoints is structurally absent.** Every reported efficacy figure in this domain comes from a single-arm study with a response primary endpoint. Median follow-up in the FDA-reviewed registration cohorts was 5.7 months (*KMT2A*r) and 3.8 months (*NPM1*m) [16] — too short to say anything about long-term disease control.

## Open research questions

1. Does adding a menin inhibitor to intensive induction improve event-free survival in newly diagnosed *NPM1*-mutated AML? (KOMET-017 intensive study and REVEAL-ND *NPM1* are powered for this; first data are not expected before 2029 [58].)
2. Does adding a menin inhibitor to venetoclax/azacitidine improve overall survival — not just CR rate — in intensive-chemotherapy-ineligible *NPM1*m/*KMT2A*r AML? (KOMET-017 non-intensive study, cAMeLot-2, NCT06652438 [58].)
3. Is menin inhibition more effective given before venetoclax than after it? A randomized sequencing trial is the only way to separate the KOMET-007 venetoclax-exposure effect [34] from confounding by prior-line number and clonal evolution.
4. Can a menin inhibitor with a distinct binding mode reproducibly salvage *MEN1*-mutant relapse? A prospective, genotype-directed switching trial stratified by specific *MEN1* allele (M327I/G331D versus T349M/G331R) is the direct test of the structural predictions in [22,23,26].
5. Can cell-free DNA surveillance for emergent *MEN1* mutations [30] trigger a pre-emptive therapy change before morphologic relapse, and does acting on that signal improve survival?
6. What is the mechanism of resistance in the majority of relapses that do **not** carry a *MEN1* mutation, and in the primary non-responders — the ~35–65% who never achieve any response [12,13,18]?
7. Which non-*MEN1* combination partner actually extends survival? The selumetinib result [31] argues that synergy on burden is not sufficient; candidate partners with mechanistic rationale but no survival data include FLT3 inhibitors [23], CDK6 inhibitors in *NUP98*r [55], RARα agonists in *RARA*-high disease [56], SETD2 inhibitors [not verified in this file], and T-cell-directed therapy exploiting menin-inhibitor-induced MHC upregulation [24].
8. Can differentiation syndrome be predicted and pre-empted? A validated plasma biomarker panel [51] plus a randomized comparison of prophylactic versus reactive corticosteroids would allow dose intensification in the *KMT2A*r AML and MPAL subsets where incidence is 33% [16].
9. Is the *KMT2A*r differentiation-syndrome mortality seen with ziftomenib (4/39) [20] an agent-specific property or a dose/lead-in problem? DS-1594b's experience — differentiation syndrome at high doses, none at 20 mg/day, mitigated by ramp-up dosing [35] — suggests schedule is at least part of the answer, and this is directly testable.
10. Does post-transplant menin-inhibitor maintenance reduce relapse in high-risk *KMT2A*r or *NPM1*m disease? Existing evidence is case-series level [48]; several phase 1/2 maintenance trials are open [58].
11. Should menin inhibition be extended to *NUP98*r, *UBTF*-TD, and KMT2A-PTD AML, and what is the minimum evidence required to do so in diseases too rare for a randomized trial? The shortest response duration in SAVE was in *NUP98*r (5.9 months) [33], which argues against assuming equivalence.
12. Can menin inhibition prevent AML in high-risk clonal hematopoiesis? Reversal of aberrant self-renewal in pre-leukemic *Npm1c/Dnmt3a* mice [7] is the only supporting evidence and remains a **hypothesis**; the toxicity profile [16,20] currently precludes a prevention trial.
13. Does menin dependency in normal megakaryopoiesis [53] set a floor on achievable dose intensity in combination regimens, and does it open a separate indication in myelofibrosis?
14. What is the right MRD endpoint after differentiation therapy? Given immunophenotypic switching in 52% of patients [49] and survival stratification by MRD status (23.6 vs 20.8 vs 3.2 months [49]), molecular *NPM1* MRD and flow MRD may not be interchangeable in this setting.

### Evidence-trail notes

- **Numbering.** Inline markers [1]–[76] correspond to the numbered list above. References [65]–[69] and [75]–[76] are narrative reviews retrieved during this search and used only for background framing, not for any numeric claim in the tables.
- **Where the evidence trail goes cold.** No randomized phase 3 result for any menin inhibitor in any setting was located as of 2026-09-09; the earliest primary completion date among the five phase 3 trials is June 2029 [58]. No phase 2 monotherapy result for bleximenib (cAMeLot-1) was located. No peer-reviewed publication of the enzomenib Horizen-1 phase 1/2 efficacy data was located — the figures quoted for enzomenib come from a retrieved review of ASH 2025 abstracts [40], not from the abstracts. No EMA authorization record for either approved menin inhibitor was retrieved; only FDA records were verified [14,15,19]. ASH 2026 had not occurred at the time of writing, so no 2026 ASH data exist; EHA 2026 material is represented here only through the SAVE publication [33] which was published simultaneously with that congress.
- **Marked unverified.** AUGMENT-102 efficacy figures (composite CR ~50–56% across two dose levels in a predominantly pediatric R/R population) appeared only in a search-engine summary of secondary oncology news coverage and are recorded as **[unverified]**; the trial's existence, phase, enrollment (N=30 actual) and completion date are verified from the ClinicalTrials.gov record [61]. The SETD2-inhibitor combination mentioned in open question 7 was noted during searching but is not cited, and is flagged as **not verified in this file**.
- **Discrepancies left visible rather than resolved.** (i) AUGMENT-101 *KMT2A*r CR+CRh is 22.8% in 57 centrally confirmed efficacy-evaluable patients [12] and 21.2% in 104 treated patients in the FDA-reviewed population [16]; a secondary review reports 22.7% in 97 patients [38]. All three denominators differ. (ii) Ziftomenib median OS in KOMET-001 phase 2 is 6.6 months in the JCO primary [18] and 6.1 months in a secondary review [38]. (iii) Ziftomenib phase 2 MRD negativity is 61% in the primary [18] and 63–64% in secondary accounts [38,40]. (iv) SAVE is reported with N=42 in the JCO publication [33] and N=33 in earlier secondary coverage of the same study — the primary figures are used throughout. (v) Median duration of CR+CRh for revumenib in *KMT2A*r disease is 6.4 months in the label [16] and is not reported in the JCO primary [12].
- **Regulatory data provenance.** Approval dates, indications, efficacy tables, boxed warnings, differentiation-syndrome and QTc incidence, and dosing in sections 2 and 3 come from the FDA approval notices [14,15,19] and from the current US prescribing information for each drug retrieved through the openFDA drug label API [16,20], not from secondary coverage.
- **Cross-links.** *NPM1* and *KMT2A* biology, HOX/MEIS1 circuitry and co-mutation patterns are treated in [`02-disease-biology-genomics.md`](02-disease-biology-genomics.md); risk classification of *NPM1*m and *KMT2A*r disease in [`01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md`](01-epidemiology-classification-risk.md); the intensive backbone into which menin inhibitors are being added in [`03-intensive-chemotherapy-backbone.md`](03-intensive-chemotherapy-backbone.md). MRD methodology, transplant, and venetoclax-based lower-intensity therapy are covered in their own domain files and appear here only where they bear directly on menin inhibition.
