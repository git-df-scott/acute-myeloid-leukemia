# Venetoclax, BCL-2 Family Targeting, and Lower-Intensity Therapy

> Venetoclax is the single largest therapeutic advance in AML since anthracycline–cytarabine induction, and it is the clearest demonstration that AML has an exploitable, druggable dependency — apoptotic priming through BCL-2. It converted a population previously offered palliation into one with a ~15-month median survival and a ~37% two-year survival [6,7], and it is now the backbone into which almost every new AML agent is being slotted. But venetoclax also defines the shape of the field's central problem: it produces remissions in roughly two-thirds of newly diagnosed unfit patients and cures almost none of them. Remissions are deep (41% of responders reach MRD <10⁻³ [8]) yet finite; relapse is driven by biology venetoclax does not touch — MCL-1 and BCL-xL dependence, monocytic and erythroid differentiation states, BAX loss, TP53 disruption, RAS/FLT3 signalling, and metabolic rewiring toward OXPHOS. Every attempt so far to close that gap by *adding* a third agent to venetoclax + hypomethylating agent (HMA) has either produced single-arm signals awaiting randomisation, or, when actually randomised, failed (ENHANCE-3, PEVENAZA) [38,55]. This domain therefore matters twice over: it is where most patients with AML are actually treated, and it is where the mechanistic reasons AML is not yet curable are most legible.

## Key takeaways

- **VIALE-A is the anchor and its ceiling is well characterised.** Azacitidine + venetoclax vs azacitidine + placebo in 431 patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy: median OS 14.7 vs 9.6 months (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.52–0.85, p<0.001); CR 36.7% vs 17.9%; CR+CRi 66.4% vs 28.3% [6]. At final analysis (43.2 months median follow-up, all 360 prespecified OS events) HR was 0.58 (0.47–0.72) and estimated 24-month OS 37.5% vs 16.9% [7]. **Phase 3 positive; FDA approved.**
- **VIALE-C missed its primary endpoint.** Venetoclax + low-dose cytarabine (LDAC) vs LDAC in 211 patients: planned primary OS analysis HR 0.75 (0.52–1.07), p=0.11 — *not statistically significant*; median OS 7.2 vs 4.1 months. An unplanned analysis after 6 further months of follow-up gave HR 0.70 (0.50–0.98), p=0.04, median OS 8.4 months [9,10]. Venetoclax + LDAC is approved but is the weaker of the two backbones and is rarely used where an HMA is available [23].
- **The fully oral regimen is now approved.** FDA approved decitabine/cedazuridine (Inqovi) **in combination with venetoclax** on **13 May 2026** for newly diagnosed AML in adults ≥75 years or unfit for intensive induction, on the single-arm ASTX727-07 trial (NCT04657081, N=101): CR 41.6% (95% CI 31.9–51.8), median time to CR 2 months, median duration of remission not reached [16,17]. Note the asymmetry: the *Venclexta* label still names only azacitidine, IV decitabine, or LDAC — the oral-HMA combination lives on the Inqovi label [15,17].
- **The first randomised test of venetoclax in *fit* patients favoured the lower-intensity arm.** PARADIGM (NCT04801797), phase 2, 172 transplant-eligible, FLT3-wild-type, intermediate/adverse-risk adults (median age 64; 51% <65): median EFS 14.5 vs 6.2 months for azacitidine+venetoclax vs intensive induction (HR 0.57, p=0.0021); ORR 88% vs 62%; transplant rate 61% vs 40%; median OS 21.5 vs 18.6 months, **not significant** (p=0.1873) [41,42]. **Phase 2 randomised, positive for EFS only — not practice-defining on survival.**
- **Deintensification failed its first prospective test.** OPTI-AML (within Beat AML, NCT03013998), randomised phase 2, 169 patients ≥60: CR within two cycles 49.4% (28-day venetoclax, n=83) vs 43% (14-day, n=86); difference 6.4% (90% CI −6.1% to 19.0%) — **did not meet non-inferiority**. Composite CR 80.7% vs 68.6%; MRD negativity similar (77.6% vs 76.5%); grade ≥3 AEs and early mortality similar. NPM1/IDH2-mutated patients did notably better with 28 days (CR 60.9% vs 33.3%) [70].
- **Randomised triplets have so far not worked.** PEVENAZA (NCT04266795): pevonedistat+azacitidine+venetoclax (n=83) vs azacitidine+venetoclax (n=81) — EFS HR 0.99 (0.61–1.60), OS HR 1.42 (0.82–2.49) [55]. ENHANCE-3 (NCT05079230): magrolimab vs placebo added to venetoclax+azacitidine, 378 randomised, **stopped for futility**; median OS 10.7 vs 14.1 months (HR 1.178, 0.848–1.637); fatal AEs 19.0% vs 11.4%, grade 5 infections 11.1% vs 6.5% [38]. **Both phase 3/randomised negative.**
- **Single-arm triplets look excellent and remain unproven.** Azacitidine+venetoclax+gilteritinib in newly diagnosed FLT3-mutated AML (N=30): CR/CRi 96%, median RFS 23.4 and OS 29.7 months, 3-year OS 46% [31]. Azacitidine+venetoclax+revumenib in NPM1m/KMT2Ar AML ≥60 (N=43): ORR 88.4%, CRc 81.4%, CR 67.4%, MRD-negative in 37/37 evaluable [33]. **Phase 1–2 signals only; no randomised comparison against the doublet exists.**
- **The HMA schedule may matter as much as the third agent, and has never been randomised.** A 335-patient five-centre retrospective comparison found 10-day decitabine + venetoclax versus standard 5-day decitabine or 7-day azacitidine + venetoclax gave CRc 82% vs 70% (p=0.029) and median OS 22.2 vs 9.1 months (p<0.001) — but also transplant rates of 25% vs 6% (p<0.001), which is a plausible complete explanation for the survival gap [22]. **Retrospective; the authors themselves call for randomised evaluation.**
- **Venetoclax is not the best option in every molecular subgroup.** In a 280-patient real-world comparison confined to IDH1-mutated newly diagnosed AML, ivosidenib + HMA beat venetoclax + HMA on CR (42.5% vs 26.3%, p=0.007), composite CR (63.0% vs 48.5%, p=0.019) and 6-month EFS (55.8% vs 38.4%, p=0.006), with less grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia; the authors conclude ivosidenib + azacitidine should be the preferred standard of care there [37]. **Retrospective, but directionally consistent with phase 3 AGILE.**
- **Venetoclax exposure has a specific prognostic geography.** Response and survival track NPM1, IDH2 and DDX41 (favourable) against TP53, FLT3-ITD and RUNX1 (unfavourable) [90]; the ELN 2024 less-intensive-therapy classification formalises higher/intermediate/lower benefit groups [94]. TP53-mutated AML derives no meaningful survival benefit from any current frontline option (median OS ~6.2 months with VEN+HMA, ~6.5 with intensive chemotherapy, ~6.1 with HMA alone) [95].
- **Resistance is mostly not BCL-2 mutation.** It is compensatory MCL-1/BCL-xL/BCL2A1 dependence, monocytic differentiation with loss of BCL2 expression [51], acquired inactivating **BAX** mutations in 17% of post-venetoclax relapses [60], biallelic TP53 loss, and FLT3/RAS pathway activation — including *non-mutational* RAS transcriptional activation coupled to monocytic differentiation [59]. Metabolic escape via nicotinamide metabolism and OXPHOS restoration is a distinct axis [56].
- **The MCL-1 arm of the strategy has essentially failed clinically.** Every clinical-stage MCL-1 inhibitor has been terminated or produced no responses, with cardiac toxicity as the recurring signal: AZD5991 ± venetoclax — asymptomatic troponin I/T elevation in 8/78 (10.3%) prospectively and 54/65 post hoc, 3 objective responses in 78 patients, trial terminated [62]; S64315 monotherapy in 38 myeloid patients — cardiac-biomarker and ejection-fraction DLTs, **no objective responses** [63]; AMG 397 terminated after 24 patients, registry reason "Strategic decisions" [66] (trade press attributes an FDA clinical hold for cardiac safety — **[unverified]**, see Evidence-trail notes). Class-wide cardiotoxicity is now stated as such in the reviews [3] and [64].
- **Venetoclax + azacitidine as *maintenance* has failed twice.** VIALE-T (post-allograft, NCT04161885) was terminated for "strategic considerations" after 465 enrolled; posted Part 2 results give a stratified OS hazard ratio of **1.2 (95% CI 0.81–1.77)** — numerically favouring best supportive care [45]. VIALE-M (post-chemotherapy maintenance, NCT04102020) had its randomised Parts 2 and 3 removed and completed as a 112-patient dose-finding study; no phase 3 relapse-free-survival result exists [46].
- **The adjacent disease tells against reflexive combination.** VERONA (NCT04401748), phase 3 in newly diagnosed higher-risk MDS, **missed OS**: HR 0.908, stratified log-rank p=0.3772 (median OS 22.18 vs 21.68 months at 41.2 months' follow-up), despite modified ORR 76.2% vs 57.7% [47]. A 1,907-patient registry analysis reproduced the pattern: HMA+venetoclax improved composite CR (48.8% vs 27.7%) but not OS (HR 0.83, 0.64–1.07) [49]. **Response rate is not a survival surrogate for venetoclax combinations.**
- **Real-world survival is consistently below VIALE-A.** 204 patients ≥75 at eight US academic centres: CR/CRi 64%, median OS 9.5 months, 30-/60-day mortality 9%/19%, post-relapse OS 3.4 months [91]. A 209-patient Dutch nationwide cohort: composite CR 69%, median OS 19.1 months overall but 14.7 months in non-transplanted patients, 3-month mortality 15% [73]. One community series found *no* survival benefit from adding venetoclax to azacitidine (median OS 14 vs 18 months, p=0.905) with markedly worse cytopenias [92]. Only ~66% of unselected unfit patients would have met VIALE-A eligibility [see Evidence-trail notes].

## 1. Mechanism: what venetoclax does, and what it does not do

Venetoclax (ABT-199) is an orally bioavailable, selective BH3 mimetic that occupies the BH3-binding groove of BCL-2, displacing sequestered activator BH3-only proteins (BIM, BID) and pro-apoptotic effectors, thereby licensing BAX/BAK-dependent mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilisation and caspase activation [1]. The wider regulatory architecture of the family — transcriptional control by NF-κB, STAT3/5 and HIF-1α, alternative splicing, microRNAs, and post-translational stability control — is reviewed comprehensively elsewhere [2] and matters here because it is the substrate on which non-mutational resistance operates [3]. Venetoclax was engineered specifically to spare BCL-xL, because the earlier dual BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitor navitoclax caused dose-limiting, on-target thrombocytopenia [1]. That design choice is the origin of both venetoclax's tolerability and its resistance profile: it leaves MCL-1, BCL-xL and BCL2A1 untouched, and any cell whose survival is routed through those proteins is intrinsically insensitive [57].

Historically, this domain begins with the phase 1b study of venetoclax with decitabine or azacitidine in 57 previously untreated patients aged ≥65: the maximum tolerated dose was never reached, the recommended phase 2 dose was 400 mg daily (or 800 mg on an interrupted schedule), CR+CRi was 61% (35/57), and — notably, given the CLL experience — no tumour lysis syndrome was observed [5]. A dedicated substudy co-administered posaconazole to characterise the CYP3A interaction that now governs most real-world dosing decisions [5].

Two mechanistic facts explain why the HMA partner matters. First, azacitidine + venetoclax in patients disrupts the TCA cycle (decreased α-ketoglutarate, increased succinate, consistent with complex II inhibition via reduced glutathionylation of succinate dehydrogenase), suppressing oxidative phosphorylation and selectively eradicating leukaemia stem cells (LSCs) — an effect shown in patient-derived material rather than only in cell lines [4]. Second, LSC elimination, not bulk blast clearance, is what tracks with clinical outcome: in a prospective cohort, refractory patients' LSCs showed perturbed apoptotic dependencies, and a flow-cytometry "mediators of apoptosis combinatorial" (MAC) score built from the BCL2 : BCL-xL : MCL1 protein ratio *within LSCs* predicted initial response with >97% positive predictive value [53]. **Evidence tier: mechanistic/translational, prospectively correlated with clinical response in a single-institution validation cohort — not a validated clinical assay.**

### Regulatory status, precisely
- **21 November 2018** — FDA *accelerated* approval, venetoclax + azacitidine or decitabine or LDAC, newly diagnosed AML, adults ≥75 years or with comorbidities precluding intensive induction; based on CR rate and remission duration in M14-358 and M14-387 [13].
- **16 October 2020** — efficacy supplements 20 and 21 to NDA 208573 approved, converting to regular approval on VIALE-A/VIALE-C [14].
- **13 May 2026** — Inqovi (decitabine/cedazuridine) approved *in combination with venetoclax* for the same population, on ASTX727-07 (N=101, CR 41.6%) [16].
- Current labels: Venclexta (effective 2026-05-20) — venetoclax 400 mg daily with azacitidine or decitabine after a 3-day ramp-up (100 → 200 → 400 mg), or 600 mg daily with LDAC after a 4-day ramp-up (100 → 200 → 400 → 600 mg), continued until progression or unacceptable toxicity [15]. Inqovi (effective 2026-05-25) — one tablet (35 mg decitabine / 100 mg cedazuridine) daily on days 1–5 of each 28-day cycle, taken 2 hours apart from venetoclax [17].

## 3. HMA backbone variants: azacitidine, IV decitabine, 10-day decitabine, oral decitabine/cedazuridine

There is **no randomised comparison of azacitidine + venetoclax against decitabine + venetoclax**, and no randomised comparison of venetoclax + HMA against venetoclax + LDAC. Choices rest on single-arm and retrospective data.

Two observations deserve emphasis. First, the MD Anderson CLAD/LDAC/VEN-alternating-with-AZA/VEN programme reports a median OS of 52 months in a cohort with 64% ELN-2022 adverse risk [25] — a number roughly 3.5× VIALE-A. Whether that reflects the regimen, patient selection at a single high-volume centre (median age 68, only 13% ≥75), a 44% transplant rate, or all three, cannot be resolved without randomisation. Second, VIALE-C's approved venetoclax dose with LDAC is 600 mg, not 400 mg [15] — a detail routinely lost when the two backbones are discussed interchangeably.

The oral regimen's significance was recognised immediately on publication as removing the parenteral-visit requirement that dominates the treatment burden of this population [19], and that argument — not a superior efficacy signal — is the case for it. Venetoclax has also been combined with CPX-351 in the relapsed/refractory setting: in 33 heavily pre-treated patients (58% prior venetoclax, 44% second salvage or later, 30% prior transplant), ORR was 46% (95% CI 30–62) and CRc 39% (25–56), rising to CRc 70% (40–89) in first-salvage TP53-wild-type patients with 71% undetectable MRD; 30- and 60-day mortality were 9% and 21%, and prolonged myelosuppression forced two dose de-escalations before a recommended phase 2 dose was found [28]. Taken with the newly diagnosed high-risk study [27] and the unfit phase 1b [26], the CPX-351 backbone has now been tested across all three settings without a randomised comparator in any of them — and in all three, myelosuppression rather than efficacy set the dose.

## 4. Triplets: the frontier, and what it has actually shown

Triplet therapy adds a targeted agent to venetoclax + HMA. The rationale is mechanistically specific: relapses after venetoclax + HMA in FLT3-mutated AML are commonly driven by expansion of the FLT3-mutated clone [31]; NPM1m and KMT2Ar AML carry a menin dependency that BCL-2 inhibition does not address; IDH1 inhibition and BCL-2 inhibition converge on the same apoptotic threshold. A practical-management synthesis for these regimens exists and is worth reading before designing one, because myelosuppression — not response rate — is the binding constraint [30].

## 5. Venetoclax resistance

### 5.1 Compensatory anti-apoptotic dependence
Resistance to venetoclax most often does not involve BCL-2 itself. Instead, cells shift survival dependence to MCL-1, BCL-xL, or BCL2A1/BFL-1, which continue to sequester BH3-only proteins and prevent BAX/BAK activation, mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilisation, and apoptosis [57]. These switches are described as both compensatory (upregulation under drug pressure) and lineage-associated (fixed by the cell's differentiation state) [3]. This is the mechanistic basis for MCL-1 and BCL-xL co-targeting (section 6) and for the MAC score biomarker [53].

### 5.2 Differentiation state — and a genuine controversy
Pei et al. showed that response to venetoclax + azacitidine tracks developmental stage: phenotypically primitive AML is sensitive, monocytic AML is resistant; resistant monocytic AML has a distinct transcriptome, **loses BCL2 expression**, and depends on MCL1 to sustain OXPHOS. Monocytic subpopulations are selectively enriched at relapse [51]. Kuusanmäki et al. independently linked venetoclax response to differentiation stage in a phenotype-based drug screen [52].

**But this is contested at the clinical level.** Waclawiczek et al. found that although cultured monocytic AML cells were resistant upfront, **monocytic differentiation was not clinically predictive in their patient cohort**; what predicted outcome was LSC elimination and combinatorial BCL2/BCL-xL/MCL1 protein levels within LSCs [53]. A single-centre real-world study reported comparably poor outcomes for myelomonocytic and monocytic AML with venetoclax-based therapy [see Evidence-trail notes]. The honest reading: differentiation state is a robust *biological* determinant of BH3-mimetic sensitivity and an unreliable *clinical* biomarker as currently measured.

### 5.3 Effector-level and genetic resistance
- **BAX.** Acquired inactivating missense or frameshift/nonsense *BAX* mutations were found in **17%** of patients relapsing after venetoclax-based therapy for AML, and were rare after genotoxic chemotherapy. Variants arose in leukaemic or preleukaemic compartments, were competitively selected in vitro under prolonged BH3-mimetic exposure, and rendered cells resistant to BCL-2 targeting while chemotherapy sensitivity was variable [60]. This is a *class* liability: no BCL-2, MCL-1 or BCL-xL inhibitor can function without BAX.
- **TP53.** Biallelic TP53 perturbation is enriched in both primary and adaptive resistance [54]; TP53-mutant AML has median OS ~6 months regardless of frontline strategy [95]; and complex karyotype and/or TP53 mutation defined the poorest VIALE-C subgroup (CR/CRi 25%, mOS 3.48 months) [11].
- **BCL2 mutations.** Venetoclax-binding-site *BCL2* variants (including G101V, well characterised in CLL) impair drug binding; sonrotoclax (BGB-11417) was designed to retain potency against G101V and does so in preclinical models [68]. **Preclinical for AML; no AML clinical efficacy data located.**

### 5.4 Signalling: FLT3 and RAS
Primary and adaptive resistance to frontline venetoclax combinations was most commonly characterised by acquisition or enrichment of clones activating FLT3 or RAS signalling, or biallelically perturbing TP53; single-cell studies showed polyclonal resistance and rapid intra-cycle clonal selection. Functionally, FLT3-ITD gain or TP53 loss conferred cross-resistance to both venetoclax and cytotoxic therapy [54].

Multiomic single-cell profiling of the venetoclax + gilteritinib phase 1b cohort extended this decisively: the combination effectively eliminated FLT3-mutant clones, but resistance arose through RAS activation by **three convergent routes** — selection of RAS-mutant clones, *non-mutational* upregulation of RAS transcriptional programmes, and a shift to RAS-associated monocytic differentiation. RAS pathway inhibition re-sensitised cells in an in vitro monocytic-differentiation model [59]. This links section 5.2 and 5.4 into one axis and identifies RAS pathway inhibition as a testable clinical strategy. **Evidence tier: translational, with in vitro rescue; no clinical trial of RAS-pathway inhibition added to venetoclax located.**

### 5.5 Metabolic and epigenetic adaptation
- **Nicotinamide metabolism.** LSCs from *relapsed/refractory* patients showed elevated nicotinamide metabolism, which activates both amino-acid metabolism and fatty-acid oxidation to drive OXPHOS and circumvent venetoclax + azacitidine. NAMPT inhibition selectively eradicated R/R LSCs while sparing normal HSPCs [56]. **Preclinical/translational; NAMPT inhibitors have not shown a clinical AML signal in the literature located here.**
- **Broader metabolic escape.** Enhanced amino-acid uptake, fatty-acid oxidation, mitochondrial structural remodelling and the integrated stress response are listed as putative but **not yet validated** resistance mechanisms in the most recent expert synthesis [57] — an explicit statement of uncertainty worth preserving rather than smoothing over.
- **m6A and chromatin.** The m6A writer RBM15 and the NuRD-complex interactor ZMYND8 were identified as mediators of resistance to both venetoclax and the dual BCL2/BCL2L1 inhibitor AZD4320; loss of either induced resistance alongside altered BCL2-family expression and monocytic differentiation [58]. **Preclinical.**

A recent synthesis of HMA/venetoclax-refractory AML organises these mechanisms into genetic (TP53, FLT3-ITD, RAS, BAX, BCL2 variants) and non-genetic (MCL1/BCL-xL upregulation, monocytic and erythroid/megakaryocytic differentiation, metabolic reprogramming, interferon-γ signalling) categories and is the most useful single map of the field [101].

### 5.6 What happens after venetoclax fails
Outcomes are dismal and this is the field's largest unmet need. Among 204 real-world patients ≥75 treated with HMA+venetoclax, 59% of responders relapsed, with post-relapse median OS **3.4 months** [91]. In a heavily pre-treated R/R cohort failing venetoclax-based salvage (n=28, median 3 prior lines, 57% prior allograft), ORR to any post-venetoclax salvage was 23% and median OS **3.9 months** (4.3 months with salvage vs 1.3 months with palliative care alone) [61]. Prior venetoclax exposure also degrades response to subsequent targeted therapy: in KOMET-007, ziftomenib + venetoclax/azacitidine achieved CRc 70% in venetoclax-naïve versus **24%** in venetoclax-exposed R/R NPM1m patients [34]. Menin-inhibitor failure is likewise a poor-prognosis state [69]. Secondary AML with prior HMA exposure is a distinct, worse population whose frontline choice materially affects outcome [96].

The preclinical rationale for MCL-1 co-targeting in AML was laid out in detail before any of these trials read out [65], which makes the uniformity of the clinical failure more informative rather than less. Two syntheses now state MCL-1 cardiotoxicity as a class problem rather than a compound problem: MCL-1 is essential for cardiomyocyte mitochondrial homeostasis, and "MCL-1 inhibitors face class-wide cardiac toxicity" [3]; the multiple-myeloma review documents six MCL-1 inhibitors entering clinical trials across six sponsors with adverse effects, "and particularly cardiotoxicity," presenting a significant barrier to clinical use [64]. Whether a therapeutic index exists at all — for instance via tumour-selective delivery (PROTACs, antibody–drug conjugates) or intermittent dosing — is unresolved [3].

The BCL-xL arm has a cleaner problem: on-target thrombocytopenia [1,3]. The navitoclax + venetoclax + decitabine phase 1 result is notable precisely because bleeding did *not* materialise at the doses used and because responders were those with dual BCL-2 *and* BCL-xL myeloblast dependence — the first prospective hint that a dependency-guided BH3-mimetic doublet is feasible [67].

## 7. Dosing, duration, deintensification, and drug interactions

The approved regimen is venetoclax 400 mg daily, continuously, until progression [15]. In practice this is rarely delivered as written: prolonged cytopenias force interruptions, and 68% of patients receiving ≥1 consolidation cycle of a gilteritinib triplet needed a dose or duration reduction of at least one drug [31].

- **OPTI-AML settled the first prospective question in the negative.** A 14-day venetoclax schedule with azacitidine for cycles 1–2 did not meet non-inferiority against 28 days on CR within two cycles (43% vs 49.4%; difference 6.4%, 90% CI −6.1% to 19.0%) in 169 genomically unselected patients ≥60. Composite CR was 80.7% vs 68.6%; MRD negativity was comparable; grade ≥3 AEs, count recovery after cycle 2, and early mortality were similar. NPM1/IDH2-mutated patients did clearly better with 28 days (CR 60.9% vs 33.3%), other subgroups equivalently (~45%). The authors are careful: because the confidence interval crossed zero, the CR rates were not *significantly different* either — this is a failed non-inferiority trial, not a demonstrated inferiority [70].
- **Higher-dose venetoclax did not help.** HiDDAV (NCT03466294, N=42) gave venetoclax 600 mg with azacitidine for induction. Toxicity was similar to 400 mg but there was no discernible clinical improvement (ORR 66.7%; DoR 12.9, EFS 7.8, OS 9.8 months). MRD-guided azacitidine discontinuation did not improve DoR, EFS or OS versus discontinuation without MRD guidance [71].
- **Retrospective and real-world data point the other way, and this discordance is unresolved.** In a 108-patient real-world Slovak cohort there was no significant difference in remission rate or OS by venetoclax duration (<14 vs 14 vs 21 vs 28 days) [74]. In the Dutch nationwide cohort, follow-up cycles were commonly initiated with 14 days (55%) or 7 days (15%) of venetoclax, and outcomes were encouraging (median OS 19.1 months) while realising "considerable VEN savings through cycle individualisation and strategic usage of drug–drug interactions" [73]. Retrospective abbreviation studies are confounded by the fact that dose reduction is triggered *by* response and by cytopenias.
- **A large real-world dataset argues the other way on abbreviation.** In 99 IDH1-mutated patients treated with venetoclax + HMA, most received well under 28 days of venetoclax per cycle — apparently in anticipation of toxicity — and outcomes were *proportionately worse with fewer days of exposure per cycle*; the same study found ivosidenib + HMA superior on CR, composite CR, time to response and 6-month EFS [37]. Dose–response for venetoclax duration therefore cannot be dismissed, and OPTI-AML's NPM1/IDH2 subgroup finding [70] points the same way.
- **Stopping therapy in remission may be viable.** Among 62 newly diagnosed patients who discontinued venetoclax ± azacitidine for poor tolerance, median OS was 44 months and median treatment-free survival 16 months; patients who stopped both drugs and those who continued azacitidine alone had the same outcomes; MRD negativity was associated with a 2-year treatment-free survival of 80% [72]. CAVEAT similarly reported that almost one-third of patients had not relapsed, many with prolonged treatment-free remission (median 17.9 months) [83]; the original dose-escalation report established that venetoclax could be given at 50–600 mg for up to 14 days alongside cytarabine and idarubicin in patients ≥65 [82]. **All retrospective or single-arm; no randomised discontinuation trial located.**
- **Pharmacological boosting is a genuinely different lever.** In HOVON 171 (NCT06014489), adding the CYP3A4 inhibitor cobicistat allowed venetoclax to be reduced to **50 mg** — an eightfold reduction — while achieving *higher* exposure than standard 400 mg (AUC₀–₂₄ geometric mean ratio 2.0, 90% CI 1.4–2.8; Cmax GMR 1.4, 1.0–2.0) in 13 evaluable patients; CR/CRi was 65% in the intention-to-treat population; cobicistat also reduced inter-patient exposure variability arising from CYP3A4 polymorphisms and enhanced antileukaemic activity in vitro [75]. **Phase 2 pharmacokinetic endpoint met; efficacy not powered.** This has direct implications for access in cost-constrained settings.
- **Azole interactions are the commonest real-world dose determinant, and the label guidance may be wrong.** Prospective pharmacokinetic evaluation of venetoclax in AML "supports re-evaluation of recommended dose adjustments with azole antifungals" [78]. A real-world analysis of azole prophylaxis in venetoclax-treated newly diagnosed AML examined its effect on outcomes [79]; in the Dutch cohort antifungals were used in 72% of first cycles and were independently associated with **lower** mortality (multivariable HR 0.605, p=0.010), while also driving venetoclax dose reductions [73]. CAVEAT prospectively explored adjusted-dose venetoclax (50 mg and 100 mg) with posaconazole [83].

## 8. Infection and tumour lysis: the toxicity that actually limits venetoclax

Infection, not tumour lysis, is the dominant hazard. In VIALE-A, any-grade infections occurred in 85% of the venetoclax arm vs 67% of controls, grade ≥3 febrile neutropenia in 42% vs 19% [6]. In ENHANCE-3, the excess mortality from adding magrolimab was driven principally by grade 5 infections (11.1% vs 6.5%) [38].

- **Invasive fungal infection (IFI) risk is moderate and front-loaded.** In 144 newly diagnosed patients on VEN/AZA, 25 (17%) developed IFI by EORTC/MSGERC criteria but only 5.6% were "proven" or "probable"; the incidence rate per 1,000 patient-days was highest at days 0–9 (8.39) and fell thereafter; none of the 10 patients on antifungal prophylaxis developed IFI [76]. In a 235-patient multicentre cohort, probable/confirmed IFI was 5.1%, with no significant difference by age, disease status, or prophylaxis use — and **50% of the IFI cases occurred *on* prophylaxis**. Bacterial infection occurred in 33.6% and 54% had at least one febrile-neutropenia admission [77]. The two studies agree on the ~5% proven/probable IFI rate and disagree on whether prophylaxis works; neither is randomised.
- **Tumour lysis syndrome is largely a laboratory event with the AML ramp-up.** In 100 consecutive AML patients on venetoclax + HMA, 34 had laboratory Cairo–Bishop TLS, 8 met clinical Cairo–Bishop criteria, but only 6 met Howard criteria; 14 had spontaneous TLS; 92/100 started with WBC <25 ×10⁹/L. Aggressive prophylaxis was uncommon and the authors judge it "likely unnecessary for most patients at low risk" [80]. In a real-world cohort, TLS occurred in 4.5% [74]. Whether the ramp-up is needed at all has been examined in a cohort treated with and without it [81]. The 3-day AML ramp-up is far shorter than the 5-week CLL ramp-up for this reason [15].

PARADIGM is the pivotal datum and must be read carefully. The registry record confirms the design as a phase 2 randomised study with event-free survival as the sole primary outcome, run by Massachusetts General Hospital with AbbVie as collaborator, still active and not recruiting [43]. It is a **randomised phase 2**, it excluded FLT3-mutated AML, its primary endpoint was EFS not OS, and OS was not significantly different. A separate report of the newly diagnosed FLAG-IDA + venetoclax cohort extends the follow-up of that regimen [85]. PARADIGM's most robust findings are arguably the process outcomes — more patients reached transplant, with fewer hospital days, less ICU use, and better quality-of-life scores [42]. Independent secondary coverage reports fewer grade ≥3 infections (28% vs 41%) and less severe bleeding (2% vs 12%) with no early deaths in the lower-intensity arm [see Evidence-trail notes]. That combination — equal survival with materially less morbidity and better access to transplant — is a real clinical argument, and it is not the same argument as "lower-intensity therapy is superior."

## 10. Transplant interface, maintenance, and adjacent settings

- **Bridge to transplant.** Venetoclax + azacitidine followed by allogeneic transplant produced good outcomes in patients >60 and appeared better than maintenance therapy in a single-centre comparison [98]. In the CLAD/LDAC/VEN programme, lower-intensity therapy compared favourably with intensive chemotherapy among patients proceeding to allograft [99]. In the Dutch nationwide cohort, transplant (18% of patients) carried HR 0.24 for death (p<0.001) [73]. **All non-randomised; selection for transplant is the dominant confounder.**
- **Maintenance after transplant fails.** VIALE-T (NCT04161885) randomised patients to venetoclax + azacitidine + best supportive care versus BSC alone after allogeneic SCT. It was **terminated for "strategic considerations"** with 465 patients enrolled; the posted results give a stratified OS hazard ratio of **1.2 (95% CI 0.81–1.77)** — that is, numerically favouring BSC — with median OS not estimable in either arm [45]. A separate phase 1 approach (venetoclax-augmented FluBu2 reduced-intensity conditioning followed by all-oral venetoclax/decitabine maintenance for poor-risk MDS/AML) has been reported [100]. **VIALE-T should be treated as a negative result for this indication, not as an inconclusive one.**
- **Maintenance after chemotherapy was never tested.** VIALE-M's randomised Parts 2 and 3 were removed; the study completed as a 112-patient, non-randomised, sequential dose-finding trial whose only primary outcomes are dose-limiting toxicities [46]. **There is no phase 3 relapse-free-survival result for venetoclax + oral azacitidine maintenance.**
- **Higher-risk MDS: negative.** VERONA (NCT04401748) missed OS (HR 0.908, p=0.3772) despite improved modified ORR (76.2% vs 57.7%); AbbVie stated the data "do not impact any current approved indications for venetoclax" [47]. The primary analysis was presented at SOHO 2025 [48]. The icMDS VALIDATE registry (1,907 patients; 134 HMA/VEN) reproduced the dissociation — composite CR 48.8% vs 27.7% (adjusted OR 2.49), OS HR 0.83 (0.64–1.07, p=0.15) — with an OS benefit confined to TP53-wild-type disease (HR 0.47, 0.29–0.74, p=0.002) and a trend in ≥10% marrow blasts (HR 0.73, p=0.06) [49].
- **Paediatric AML.** In 38 children with relapsed/refractory AML given compassionate-use venetoclax + azacitidine across four European countries, composite response was 26.3%, median PFS among responders 22 months, and overall cohort median OS 6.1 months; grade ≥3 cytopenias 94% and positive blood cultures 29% [97]. Response rates are substantially lower than in older adults. **Retrospective; the authors call for prospective paediatric trials.**

The earliest real-world series, published within a year of the phase 1b data, already reported response rates close to the trial estimates and was widely used to justify adoption ahead of VIALE-A [93]. The pattern since has been consistent: composite response rates in the real world approximate the trial (60–69%), while median OS is frequently 9–15 months rather than 14.7 months, and 60-day mortality reaches 19% in the oldest cohorts. Genotype explains much of the spread [90,94], eligibility drift explains more, and the community series [92] is a reminder that when venetoclax is delivered at reduced dose to a frailer population with worse cytogenetics, the benefit can disappear entirely.

## What is established vs contested

**Established**
1. Venetoclax + azacitidine improves overall survival versus azacitidine alone in adults ineligible for intensive chemotherapy; the effect persisted to a final analysis with all 360 prespecified events [6,7].
2. Venetoclax has FDA regular approval with azacitidine, decitabine, or LDAC for newly diagnosed AML in adults ≥75 years or unfit for intensive induction [13,14,15]; the fully oral decitabine/cedazuridine + venetoclax combination was approved 13 May 2026 [16,17].
3. VIALE-C did not meet its primary OS endpoint on the planned analysis [9].
4. Achieving CRc with MRD <10⁻³ identifies a distinct, much better-prognosis group after venetoclax + azacitidine (adjusted OS HR 0.285) [8].
5. Genotype strongly conditions benefit: NPM1, IDH1/IDH2 favourable; TP53, complex karyotype, FLT3-ITD, RUNX1 unfavourable [11,90,94,95].
6. Resistance is dominated by non-BCL2 mechanisms — alternate anti-apoptotic dependence, differentiation state, BAX loss, TP53 disruption, FLT3/RAS activation [51,54,57,59,60].
7. MCL-1 inhibitors as a class have failed to establish a therapeutic window in myeloid malignancy, with cardiac toxicity the recurring dose-limiting signal and no clinically meaningful monotherapy activity [3,62,63,64,66].
8. Adding magrolimab to venetoclax + azacitidine is harmful, not merely futile (fatal AEs 19.0% vs 11.4%) [38]; adding pevonedistat is futile [55].
9. Venetoclax + azacitidine maintenance after allogeneic transplant did not improve survival in the randomised portion of VIALE-T [45].
10. Adding venetoclax to azacitidine in higher-risk MDS improves response without improving survival [47,48,49].
11. Infection is the principal treatment-related hazard; the AML ramp-up carries a low rate of clinically significant TLS [6,76,77,80].
12. Post-venetoclax-failure survival is measured in months, and prior venetoclax exposure degrades response to subsequent targeted agents [34,61,91].

**Contested or unresolved**
1. Whether monocytic differentiation is a *clinically* actionable predictor. Pei and Kuusanmäki support it biologically [51,52]; Waclawiczek found it non-predictive in patients and proposed LSC BCL2-family ratios instead [53].
2. Whether abbreviated venetoclax is safe. OPTI-AML failed non-inferiority prospectively [70] and a 280-patient real-world dataset found worse outcomes with fewer venetoclax days per cycle [37]; against that, a nationwide Dutch cohort using 7- or 14-day continuation cycles reported encouraging survival [73] and a single-centre series found no difference across <14/14/21/28 days [74]. The confidence interval in OPTI-AML crossed zero, so neither position is excluded — but the two datasets that examined dose–response, rather than dose category, both found one.
3. Whether venetoclax + azacitidine should replace intensive induction in fit patients. PARADIGM improved EFS but not OS [41,42]; a propensity-adjusted retrospective series numerically favoured 7+3 for OS (18.0 vs 9.5 months, p=0.14) [87]. VINCENT is ongoing [86].
4. Whether antifungal prophylaxis reduces IFI in venetoclax recipients. Zero IFI among 10 prophylaxed patients in one cohort [76]; 50% of IFI cases occurred on prophylaxis in another [77]; antifungal use associated with lower mortality in a third [73]. No randomised data located.
5. Whether label-recommended venetoclax dose reductions with azole antifungals are correct [78].
6. Whether the exceptional single-centre survival with CLAD/LDAC/VEN alternating with AZA/VEN (median OS 52 months) [25] reflects the regimen or patient selection.
7. Whether triplets will improve survival at all, given that every randomised triplet to date has not [38,55], while every single-arm triplet reports CRc 80–96% [29,31,33,34,35,36].
8. Whether MCL-1 can ever be drugged safely, e.g. via tumour-selective delivery [3,64].
9. Whether venetoclax discontinuation in deep remission is safe [72,83].
10. Optimal HMA schedule with venetoclax (5-day vs 7-day azacitidine vs 10-day decitabine vs oral ASTX727) — no randomised comparison exists [18,20,22].

## Barriers to cure in this domain

1. **BAX is the single point of failure for the entire BH3-mimetic class.** Acquired inactivating *BAX* mutations arise in 17% of post-venetoclax relapses and were competitively selected under prolonged BH3-mimetic exposure [60]. No BCL-2, MCL-1, or BCL-xL inhibitor can kill a BAX-null cell. Continuous, indefinite venetoclax dosing is therefore an active selective pressure for a lesion that closes off the whole class — an argument for time-limited or intermittent exposure that has never been tested against this endpoint.
2. **Venetoclax kills by lineage and differentiation state, and AML relapses from the state it does not kill.** Primitive blasts are BCL-2-dependent; monocytic blasts lose BCL2 expression and route survival through MCL-1-supported OXPHOS; erythroid/megakaryocytic blasts depend on BCL-xL [51,57]. Relapse is therefore not stochastic but a predictable outgrowth of a pre-existing state, and single-target apoptotic therapy cannot address it [51].
3. **The obvious pharmacological answer to (2) — MCL-1 inhibition — is blocked by cardiac safety, not by chemistry.** Three clinical-stage MCL-1 inhibitor trials in myeloid/haematologic disease were formally terminated (AMG 397, AZD5991, PRT1419) and the S64315 programme completed without advancing [66]; the only completed phase 1 with reported efficacy in myeloid malignancy produced **no objective responses**, with cardiac-biomarker and ejection-fraction dose-limiting toxicities and an intolerable top dose [63]; the largest MCL-1 phase 1 produced 3 responses in 78 patients alongside pervasive troponin elevation [62]. Two independent reviews now describe the cardiac toxicity as class-wide rather than compound-specific [3] and identify it as the principal barrier to clinical use [64]. Whether a tumour-selective delivery strategy (PROTAC, ADC) or an intermittent schedule can open a therapeutic window is untested [3].
4. **Composite CR is not tracking survival in this domain, which means the discovery pipeline is optimising the wrong endpoint.** Single-arm triplets report CRc 80–96% and are advanced on that basis; the two randomised triplet tests (PEVENAZA, ENHANCE-3) showed EFS HR 0.99 and OS HR 1.178 respectively [38,55], and VERONA improved ORR by 18.5 absolute points with no OS effect [47]. Adding response-improving agents to venetoclax + HMA has repeatedly failed to add survival.
5. **Myelosuppression is the binding constraint on combination intensity, and it is mediated by infection deaths.** ENHANCE-3's excess mortality was driven by grade 5 infections [38]; triplets require dose or duration reduction in the majority of patients reaching consolidation [31]; 60-day mortality reaches 19% in real-world patients ≥75 [91]. There is limited headroom to add cytotoxicity, so gains must come from selectivity, not addition.
6. **Convergent, partly non-genetic RAS activation defeats sequential targeting.** In the venetoclax + gilteritinib cohort, resistance arose via RAS-mutant clonal selection, non-mutational RAS transcriptional upregulation, *and* monocytic differentiation — three routes to one phenotype [59]. Two-thirds of relapses after HMA+VEN+FLT3i are FLT3-wild-type [31,32]. Adding inhibitors one target at a time cannot outpace a convergent, partly epigenetic escape.
7. **TP53-mutant AML is untouched by anything in this domain.** Median OS ~6 months with VEN+HMA, intensive chemotherapy, or HMA alone [95]; CR/CRi 25% with complex karyotype and/or TP53 on VEN+LDAC [11]; magrolimab, developed specifically for this population, failed in ENHANCE-2 and ENHANCE-3 [38,39]. This is a fully unaddressed subgroup, not a partially addressed one.
8. **Continuous therapy until progression is incompatible with cure-directed logic and with tolerability, and the deintensification evidence is contradictory.** The label mandates indefinite dosing [15]; prospective abbreviation failed non-inferiority [70]; higher dosing added nothing [71]; retrospective abbreviation looks safe [73,74]; MRD-guided discontinuation did not help [71]. There is no evidence-based stopping rule.
9. **The population that most needs venetoclax is systematically excluded from the evidence that governs it.** Only about two-thirds of unselected unfit patients met VIALE-A eligibility criteria [see Evidence-trail notes]; real-world median OS is often 9–15 months against the trial's 14.7 [73,91,92]; paediatric R/R response is 26.3% [97]. The efficacy estimate does not transfer.
10. **Post-venetoclax disease is a new, harder disease and there is no established therapy for it.** ORR to post-venetoclax salvage is ~23% with median OS 3.9 months [61]; post-relapse OS after HMA+VEN is 3.4 months [91]; prior venetoclax cuts menin-inhibitor CRc from 70% to 24% [34]. Frontline venetoclax may be converting a fraction of patients into a state with fewer options than they started with — a possibility that has never been formally studied.
11. **Access and cost structure the achievable benefit.** Cobicistat boosting reproduced venetoclax exposure at 50 mg [75], and cycle individualisation delivered "considerable VEN savings" in a national health system [73] — both signals that the standard regimen is priced and dosed beyond what pharmacology requires, and that the constraint on global benefit is economic as much as biological.

## Open research questions

1. Does time-limited venetoclax exposure (fixed duration, or MRD-triggered discontinuation) reduce the emergence of *BAX* and other class-terminal resistance lesions compared with continuous dosing until progression? **Aim: randomise fixed-duration versus continuous venetoclax after MRD-negative CRc, with acquired BAX/BCL2 variant frequency at relapse as a co-primary endpoint alongside RFS.**
2. Can a dependency-guided BH3-mimetic doublet outperform empirical venetoclax? **Hypothesis: patients selected as dual BCL-2/BCL-xL-dependent by LSC MAC score [53] or ex vivo BH3 profiling derive greater benefit from navitoclax + venetoclax + decitabine [67] than from venetoclax + decitabine alone.**
3. Is there any therapeutic index for MCL-1 inhibition in AML if exposure is made tumour-selective (ADC, PROTAC) or intermittent, with prospective cardiac-biomarker and cardiac-MRI monitoring built into dose escalation? **Aim: a first-in-human trial with cardiac safety as a co-primary endpoint rather than a stopping rule discovered post hoc [3,62,63].**
4. Does RAS pathway inhibition added to venetoclax ± FLT3 inhibitor overcome the convergent monocytic/RAS resistance state identified in single-cell studies? **Hypothesis derived directly from the in vitro rescue in [59]; no clinical trial located.**
5. Which triplet, if any, improves overall survival over venetoclax + azacitidine? **Aim: randomised phase 3 of AZA+VEN+gilteritinib versus AZA+VEN in FLT3-mutated AML, and AZA+VEN+menin inhibitor versus AZA+VEN in NPM1m/KMT2Ar AML — the two settings where single-arm CRc (96%, 81–88%) most exceeds the doublet [31,33,34].**
6. Should venetoclax + azacitidine replace intensive induction in fit adults, and for which genotypes? **PARADIGM excluded FLT3-mutated AML and did not show an OS difference [41]; VINCENT tests NPM1-mutated disease [86]. A trial powered for OS, stratified by ELN 2022/2024 risk and by transplant intent, is the missing study.**
7. Is monocytic differentiation clinically actionable? **Aim: prospectively assign monocytic-predominant AML to a non-BCL-2-anchored regimen (e.g. MCL-1-sparing OXPHOS or RAS-directed therapy) and compare with venetoclax + HMA, resolving the Pei/Waclawiczek discordance [51,53].**
8. What is the correct venetoclax dose and duration in the presence of an azole, and does cobicistat boosting preserve efficacy at 50 mg? **Aim: a randomised non-inferiority trial of cobicistat-boosted low-dose venetoclax versus standard 400 mg, with CRc and cost per remission as co-primary endpoints — directly extending HOVON 171 [75,78].**
9. Does antifungal prophylaxis reduce invasive fungal infection or mortality in venetoclax + HMA recipients? **Aim: the randomised trial the three discordant cohort studies [73,76,77] cannot substitute for.**
10. Does frontline venetoclax exposure worsen salvage outcomes relative to frontline HMA or intensive chemotherapy — that is, is there an opportunity cost to using venetoclax first? **Testable in existing randomised cohorts (VIALE-A, VIALE-C, PARADIGM, PEVENAZA) by comparing post-progression survival and response to first salvage by original arm.**
11. Can TP53-mutant AML be made venetoclax-responsive at all, or should it be routed out of BCL-2-anchored therapy entirely? **Aim: a dedicated TP53-mutant frontline platform trial in which venetoclax + HMA is the comparator rather than the backbone [11,39,95].**
12. Why does composite CR fail to predict survival for venetoclax combinations, and what endpoint should replace it in early-phase triplet selection? **Aim: retrospective validation of MRD-negative CRc, EFS, and duration of transfusion independence as go/no-go endpoints across the randomised venetoclax dataset [8,38,47,55].**
13. What is the optimal HMA partner and schedule? **Aim: randomised comparison of azacitidine 7-day, decitabine 5-day, decitabine 10-day, and oral decitabine/cedazuridine, each with venetoclax — the comparison the field has assumed away for six years [18,20,22].**
14. Can paediatric AML be made venetoclax-responsive? **26.3% composite response in R/R children [97] versus 66.4% CRc in newly diagnosed adults [6] is a large, unexplained gap; a biology-first comparison of BCL2-family dependence between paediatric and adult AML would frame a prospective trial.**

### Evidence-trail notes

- **Today is 2026-09-09.** ASH 2026 (December 2026) has not occurred; **no ASH 2026 data exists and none is cited here**. The most recent conference material actually cited here is ASH 2025 (December 2025 — via the peer-reviewed synthesis [29] and abstracts [40], [41], [48], with secondary coverage [42]) and ASCO 2026 (June 2026 — abstract [44]). EHA 2026 (Stockholm, June 2026) material was encountered in search — including ziftomenib plus 7+3 in NPM1-mutated AML from KOMET-007, and pooled QTc safety analyses of bleximenib from cAMelot-1/ALE1002 — but **no EHA 2026 primary abstract could be retrieved, so none of it is cited or quoted**. Anyone extending this file should treat EHA 2026 as an unsearched gap rather than an empty one.
- **ashpublications.org, ascopubs.org and clinical-lymphoma-myeloma-leukemia.com abstract pages returned HTTP 403** on every direct retrieval attempt (including with a browser user-agent). Conference-abstract citation metadata in [40,41,44,48] was therefore verified through the Crossref API (DOI, title, journal, volume, supplement, page, author count, first author) rather than by reading the abstract page. Where numeric results from those abstracts are quoted, the source of the number is named inline: for VICEROY the numbers come from the peer-reviewed ASH-2025 synthesis [29]; for PARADIGM from The ASCO Post [42]; for the quizartinib triplet from secondary conference reporting.
- **PARADIGM's additional safety numbers** (grade ≥3 infections 28% vs 41%, severe bleeding 2% vs 12%, no early deaths in the azacitidine+venetoclax arm) come from secondary conference coverage encountered in search, not from a retrieved primary abstract, and are **[unverified]** at the primary-source level.
- **The claim that only ~66% of unselected unfit patients would have met VIALE-A eligibility** is from a 21-patient single-centre retrospective cohort (Gruber N, Singer J — *Open Medicine (Warsaw)* 2026;21(1):20261499; PMID 42614847; DOI 10.1515/med-2026-1499). Given the tiny N it is quoted as a signal only and is **[unverified]** as a generalisable figure; it is deliberately not given a numbered reference in the main list.
- **The real-world report of comparably poor outcomes for myelomonocytic and monocytic AML on venetoclax-based therapy** referred to in section 5.2 is Zhao L, et al. — *Annals of Hematology* 2024;103(4):1197–1209 (PMID 38329487; DOI 10.1007/s00277-024-05646-7); it was retrieved as a citation but its numeric findings were not, so no figures from it are quoted.
- **[unverified]:** the assertion that VIALE-A's regular approval (SUPPL 20/21, 2020-10-16 [14]) was granted *specifically* on VIALE-A and VIALE-C is supported by the openFDA submission dates and by secondary coverage, but the FDA approval-notice page for the 2020 conversion could not be retrieved (the 2018 accelerated-approval notice [13] was retrieved; the 2020 accessdata approval-letter PDF exceeded the fetch size limit).
- **[unverified]:** the October 2021 FDA clinical hold on the AZD5991 + venetoclax trial, and the cardiac safety signal that triggered it, rest on trade-press reporting (BioSpace, 21 October 2021) plus the CT.gov termination record [66] and the troponin data in the primary publication [62]. - **Evidence trail goes cold on:** (i) any randomised comparison of venetoclax + HMA versus venetoclax + LDAC — none located; (ii) any randomised comparison among HMA backbones (azacitidine vs IV decitabine vs 10-day decitabine vs oral decitabine/cedazuridine) with venetoclax — none located; (iii) any published phase 3 relapse-free-survival result for VIALE-M — the randomised portion was removed from the protocol [46]; (iv) any clinical trial of RAS-pathway inhibition added to venetoclax in AML — none located, despite the direct preclinical rationale in [59]; (v) any clinical AML efficacy data for sonrotoclax or lisaftoclax — AML-specific clinical results were not located (sonrotoclax AML evidence is preclinical [68]); (vi) any randomised trial of antifungal prophylaxis in venetoclax recipients; (vii) any prospective randomised venetoclax discontinuation trial; (viii) prospective paediatric venetoclax + azacitidine trial data — only retrospective compassionate-use series were located [97].
